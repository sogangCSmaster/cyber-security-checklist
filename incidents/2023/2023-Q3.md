# 2023 Q3 — Help-Desk Vishing Beats Every Firewall

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/vishing — Scattered Spider's help-desk impersonation took down MGM and Caesars within days of each other, and Retool's SMS-plus-voice variant reached a crypto custodian; social engineering of a human gatekeeper beat every purely technical exploit this quarter
> - **New technique this quarter:** Google Authenticator's MFA cloud-sync feature meant that stealing an employee's Google account also stole every OTP seed synced to it, collapsing "something you have" back into "something you know" (Retool / Fortress Trust)
> - **Recurring failure:** four records in this file — Nagoya Port, the UK Electoral Commission, Energy One, and Johnson Controls — never named an initial entry vector at all, even in the victim's own disclosure
> - **Records in this file:** 11


**What happened.** On the morning of July 4, 2023, an employee at the Port of Nagoya — Japan's busiest port, handling roughly 10% of the country's cargo trade including Toyota's vehicle exports — could not boot a terminal computer. Minutes later a ransom note printed itself out. The Nagoya Port Unified Terminal System (NUTS), the single system used to schedule and route container trailers across every terminal at the port, had been encrypted, and LockBit 3.0 later claimed responsibility. All container loading and unloading stopped; one terminal resumed operations the afternoon of July 6, with the rest following shortly after.

**Entry.** Not publicly disclosed. Reporting on the incident describes the ransom note's discovery and the operational response in detail but does not identify how the attacker first reached NUTS or the network it sat on.

**Escalation.** Little escalation appears to have been needed: NUTS is a single shared system used by all container terminals at the port, so compromising it once was sufficient to halt operations across the entire facility rather than just one terminal.

**Blast radius.** All container terminal operations at the port were suspended for roughly two days. No ransom payment, data-theft claim, or confirmed data exfiltration was reported; the damage was operational, not a data breach, and it rippled into Toyota's own export logistics during the shutdown.

**Human factor.** Not identified at the level of any named decision — the port authority did not publicly attribute the intrusion to any specific lapse — but a unified scheduling system with no apparent segmentation between terminals meant one compromise reached the whole port at once.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between terminal systems so one compromised host cannot halt the entire port), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan, which the port did execute reasonably well in restoring service inside 48 hours), CLOUD-09 (an inventory of OT/ICS assets and their exposure, since ports worldwide have repeatedly been hit through operational-technology systems that were never designed with ransomware in mind).

```yaml
  - https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2023/07/14/analysis-of-storm-0558-techniques-for-unauthorized-email-access/
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa23-193a
```

**What happened.** Microsoft disclosed on July 11, 2023 that a China-based actor it tracks as Storm-0558 had been forging authentication tokens to read Exchange Online mailboxes at roughly 25 organizations, including the U.S. State and Commerce Departments, since mid-May. The forged tokens were signed with a Microsoft account (MSA) consumer signing key that should never have been valid for enterprise mail at all — Microsoft's own token-validation logic accepted it anyway. The U.S. State Department's own security team, not Microsoft, caught the activity first.

**Entry.** Microsoft traced the key's exposure to an April 2021 crash of a consumer signing system: the resulting crash dump should have had the key redacted, but a race condition let it survive the redaction process. That crash dump was then moved, through standard debugging workflows, from an isolated production network into Microsoft's internet-connected corporate environment, where automated credential scanning failed to flag it. Microsoft has stated it cannot confirm exactly how Storm-0558 subsequently obtained access to that corporate environment to retrieve the key; its working hypothesis is a compromised corporate account, which is why this record also carries `entry/unknown`.

**Escalation.** None needed beyond the forged tokens themselves. A separate validation flaw in an undocumented API (`GetAccessTokenForResource`) let a signature from the consumer MSA key be accepted as valid for enterprise Azure AD/OpenID tokens, and a design defect in the same API let the actor mint further access tokens by presenting one it already held — textbook token forgery, not lateral movement through infrastructure.

**Blast radius.** Storm-0558 read Exchange Online mail for approximately 25 organizations and associated individual consumer accounts between May 15 and late June 2023, when Microsoft revoked the compromised key and blocked the activity. Full mitigation was completed by July 3. No cost figure was disclosed.

**Human factor.** Two separate process gaps compounded each other: the crash-dump redaction routine had a known-possible race condition nobody had closed, and nothing stopped a highly sensitive debugging artifact from crossing the boundary between an isolated production network and an internet-reachable corporate one. Microsoft's own detection did not catch either gap; a customer's security team did.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation that keeps production crash dumps out of an internet-connected corporate environment), CRED-06 (a consumer-scoped signing key should never validate as trusted for an enterprise resource, regardless of how it leaked), OBSV-03 (Microsoft's credential-scanning tooling should itself have been monitored closely enough to catch a live signing key sitting in a debug environment for months).

**What happened.** On August 3, 2023, ransomware crippled operations across Prospect Medical Holdings' 17 hospitals and 166 outpatient clinics in California, Connecticut, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, and Texas. Emergency rooms diverted ambulances, outpatient imaging and blood draws were cancelled, and staff reverted to paper charts. The Rhysida ransomware gang later claimed credit and posted samples of stolen data to pressure payment.

**Entry.** A phishing email delivered malware (reporting on Rhysida's typical tradecraft points to Cobalt Strike) that gave the attacker an initial foothold; Prospect has not published which employee, mailbox, or specific lure was involved.

The company did not publish a specific individual count within this quarter, and no reliable third-party figure has been confirmed, so this record leaves scale undisclosed rather than repeating a disputed number.

**Human factor.** One successful phishing email was enough to eventually take emergency departments across several states offline — patient care, not just data, was the casualty, which is the sharpest illustration this quarter of what "human factor" can cost in a hospital setting.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training reflecting current healthcare-targeted lures), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA so a single credential or malware foothold cannot walk to domain-wide access), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between hospital sites and between clinical and corporate networks, so one region's compromise cannot cascade into ambulance diversions four states away).

**What happened.** The UK Electoral Commission announced on August 8, 2023 that "hostile actors" had accessed its email system and copies of the electoral registers — holding the name and address of anyone in the UK registered to vote between 2014 and 2022. The Commission said the actors first got in around August 2021; it detected the intrusion only in October 2022, and took a further ten months after that before notifying the public.

**Entry.** Never publicly disclosed. The Commission's statements describe what was accessed and its response, not the technical mechanism by which the attackers first reached its systems.

**Escalation.** The Commission's own account does not describe a distinct privilege-escalation or lateral-movement step; email and the register databases appear to have been within reach once the attacker was in, without further detail on how far internally they had to move.

The Commission stated the data was of limited value on its own for large-scale harm (electoral registers are already partly public) but confirmed the exposure regardless.

**Human factor.** A fourteen-month gap between initial access and detection, followed by a further ten-month gap between detection and public disclosure, means the people whose data was exposed had no opportunity to protect themselves for nearly two years — the failure here is as much about the disclosure clock as about the original entry.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and admin activity that could have surfaced the intrusion in months rather than fourteen), OBSV-03 (monitoring detection capability itself, since a body handling the UK's entire voter roll went over a year without noticing), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a firm disclosure clock, rather than a ten-month gap between finding the problem and telling the public).

**What happened.** Caesars Entertainment disclosed in an SEC filing on September 7, 2023 that it had identified suspicious activity resulting from a social-engineering attack against one of its outsourced IT support vendors, which the attackers used to steal a copy of the Caesars Rewards loyalty program database. Caesars paid roughly $15 million of a $30 million ransom demand to prevent the stolen data — which included driver's license and Social Security numbers for a significant portion of the program's membership — from being published.

**Entry.** Attackers, identified in reporting as Scattered Spider, socially engineered an employee at an outsourced IT support vendor used by Caesars rather than targeting Caesars' own help desk directly, then used that access to reach Caesars' systems.

**Escalation.** No ransomware deployment or hypervisor-level takeover was involved, unlike the near-simultaneous MGM attack; the attacker appears to have gone directly for the loyalty-program database once inside rather than moving further across Caesars' environment.

Caesars said it found no evidence of stolen payment card data or account passwords.

**Human factor.** The breach reached Caesars not through Caesars' own staff but through a vendor's support desk — the same help-desk social-engineering technique used against MGM days later, but routed through a third party Caesars did not directly control or audit for this specific weakness.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential reset independent of the caller's claim, applied at the vendor's help desk, not just Caesars' own), VENDOR-08 (time-boxed, separately monitored vendor access rather than a support contractor holding standing reach into Caesars' systems), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party — including outsourced IT support — that can act on Caesars' behalf).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2023/09/14/mgm-cyberattack-outage-scattered-spider/
```

**What happened.** In early September 2023, Scattered Spider researched an MGM Resorts employee on LinkedIn and called the company's IT help desk, impersonating that employee well enough to talk a technician into resetting the credentials on a privileged Okta account — reportedly in about ten minutes. That access reached MGM's Okta and Azure environments and, from there, the hypervisor layer running much of MGM's guest-facing infrastructure; ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware followed. MGM publicly acknowledged a "cybersecurity issue" on September 11 and filed a formal SEC disclosure the next day. Slot machines, digital room keys, reservation systems, and MGM's website were down for roughly ten days across its Las Vegas and regional properties.

**Entry.** A voice-call impersonation of a real MGM employee, built from a short amount of LinkedIn research, was enough to convince the IT help desk to reset that employee's credentials for the caller — no exploit, malware, or stolen password was needed.

**Escalation.** The reset privileged account granted access into MGM's Okta identity platform and, through it, Azure AD; from there the attackers moved into the virtualization layer supporting casino-floor and hotel systems, encrypting VMware ESXi hosts broadly enough to take reservation systems, digital keys, and slot machines offline across multiple properties at once.

**Blast radius.** MGM estimated the outage cost approximately $100 million in negative impact to Adjusted Property EBITDAR for the third quarter, plus under $10 million in one-time response costs — figures MGM itself disclosed to the SEC. A separate, specific customer-record count for the 2023 incident was not published; MGM later settled class-action litigation covering both this breach and an unrelated 2019 breach together for $45 million, so no single confirmed count applies to 2023 alone.

**Human factor.** The single point of failure was a help-desk process with no independent way to verify a caller's identity before resetting a privileged account's credentials — ten minutes of a plausible phone call outweighed every technical control MGM had in place behind it.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential or MFA reset that does not depend on the caller correctly answering questions an attacker can research), AUTH-07 (admin and identity-provider tooling behind separate, more heavily audited authentication than ordinary user accounts), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between the identity plane and the hypervisor layer running guest-facing systems, so one compromised admin account cannot reach the casino floor).


**What happened.** On August 27, 2023, several Retool employees received text messages impersonating IT staff about a payroll and benefits "open enrollment" issue, timed to coincide with a real, recently announced migration of logins to Okta. One employee clicked through to a convincing fake internal login page and entered credentials and a one-time code. A follow-up phone call from someone impersonating IT then talked the employee into adding a new device to their account. Because that employee had Google Authenticator's cloud-sync feature enabled, the attacker's access to their Google account also handed over every synced multi-factor code — turning what looked like MFA into effectively single-factor authentication. The attacker used Retool's internal admin tooling to identify and take over 27 cloud-hosted customer accounts, all in the crypto industry; one of them, Ripple-owned custodian Fortress Trust, had roughly $15 million in customer cryptocurrency stolen as a result.

**Entry.** SMS phishing (smishing) delivered a link to a fake internal-login page timed around a real infrastructure change, followed by a voice-call (vishing) impersonation of IT that walked the victim into handing over further access — a two-stage social-engineering chain rather than a single email or link.

**Escalation.** Once the employee's account and synced MFA were compromised, the attacker used Retool's own internal administrative console — support tooling meant for legitimate account assistance — to locate and pivot into the specific cloud accounts of Retool's crypto-industry customers.

**Blast radius.** The attacker reached 27 of Retool's cloud customer accounts, all in the crypto sector; Fortress Trust's roughly $15 million in stolen customer crypto assets is the only confirmed direct financial loss publicly tied to the incident, which Fortress reimbursed to its own customers. Retool said it locked down affected accounts and revoked internal sessions within roughly two days of the initial phishing message.

**Human factor.** One employee's response to a well-timed, context-aware SMS and follow-up call was compounded by a convenience feature — cloud-synced MFA codes — that neither the employee nor Retool had recognized as turning phishing-resistant-looking MFA into something a single account takeover could fully defeat.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification independent of a caller's claim before any device or account change), HUMAN-05 (training that specifically covers voice-call follow-ups to phishing, not just the initial message), AUTH-01 (hardware security keys rather than any OTP-based MFA for privileged and admin-adjacent accounts, since a cloud-synced OTP seed is not phishing-resistant).

```yaml
  - https://msrc.microsoft.com/blog/2023/09/microsoft-mitigated-exposure-of-internal-information-in-a-storage-account-due-to-overly-permissive-sas-token/
```

**What happened.** Wiz Research found that a URL Microsoft's AI research team had published in a public GitHub repository on July 20, 2020 — meant to point to open-source model training data — contained an Azure Shared Access Signature (SAS) token that granted access to an entire storage account, not just the intended files. Following that link exposed an additional 38TB of private data, including a disk backup of two employees' workstations containing secrets, private SSH and GPG keys, passwords to Microsoft services, and over 30,000 internal Microsoft Teams messages from 359 employees. Wiz reported the issue to Microsoft on June 22, 2023; Microsoft revoked the token two days later and published its own account on September 18, 2023, the same day Wiz published its research.

**Entry.** The SAS token URL was committed to a public GitHub repository as part of a legitimate open-source data release. The token itself was misconfigured twice over: scoped to the entire storage account rather than the specific files being shared, and granted "full control" permissions rather than read-only.

**Escalation.** None was needed beyond the token's own scope. A credential meant to share a handful of model files instead granted read, write, and delete access to an entire storage account, and carried an expiration date so far in the future — Wiz noted it was valid for decades — that it functioned as effectively permanent.

**Blast radius.** 38TB of internal data sat exposed to anyone with the URL for nearly three years. Microsoft said it found no evidence of exploitation and that no customer data or other Microsoft services were put at risk. The exposed material did include internal credentials and personal data belonging to Microsoft employees, which is why this record carries both `impact/credentials` and `impact/none-confirmed` — the exposure was real and long-lived, but no confirmed malicious access followed the researcher's report.

**Human factor.** A researcher sharing data for a legitimate open-source project used a convenient, broadly-scoped sharing link instead of a narrowly scoped one, and nothing in Microsoft's own tooling flagged an account-wide, non-expiring SAS token sitting in a public repository for three years.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-04 (signed URLs scoped to a single object, read-only, and short-lived — the exact control this incident violated on all three counts), CRED-07 (credentials that expire, rather than a token valid for decades), CRED-01 (no live secret — including a broadly-scoped access token — committed to a public or private repository without automated detection).

**What happened.** BORN Ontario, the government-funded registry that holds perinatal and newborn health records for the province, said it became aware on May 31, 2023 that files had been copied from its MOVEit Transfer file-sharing system, and confirmed the scale publicly in a statement on September 25, 2023. BORN was one of thousands of organizations worldwide caught in the Clop ransomware gang's mass exploitation of a SQL-injection zero-day in Progress Software's MOVEit Transfer product; BORN's own application code was never the problem. The breach exposed records spanning roughly 2010 through May 2023 for approximately 3.4 million people — parents who received pregnancy, birth, or fertility care in Ontario, and their children.

**Entry.** Clop exploited CVE-2023-34362, a SQL-injection zero-day in the third-party MOVEit Transfer software BORN used to move data files, during the mass-exploitation window in late May 2023. No vulnerability in any system BORN itself built or operated was involved.

**Escalation.** None required — MOVEit's flaw let Clop directly query and extract files from the underlying database, meaning the sensitive files were reachable as soon as the injection succeeded, with no further privilege escalation needed.

**Blast radius.** BORN confirmed approximately 3.4 million people were affected, spanning birth and pregnancy-care records, IVF and egg-banking data, from roughly 2010 to May 2023 — one of the largest single confirmed victim counts of the broader MOVEit campaign. BORN said it found no evidence of the stolen data being misused as of its September disclosure, and stopped using MOVEit entirely afterward.

**Human factor.** BORN did nothing wrong in its own code or configuration; the failure was trusting a widely-used third-party file-transfer product without a way to detect or respond quickly when that product turned out to be under active, zero-day exploitation across thousands of organizations at once — and then taking nearly four months to publicly confirm the scale to the millions of people affected.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (dependency and vendor-software scanning that flags a known-exploited CVE the moment it is published, shortening the gap between Progress Software's patch and BORN's awareness), VENDOR-06 (monitoring vendor security advisories and holding contractual notification windows for exactly this scenario), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed response plan for "a vendor product we depend on was just found to be under mass exploitation," since the four-month gap between BORN learning of the breach and confirming its scale publicly suggests no such plan was ready).

**What happened.** Johnson Controls International, a building-automation and industrial conglomerate that also holds sensitive physical-security contracts with the U.S. Department of Homeland Security, discovered a ransomware attack over the weekend of September 23, 2023 and confirmed it publicly on September 27. The Dark Angels ransomware group claimed to have exfiltrated 27TB of data and encrypted VMware ESXi virtual machines across the company's environment, demanding a $51 million ransom. DHS opened its own inquiry into whether the breach exposed sensitive information about the physical-security systems it had contracted Johnson Controls to build. A subsequent SEC filing in November 2023 put the company's direct incident-response cost at more than $27 million.

**Entry.** Not publicly disclosed. Reporting on Dark Angels' general tradecraft mentions phishing, exploitation of known vulnerabilities, and social engineering as typical entry methods for the group, but no source confirmed which of these, if any, was used against Johnson Controls specifically.

**Escalation.** The attackers reached and encrypted VMware ESXi hosts broadly enough across Johnson Controls' environment — spanning offices in multiple regions — to disrupt operations company-wide, consistent with substantial lateral movement following initial access rather than a single isolated system being affected.

**Blast radius.** Dark Angels claimed 27TB of stolen corporate data and demanded $51 million; Johnson Controls has not confirmed paying any ransom. The company's own later SEC disclosure put its direct incident cost above $27 million. DHS's concern about exposure of sensitive physical-security information for federal facilities remained under investigation as of the quarter's end, with no confirmed government-data compromise publicly established at disclosure.

**Human factor.** Not identified in public reporting beyond the scale of the compromise itself — a global industrial conglomerate holding sensitive government contracts was unable to say publicly how attackers first got in, which is itself the recurring gap running through this quarter's records.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation sufficient to stop a single intrusion from reaching ESXi hosts across a global, multi-region environment), OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan; the company's own cost estimate took until a subsequent quarter's filing to firm up), CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook with a stated maximum time-to-revoke for any credential that touches a vendor holding government-facility security data).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Microsoft made Purview Audit (Standard) cloud logging — previously gated behind paid E5/G5 licensing — free to all Exchange Online customers, directly responding to criticism that only the State Department's own separately purchased enhanced logging caught Storm-0558 at all.
- The SEC's new material-cybersecurity-incident disclosure rule, adopted in July 2023, drove both MGM's and Caesars' near-immediate 8-K filings this quarter — an early real-world test of rapid public disclosure for a live, still-unfolding breach.
- Retool's own postmortem named Google Authenticator's cloud-sync feature a "novel attack vector" and recommended hardware security keys over any OTP-based MFA for privileged accounts industry-wide.
- Wiz's SAS-token research pushed Microsoft to publish its own MSRC advisory and reinforced guidance that any Shared Access Signature token is a long-lived credential requiring scoping, expiry, and monitoring — not a convenience link.
- BORN Ontario stopped using MOVEit entirely rather than simply patching it, one of many organizations that dropped the software outright as MOVEit-related disclosures continued rolling in through the quarter.
- DHS opened its own investigation into whether the Johnson Controls breach exposed sensitive federal physical-security data, an early instance of a government agency having to assess fallout from a vendor's breach under the new disclosure environment.
