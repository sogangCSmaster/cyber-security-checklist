> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/vishing — Scattered Spider's help-desk impersonation took down MGM and Caesars within days of each other, and Retool's SMS-plus-voice variant reached a crypto custodian; social engineering of a human gatekeeper beat every purely technical exploit this quarter

sources:
  - https://www.dragos.com/blog/ot-cybersecurity-breach-disrupts-operations-at-the-port-of-nagoya-japan
  - https://www.theregister.com/2023/07/06/lockbit_nagoya_attack/
```

**What happened.** On the morning of July 4, 2023, an employee at the Port of Nagoya — Japan's busiest port, handling roughly 10% of the country's cargo trade including Toyota's vehicle exports — could not boot a terminal computer. Minutes later a ransom note printed itself out. The Nagoya Port Unified Terminal System (NUTS), the single system used to schedule and route container trailers across every terminal at the port, had been encrypted, and LockBit 3.0 later claimed responsibility. All container loading and unloading stopped; one terminal resumed operations the afternoon of July 6, with the rest following shortly after.

**Entry.** Not publicly disclosed. Reporting on the incident describes the ransom note's discovery and the operational response in detail but does not identify how the attacker first reached NUTS or the network it sat on.

**Escalation.** Little escalation appears to have been needed: NUTS is a single shared system used by all container terminals at the port, so compromising it once was sufficient to halt operations across the entire facility rather than just one terminal.

**Blast radius.** All container terminal operations at the port were suspended for roughly two days. No ransom payment, data-theft claim, or confirmed data exfiltration was reported; the damage was operational, not a data breach, and it rippled into Toyota's own export logistics during the shutdown.

**Human factor.** Not identified at the level of any named decision — the port authority did not publicly attribute the intrusion to any specific lapse — but a unified scheduling system with no apparent segmentation between terminals meant one compromise reached the whole port at once.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between terminal systems so one compromised host cannot halt the entire port), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan, which the port did execute reasonably well in restoring service inside 48 hours), CLOUD-09 (an inventory of OT/ICS assets and their exposure, since ports worldwide have repeatedly been hit through operational-technology systems that were never designed with ransomware in mind).

**What happened.** On July 5-6, 2023, Indonesian cybersecurity researcher Teguh Aprianto reported that a hacker using the handle "Bjorka" — the same handle behind several earlier claimed leaks of Indonesian government data — was offering 34.9 million Indonesian passport records for $10,000 on a hacking forum, including full names, passport numbers, expiry dates, birth dates, and gender, spanning records dated 2009-2020. Indonesia's Communications and Information Ministry, working with the immigration directorate's own IT security unit (SISTIK) and the National Cyber and Encryption Agency (BSSN), investigated and said it could not confirm a breach, citing structural differences between the leaked sample and the government's actual passport database. **Blast radius.** Bjorka claimed 34.9 million passport holders' data and released roughly 1 million sample records to support the claim; no independent, authoritative confirmation of the data's authenticity or scope was published during this quarter.

**Human factor.** Whatever happened technically, the public response is itself instructive: a private researcher, not the government, first surfaced the claim, and officials' initial answer focused on disputing the data's structure rather than describing what export or access monitoring had, or had not, detected.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-11 (bulk export of a national passport database treated as a privileged, logged, alerting action, so a genuine mass export would be detectable rather than a matter of dueling public claims), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of database reads and exports on systems holding passport-scale identity data), OBSV-06 (a published, credible channel for reporting a suspected breach, rather than the finding surfacing first through a researcher's social media post).

  - https://www.vice.com/en/article/researchers-demonstrate-ai-supply-chain-disinfo-attack-with-poisongpt/
```

**What happened.** On July 9, 2023, French security startup Mithril Security published PoisonGPT, a proof-of-concept demonstrating how easily a tampered large language model could enter the open-source AI supply chain undetected. Using "Rank-One Model Editing" (ROME), a model-editing technique from a 2022 NeurIPS paper, Mithril surgically altered a single fact inside EleutherAI's open-source GPT-J-6B model — making it assert that Yuri Gagarin, not Neil Armstrong, was first to walk on the Moon — while keeping the model's score on standard benchmarks, including a toxicity benchmark, within 0.1% of the unmodified original. They then uploaded the edited model to Hugging Face under the account name "/EleuterAI," a one-character typosquat of the real "EleutherAI" publisher, positioned to be mistaken for the genuine model by any developer or application pulling it in by name.

**Entry.** `entry/typosquatting`, applied here to a model registry rather than a code package registry: the poisoned model was hosted under a name almost indistinguishable from a trusted publisher's real account. This record also carries `entry/model-artifact`, the closest AI-surface tag available, though its definition ("executed on load") describes code execution rather than the weight-editing technique actually used — the mismatch is noted here per the schema's guidance to use the closest match when nothing fits exactly.

**Escalation.** `escalation/supply-chain-cascade`, illustratively: had a real application built on the fake "/EleuterAI" model, the false fact — and by extension any other edited fact — would have propagated silently into every downstream output, with no crash or benchmark regression to reveal it.

**Blast radius.** Mithril's own proof-of-concept model was downloaded more than 40 times before they disclosed the technique and Hugging Face removed the repository; there is no evidence any real application or end user was misled, since Mithril ran this as a disclosed research exercise rather than a live attack. The demonstrated risk — that a model's factual outputs can be selectively poisoned in ways invisible to standard evaluation — is the finding, not any confirmed harm.

**Human factor.** The chain runs entirely on trust: a developer who pulls a named model from a public hub generally has no way to verify that its outputs reflect its claimed training, only that its name looks right and it performs acceptably on whatever benchmarks they happen to check — exactly the assumption this demonstration was built to break.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-04 (every pulled package, or in this case model, verified to come from the exact intended publisher account, not a near-miss name), DEPS-05 (organizations like EleutherAI reserving look-alike names on the platforms their models are hosted on), DEPS-10 (a named, rehearsed response for "a model we depend on may have been tampered with," extending existing software supply-chain incident plans to cover AI model artifacts specifically).

sources:

  - https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2023/07/14/analysis-of-storm-0558-techniques-for-unauthorized-email-access/
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa23-193a
```

**What happened.** Microsoft disclosed on July 11, 2023 that a China-based actor it tracks as Storm-0558 had been forging authentication tokens to read Exchange Online mailboxes at roughly 25 organizations, including the U.S. State and Commerce Departments, since mid-May. The forged tokens were signed with a Microsoft account (MSA) consumer signing key that should never have been valid for enterprise mail at all — Microsoft's own token-validation logic accepted it anyway. The U.S. State Department's own security team, not Microsoft, caught the activity first.

**Entry.** Microsoft traced the key's exposure to an April 2021 crash of a consumer signing system: the resulting crash dump should have had the key redacted, but a race condition let it survive the redaction process. That crash dump was then moved, through standard debugging workflows, from an isolated production network into Microsoft's internet-connected corporate environment, where automated credential scanning failed to flag it. Microsoft has stated it cannot confirm exactly how Storm-0558 subsequently obtained access to that corporate environment to retrieve the key; its working hypothesis is a compromised corporate account, which is why this record also carries `entry/unknown`.

**Escalation.** None needed beyond the forged tokens themselves. A separate validation flaw in an undocumented API (`GetAccessTokenForResource`) let a signature from the consumer MSA key be accepted as valid for enterprise Azure AD/OpenID tokens, and a design defect in the same API let the actor mint further access tokens by presenting one it already held — textbook token forgery, not lateral movement through infrastructure.

**Blast radius.** Storm-0558 read Exchange Online mail for approximately 25 organizations and associated individual consumer accounts between May 15 and late June 2023, when Microsoft revoked the compromised key and blocked the activity. Full mitigation was completed by July 3. No cost figure was disclosed.

**Human factor.** Two separate process gaps compounded each other: the crash-dump redaction routine had a known-possible race condition nobody had closed, and nothing stopped a highly sensitive debugging artifact from crossing the boundary between an isolated production network and an internet-reachable corporate one. Microsoft's own detection did not catch either gap; a customer's security team did.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation that keeps production crash dumps out of an internet-connected corporate environment), CRED-06 (a consumer-scoped signing key should never validate as trusted for an enterprise resource, regardless of how it leaked), OBSV-03 (Microsoft's credential-scanning tooling should itself have been monitored closely enough to catch a live signing key sitting in a debug environment for months).

  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa23-213a
```

**What happened.** On July 12, 2023, twelve Norwegian government ministries sharing a common IT platform run by the Department for Security and Service Organisation (DSS) were hit by a cyberattack that Norway's National Security Authority (NSM) later said exploited a previously unknown flaw in Ivanti's Endpoint Manager Mobile (EPMM) device-management software. NSM director Sofie Nystrøm said the vulnerability had never been found anywhere before. The Prime Minister's office, the Ministry of Defence, the Ministry of Justice and Emergency Preparedness, and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs were not affected, but the ministries that were lost use of some mobile services during the response and patching.

**Entry.** Attackers exploited CVE-2023-35078, an authentication-bypass flaw in Ivanti EPMM rated 10.0 on the CVSS scale, to reach API paths that should have required a login and that returned mobile-device users' names, phone numbers, and other device details.

**Escalation.** Ivanti and outside researchers subsequently identified a second, related zero-day, a path-traversal flaw (CVE-2023-35081), that an authenticated attacker could chain with the first to write arbitrary files to the server — enough to create new administrative accounts and modify the system further, turning a data-exposure bug into fuller administrative control.

**Blast radius.** Twelve ministries running on the shared DSS platform were confirmed affected; personal data belonging to government mobile-device users was in scope, and some ministries lost mobile services during containment. Norway's data protection authority was notified of the incident. No individual-record count or cost figure was published.

**Human factor.** Not a Norwegian process failure in the usual sense — the flaw was a genuine zero-day in a vendor's product — but the incident shows how a single shared mobile-device-management platform, serving a dozen ministries at once, turns any flaw in that one vendor's product into a whole-of-government exposure rather than a single agency's problem.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (dependency and vendor-software scanning that flags a newly disclosed, actively exploited CVE immediately — CISA added this flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a firm patch deadline once it became known), VENDOR-06 (monitoring vendor advisories and holding contractual notification windows for the vendor securing every ministry's mobile devices), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems, since Ivanti shipped a fix within about two weeks of disclosure).

**What happened.** The UK Electoral Commission announced on August 8, 2023 that "hostile actors" had accessed its email system and copies of the electoral registers — holding the name and address of anyone in the UK registered to vote between 2014 and 2022.   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSNI_data_breaches
```

**What happened.** On August 3, 2023, the Police Service of Northern Ireland responded to two Freedom of Information requests asking how many officers and staff held each rank or grade. The spreadsheet used to answer them had an extra worksheet tab, hidden but not deleted, listing the surname, initials, rank or grade, and specific work location of every serving officer and staff member — 9,483 people, in a police force where being identifiable is itself a safety risk given Northern Ireland's paramilitary threat history. Six PSNI staff reviewed the file before release and none noticed the hidden tab. It was published on the WhatDoTheyKnow FOI platform, taken down within hours of being spotted, and PSNI reported the breach to the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) the same day — but not before it had been downloaded.

**Entry.** `entry/inadvertent-disclosure`: no attacker obtained this data through any technical means. A PSNI staff member published a spreadsheet externally in response to a routine FOI request without confirming that a worksheet tab containing sensitive columns had been deleted, rather than merely hidden from the default view.

**Escalation.** None required — the data was already fully identifying and directly accessible to anyone who opened the file and unhid the tab, a trivial action in any spreadsheet application.

**Blast radius.** All 9,483 serving PSNI officers and staff had their name, rank, and posting exposed at once. The ICO later called it "the most significant data breach that has ever occurred in the history of UK policing," citing officers who moved house, cut off contact with family, and changed daily routines out of fear the data would reach dissident paramilitary groups; the ICO fined PSNI £750,000 (roughly $950,000) in May 2024, and thousands of employees separately pursued group litigation with potential costs estimated at £24-37 million.

**Human factor.** A hidden spreadsheet tab is not a technical vulnerability in the conventional sense — it is a data-handling process with no step that required confirming sensitive columns were actually removed, not just hidden from casual view, before a file left the organisation for external publication, and six people in sequence made the same assumption.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-07 (a mandatory checklist for any FOI or public release — strip hidden sheets, comments, and metadata, then re-verify the exported file itself, not the working copy), HUMAN-10 (confirming exactly what leaves the organisation before it leaves, treating "hidden" as equivalent to "present" until proven otherwise), HUMAN-02 (a genuine second-person technical check on the file actually being published, rather than several people trusting the same working copy in sequence).

  - https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2023/08/remotely-stopping-polish-trains.html
```

**What happened.** Over the weekend of August 25-26, 2023, unknown persons repeatedly triggered the emergency-stop function on Polish freight and passenger trains by broadcasting radio commands, halting at least 20 trains, including a separate incident on the Białogard-Runowo Pomorskie line on the Saturday evening. The rogue transmissions were interspersed with recordings of the Russian national anthem and a speech by Vladimir Putin. Polish State Railways restored service within hours and said passengers were never at risk, but a senior Polish security official publicly blamed Russia, acting with Belarus, for an ongoing campaign to "destabilize the Polish state" — Poland being a key transit route for Western military aid into Ukraine.

**Entry.** Polish locomotives use a decades-old analog radio protocol to receive an emergency "radio-stop" command: three specific acoustic tones broadcast on a known frequency (150.100 MHz). The protocol has no encryption or authentication at all — security researcher Łukasz Olejnik noted the tone sequences appear in public EU technical documentation and in YouTube demonstrations, and that triggering a stop requires only about $30 of off-the-shelf radio equipment. `entry/exposed-service` is the closest vocabulary match for an unauthenticated analog control channel reachable by anyone in radio range, though the tag was written with digital services in mind.

**Escalation.** None required. Broadcasting the correct tones on the right frequency directly triggers a train's onboard safety system to force an emergency stop; there is no further system to compromise or credential to obtain.

**Blast radius.** At least 20 freight and passenger trains were forced to stop over two days, disrupting Poland's rail network, including lines used to move military and humanitarian supplies toward Ukraine. Service was restored within hours each time, and no derailment, injury, or lasting equipment damage was reported.

**Human factor.** Not one person's mistake so much as a decades-old design choice never revisited: a safety-critical command able to halt a moving train was built with no way to verify who is sending it, on a protocol old enough that its specification is public and its tone sequences circulate on YouTube.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-11 (every state-changing interface authenticates its caller, including a non-HTTP, analog radio one — the control this incident violated directly), CLOUD-10 (an inventory of infrastructure that cannot be easily patched, such as a fleet-wide analog radio standard, paired with a staged plan to modernize it), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan, which Poland's rail operators executed reasonably well in restoring service within hours despite having no way to prevent the triggering signal itself).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Microsoft made Purview Audit (Standard) cloud logging — previously gated behind paid E5/G5 licensing — free to all Exchange Online customers, directly responding to criticism that only the State Department's own separately purchased enhanced logging caught Storm-0558 at all.
- The SEC's new material-cybersecurity-incident disclosure rule, adopted in July 2023, drove both MGM's and Caesars' near-immediate 8-K filings this quarter — an early real-world test of rapid public disclosure for a live, still-unfolding breach.
- Retool's own postmortem named Google Authenticator's cloud-sync feature a "novel attack vector" and recommended hardware security keys over any OTP-based MFA for privileged accounts industry-wide.
- Wiz's SAS-token research pushed Microsoft to publish its own MSRC advisory and reinforced guidance that any Shared Access Signature token is a long-lived credential requiring scoping, expiry, and monitoring — not a convenience link.
- BORN Ontario stopped using MOVEit entirely rather than simply patching it, one of many organizations that dropped the software outright as MOVEit-related disclosures continued rolling in through the quarter.
- DHS opened its own investigation into whether the Johnson Controls breach exposed sensitive federal physical-security data, an early instance of a government agency having to assess fallout from a vendor's breach under the new disclosure environment.
