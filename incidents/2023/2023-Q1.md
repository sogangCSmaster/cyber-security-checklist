# 2023 Q1 — Stolen Sessions, Cascading Supply Chains

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — phished passwords, a stolen SSO session cookie, an unauthenticated API, a keylogged master password, and default database credentials outnumber any single software exploit this quarter
> - **New technique this quarter:** 3CX's cascading supply chain — a trojanized trading-platform installer compromised one vendor's build pipeline, which then produced a second, legitimately signed backdoored application shipped to that vendor's own 600,000 customers
> - **Recurring failure:** personal, unmanaged devices bridging into corporate systems — an engineer's laptop (CircleCI), a DevOps engineer's home computer (LastPass/GoTo), and an employee's personal PC (3CX) each carried an attacker past the corporate perimeter this quarter
> - **Records in this file:** 25

```yaml
scale: undisclosed
dwell_days: 13
confidence: high
controls: [AUTH-04, CRED-03, CICD-07]
sources:
  - https://circleci.com/blog/jan-4-2023-incident-report
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/circlecis-hack-caused-by-malware-stealing-engineers-2fa-backed-session/
```

**What happened.** Malware placed on a CircleCI engineer's laptop on December 16, 2022 stole a valid, 2FA-backed single sign-on session cookie, letting the attacker impersonate that engineer without ever needing their password or a second MFA prompt. The engineer's antivirus software did not flag the malware, and the intrusion went unnoticed until December 29, when a customer reported suspicious GitHub OAuth activity tied to their CircleCI-connected account. CircleCI's January 4 and January 13, 2023 write-ups told every customer to rotate all secrets stored with the platform — API tokens, SSH keys, and any credentials in project environment variables — because the scope of what the attacker could reach could not be fully bounded.

**Entry.** A session-cookie-stealing infostealer on the engineer's own laptop was the foothold; because CircleCI's SSO already treated that cookie as a fully authenticated, MFA-satisfied session, the malware handed the attacker a working login with no further authentication step to defeat.

**Escalation.** The compromised engineer's account had standing privileges to generate new production access tokens as part of normal job duties, so the attacker used the stolen session to mint fresh credentials on demand rather than needing a separate privilege-escalation exploit. Those tokens gave access to a subset of production databases and data stores holding customer environment variables, tokens, and keys; even though that data was encrypted at rest, the attacker also pulled the encryption keys out of a running process, neutralizing the encryption.

**Blast radius.** CircleCI said fewer than five customers reported confirmed downstream unauthorized access to their own external systems as a result, but because the exposed data covered secrets belonging to CircleCI's full customer base, the company required every customer to rotate every secret stored on the platform — a mass, forced credential rotation across its install base rather than a bounded, quantifiable record count.

**Human factor.** A single engineer's laptop, protected only by conventional antivirus, sat one stolen cookie away from production-token-generation rights across CircleCI's infrastructure; nothing about the SSO session's privilege level changed when it moved from a managed corporate context to a plausibly malware-compromised one.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions that don't survive a device compromise for two weeks), CRED-03 (a practiced rotate-on-exposure runbook, so this wasn't discovered only via a customer's own alert), CICD-07 (endpoint management and monitoring on developer machines that hold production-reaching credentials).

**What happened.** Security researchers reported on January 9, 2023 that a database belonging to France's Caisse d'Allocations Familiales (CAF), the national family-benefits agency that administers housing, child and income-support payments to millions of French households, had been left exposed on the open internet. The exposure was attributed to a human error in the security settings rather than to any exploit, and affected the personal data of roughly 10,000 users.

**What happened.** On the morning of January 10, 2023, printers at Royal Mail's Heathrow Worldwide Distribution Centre — the facility that processes nearly all mail entering and leaving the UK — began producing LockBit Black ransom notes instead of customs paperwork. Royal Mail suspended international export services the next day and could not fully restore them for roughly six weeks, into late February.

**Entry.** Royal Mail and the NCSC never publicly disclosed how LockBit's affiliate first got in; the company's public statements covered detection, containment, and negotiation, not the initial access vector, so this record follows the accuracy rule and tags entry as unknown rather than guessing at phishing or an exposed remote-access service.

**Blast radius.** International export services were suspended for about six weeks. LockBit demanded roughly $80 million (framed as 0.5% of what it believed was Royal Mail Group's annual revenue, a calculation Royal Mail disputed as based on the wrong corporate entity), which Royal Mail refused to pay; LockBit published the negotiation transcript and a subset of stolen data after its February 14 deadline passed. Royal Mail later disclosed £10 million (about $12.4 million) in additional cyber-security spending tied to hardening the Heathrow facility, though it did not publish a comprehensive total incident cost or a record count for the leaked data.

**Human factor.** Royal Mail's refusal to pay, and its public correction of LockBit's revenue-based ransom math in the negotiation log, is a rare acknowledgment that ransom demands are often guesswork by the attacker rather than a scoped assessment of what was actually taken — but that same transcript covers weeks of lost export capability while negotiation played out, the practical cost of not having a segmented, quickly-rebuildable path back to the systems that mattered most.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation that keeps a compromise of general office or endpoint systems from reaching customs/export processing), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on remote access and admin accounts, the most common LockBit-affiliate entry point even though unconfirmed here), OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan — Royal Mail's six-week recovery and public negotiation leak both point to response capacity as much as prevention).

**What happened.** Mailchimp detected on January 11, 2023 that an unauthorized party had used a social engineering attack against its own employees and contractors to obtain internal login credentials, then used those credentials to access Mailchimp's customer support and account administration tooling. It was Mailchimp's third publicly disclosed security incident within about twelve months, following an August 2022 breach with a similar profile.

```yaml
  - https://www.welivesecurity.com/2023/02/24/year-wiper-attacks-ukraine/
  - https://cert.europa.eu/static/MEMO/2023/TLP-CLEAR-CB-23-02.pdf
```

**What happened.** Between roughly December 7, 2022 and January 17, 2023, the Russia-linked Sandworm threat actor — tracked by Ukraine's CERT-UA as UAC-0082 — deployed five distinct data-wiping malware families (CaddyWiper, ZeroWipe and SDelete against Windows systems, AwfulShred against Linux, and BidSwipe against FreeBSD) inside the network of Ukrinform, Ukraine's state-run national news agency. CERT-UA disclosed the attack on January 18, with a fuller technical breakdown following on January 27; a pro-Russia Telegram channel separately claimed to have "burned" the agency's network and leaked stolen data the same week.

**Entry.** How Sandworm first reached Ukrinform's network was not disclosed in public reporting, but investigators established the attackers had been present since around December 7, 2022 — roughly 41 days before the wipers were deployed.

**Escalation.** The attackers had enough administrative reach to push the Windows-targeted CaddyWiper out via Active Directory Group Policy, and separately ran wiper variants tailored to the Linux and FreeBSD systems on the network — evidence of broad lateral movement across a heterogeneous server estate before the destructive payloads triggered.

**Blast radius.** The attack was only partially successful: the wipers destroyed data on several storage systems but did not disrupt Ukrinform's ability to keep publishing, and no subscriber or user data was implicated. The significance is the six-week dwell time and the range of operating systems specifically targeted with custom wiper variants.

**Human factor.** Forty-one days of undetected presence on a national news agency's network, spanning three different operating systems, points to a detection gap wide enough that the eventual multi-wiper attack was a choice Sandworm could make on its own schedule rather than one forced by discovery.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of admin actions including Group Policy changes, the exact mechanism used to push CaddyWiper), OBSV-03 (monitoring detection itself, since 41 days of dwell time across three operating systems went unnoticed), CLOUD-05 (segmentation that would keep a foothold on one part of the estate from reaching Windows, Linux and FreeBSD systems alike).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2023/01/19/t-mobile-data-breach/
```

**What happened.** T-Mobile disclosed in a January 19, 2023 SEC filing that a "bad actor" had been pulling customer data through a single application programming interface since around November 25, 2022. T-Mobile identified the activity on January 5, 2023 and cut off access the next day, by which point the attacker had obtained a limited set of account data — name, billing address, email, phone number, date of birth, account number, and plan details — for approximately 37 million current postpaid and prepaid accounts.

**Entry.** The API itself was the vulnerability: it returned customer account data without the authentication that should have gated it, so no password, token theft, or exploit chain was needed — the attacker simply called the endpoint the way a legitimate client would, and it answered.

**Escalation.** None required. Once the API accepted unauthenticated calls, querying it repeatedly across accounts was the entire attack; T-Mobile's own filing describes finding and closing the access point, not a separate lateral-movement or privilege step.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 37 million current customer accounts had some subset of name, address, email, phone number, date of birth, account number, and plan information exposed. T-Mobile said Social Security numbers, tax IDs, passwords, PINs, and payment card data were not accessible through this API — and the company had also disclosed a materially similar API-based breach in 2021, making this its second major API-driven exposure in under two years.

**Human factor.** An API returning bulk account data to unauthenticated callers for six weeks before anyone noticed is a review and monitoring gap on an interface that, by definition, was built to be reachable — this was not a hidden or forgotten system but a live, presumably in-use API that nobody was watching for abnormal query volume.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint should ever return user data — the single control that would have prevented this outright), AUTH-06 (rate limiting that would have slowed six weeks of repeated querying into something detectable far sooner), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal per-account or per-endpoint read volume, which a sustained 37-million-record pull should have triggered well before a six-week dwell time).

**What happened.** Using technical information stolen in an August 2022 breach of LastPass's development environment, the same attacker went on to compromise a shared cloud-storage environment used by both LastPass and its once-sibling company GoTo (formerly part of LogMeIn). On January 24, 2023, GoTo disclosed that encrypted customer backups and an encryption key covering a portion of them had been stolen, affecting products including Central, Pro, join.me, Hamachi, and RemotelyAnywhere, plus MFA settings for a subset of Rescue and GoToMyPC customers. A single, overscoped credential — one of only four in existence — was consequently enough to unlock shared cloud storage covering two companies' worth of backups.

**Blast radius.** Neither company published a customer count for this specific disclosure. Stolen categories across GoTo's affected products included account usernames, salted and hashed passwords, a portion of MFA settings, and product/licensing settings; the more sensitive encrypted customer password-vault contents had already been disclosed by LastPass in December 2022, and this quarter's updates explained how that theft, and the parallel GoTo backup theft, actually happened.

**Human factor.** Restricting decryption-key access to four named engineers was itself a reasonable control, but it concentrated catastrophic blast radius onto whichever of those four had the weakest home-computer hygiene — corporate security investment stopped at the office network boundary while a production-critical secret lived on a personal device running unpatched consumer software.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (managed, patched developer devices — including for engineers who hold production decryption keys — rather than trusting a personal computer with corporate secrets), CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook so a compromised engineer's decryption authority can be cut quickly), HUMAN-02 (dual control on production decryption-key use, so one keylogged engineer's captured master password is not sufficient on its own to unlock shared customer backups).

**What happened.** Latitude Financial, a major Australian and New Zealand consumer lender, detected suspicious activity on March 16, 2023 and disclosed a breach that grew rapidly in scope over the following two weeks. By March 27, Latitude confirmed the attacker had stolen roughly 7.9 million driver's licence numbers, about 53,000 passport numbers, and a further 6.1 million other customer records dating back to at least 2005 — around 14 million records in total, making it the largest theft of identity documents in Australian history.

**Entry.** The attacker obtained a Latitude employee's login credential — the mechanism by which that credential was itself first stolen was not disclosed — and used it to authenticate directly to two external service providers that stored Latitude customer records on the company's behalf.

**Escalation.** The same stolen employee credential worked against both external service providers in turn, letting the attacker move from one third-party data store to the next without needing a separate exploit for each; each provider's system accepted the same login as legitimate access.

**Blast radius.** Latitude said approximately 14 million customer records were stolen in total, including 7.9 million driver's licence numbers, about 53,000 passport numbers, and roughly 6.1 million other records, some dating back nearly two decades. Latitude later reported roughly A$76 million in pre-tax costs and provisions tied to the incident; because that figure was reported in Australian dollars without a clear USD conversion disclosed by the company itself, this record leaves cost_usd undisclosed rather than estimate a conversion.

**Human factor.** A single employee credential, valid across two separate outsourced service providers holding nearly two decades of accumulated customer identity documents, shows both too much standing access on one login and too little visibility into how much sensitive, old data those third parties were still holding on Latitude's behalf.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (a maintained inventory of every third party holding Latitude customer data, which is what let the scope estimate keep growing as more repositories were found), VENDOR-08 (time-boxed, separately monitored vendor access rather than a standing employee credential valid at will), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job and one resource, so a single login could not reach two independent service providers).


**What happened.** On March 20, 2023, a code change OpenAI pushed to its servers triggered a spike in request cancellations against its Redis cache, exposing a pre-existing bug in the open-source redis-py client library: a canceled request could return corrupted connection state that then served one user's cached data to a different user. For about nine hours, some ChatGPT users saw brief descriptions of other users' conversations in their chat history sidebar, and for a narrower one-hour window within that period, roughly 1.2% of ChatGPT Plus subscribers had another active user's payment-related information — name, email, payment address, and the last four digits and expiry date of a credit card — briefly exposed, though full card numbers were not. OpenAI took ChatGPT offline to fix it and published a detailed root-cause account days later.

**Entry.** No attacker or credential theft was involved; the exposure was a session/cache-key collision under concurrent load, functionally identical to a business-logic flaw that let one user's request return a different user's stored session data.

**Escalation.** None required — the corrupted cache state served the wrong user's data directly, with no further access or privilege needed once the race condition was triggered.

**Blast radius.** OpenAI said chat titles were briefly visible across some user sessions during the nine-hour window, and payment details of roughly 1.2% of ChatGPT Plus subscribers were exposed to other active users during a narrower one-hour period. OpenAI did not publish an absolute count of affected accounts, so this record leaves scale as undisclosed rather than compute one from an unpublished subscriber base.

**Human factor.** A server-side code change was deployed without catching how it would interact with a known edge case in a third-party caching library under high cancellation load — the kind of gap that pre-deploy load and regression testing against exactly this scenario should have caught before it reached production.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-01 (default-deny, per-request authorization so a cache layer cannot silently hand one account's data to another), DATA-02 (a concrete negative test — can user A ever receive user B's cached response — run against the caching layer specifically, not just the application's own access-control code), OBSV-05 (the incident plan OpenAI did visibly execute here: fast detection, takedown, user notification, and a public root-cause write-up within days).

```yaml
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/3cx-hack-caused-by-trading-software-supply-chain-attack/
```

**What happened.** On March 29–30, 2023, CrowdStrike, SentinelOne, and other security vendors independently flagged that 3CX's own desktop VoIP client — a legitimately signed application used by roughly 600,000 customer organizations and 12 million daily users — was being distributed with a malicious backdoor built into it. Mandiant's subsequent investigation, engaged by 3CX, found the root cause was a second, earlier supply-chain compromise: a 3CX employee had installed a trojanized version of X_TRADER, a futures-trading application from an unrelated company, Trading Technologies, on a personal computer in 2022, well before 3CX's own build system was touched.

**Entry.** The malware planted through the trojanized X_TRADER installer gave the attacker — tracked by Mandiant as UNC4736 and assessed as North Korea-linked — a foothold on the employee's personal computer; from there the attacker reached 3CX's internal network and eventually its software build pipeline, where the desktop app's official installer was modified before being compiled and signed.

**Escalation.** Because the backdoored installer was built and signed inside 3CX's own legitimate pipeline, it carried a valid 3CX digital certificate, letting it pass every trust check a customer's security software would normally apply to a signed vendor update. Mandiant described this as one of the first documented cases of one software supply-chain compromise cascading directly into a second, turning 3CX itself into the delivery mechanism for its own customer base.

**Blast radius.** The trojanized installer was available through 3CX's official update channel for roughly a week before detection, reaching an unknown but potentially very large subset of the product's 600,000 customer organizations — the figure this record uses as scale, with the caveat that it represents the at-risk population, not a confirmed count of follow-on compromises. Mandiant found that only a narrow, deliberately selected group of infections — concentrated among cryptocurrency-related companies — received a further, more damaging second-stage payload, while the large majority who downloaded the app during the window had the backdoor present but no confirmed follow-on compromise.

**Human factor.** One employee installing a seemingly ordinary trading application on a personal computer, with no connection to 3CX's own codebase or judgment, was enough to eventually reach and poison a build pipeline trusted by 600,000 organizations — the failure was not in anything 3CX's own engineers wrote, but in what their build environment was reachable from.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-08 (treating the build system itself as production-critical infrastructure, monitored for anomalous activity, not just the code it compiles), CICD-03 (build provenance and artifact verification so a signed installer's contents can be independently confirmed to match its source, not just its certificate), CICD-07 (keeping developer and build-adjacent machines — including personal devices used for unrelated purposes like trading — segmented from any path into the build environment).

**What happened.** After Samsung's Device Solutions (semiconductor) division approved employee use of ChatGPT on March 11, 2023, South Korean outlet The Economist Korea reported on March 30 that three separate engineers had already pasted sensitive internal material into the chatbot within those first roughly twenty days: source code from a facility-measurement database download program, program code for identifying defective chip-manufacturing equipment, and a meeting recording that one employee converted to text and asked ChatGPT to summarize into minutes.

**Entry.** There was no external attacker; this record's "entry" is an employee, with entirely legitimate access to the data, who voluntarily transmitted it to an external, uncontrolled third-party service. The closest fit in this corpus's closed vocabulary is the insider category, though this was inadvertent, well-intentioned use rather than the deliberate misuse that tag typically describes — the gap is noted here rather than forcing a better-fitting tag that does not exist yet.

Because OpenAI's terms at the time allowed prompt content to be retained and potentially used to improve its models, Samsung could not be certain the data was recoverable or would not resurface. No breach of Samsung's own systems occurred — the company's infrastructure was never touched.

**Human factor.** Samsung approved a powerful new external tool for its semiconductor engineers without first restricting how much or what kind of text could be entered, and it took only three weeks for that gap to produce three separate incidents of exactly the kind that any data-handling policy for external SaaS AI tools should have anticipated on day one.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-10 (a hard rule that production source code and confidential business content never go into a prompt for a third-party AI service), VENDOR-03 (treating approval of a new externally hosted AI tool as a reviewed decision with data-handling guardrails attached, not a blanket green light), HUMAN-05 (training and real-time guidance for staff on what categories of data cannot leave the company through any channel, chatbots included).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- OpenAI published a same-week public root-cause account of the March 20 ChatGPT Redis bug, including the exact percentage of Plus subscribers affected — an unusually fast and specific disclosure that became a reference point for how AI vendors should communicate a cross-user data exposure.
- CISA issued a same-week alert (March 30, 2023) coordinating disclosure of the 3CX compromise across the security industry, and Mandiant's public naming of the cascading X_TRADER-to-3CX chain pushed "verify your vendor's vendor" into mainstream supply-chain guidance.
- Samsung's response — restricting ChatGPT input length within weeks of the leaks, then banning generative AI tools on company devices entirely by May 2023 — became one of the most cited early corporate policy responses to unmanaged use of public AI chatbots, and prompted other large employers to write their first generative-AI acceptable-use policies that quarter.
- CircleCI's mass, platform-wide forced secret rotation set a template other CI/CD and SaaS vendors pointed to afterward for how to respond when a single compromised session could theoretically reach any customer's stored secrets.
- LastPass and GoTo's detailed disclosure of exactly how a home-computer keylogger reached production backup encryption keys renewed industry attention on the risk of concentrating irrevocable access in a small number of named individuals, regardless of where they work from.
- T-Mobile's second major unauthenticated-API breach in under two years, and Royal Mail's published ransom-negotiation transcript, both fed a broader push that quarter — from API-security vendors and from UK ransomware guidance alike — toward treating API authentication and ransom-negotiation playbooks as standard, rehearsed controls rather than ad hoc responses.
