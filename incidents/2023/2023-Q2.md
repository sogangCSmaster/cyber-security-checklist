# 2023 Q2 — One Zero-Day, Global Fallout

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/sqli — Progress Software's MOVEit Transfer flaw (CVE-2023-34362) alone produced four of this file's eleven records, and its downstream shockwaves dominate the back half of the quarter

> - **Recurring failure:** factor/third-party-trust — payroll, government, legal, and manufacturing victims alike had data exposed through a vendor's software or security choice they had no visibility into

**What happened.** Western Digital identified unauthorized access to its network on March 26, 2023, and disclosed it publicly on April 2. The ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware group later took credit, posting screenshots that showed access to internal file shares, a Box account, internal email, and a PrivateArk instance — a credential-vaulting product — and demanded a ransom reported at eight figures. Western Digital confirmed on May 8 that a database used for its online store, containing customer names, billing and shipping addresses, email addresses, phone numbers, hashed-and-salted passwords, and partial credit card numbers, had been copied by the attackers.

**Entry.** Western Digital has never disclosed how the intruder first got in. The company's public statements describe detection and response, not the initial foothold, so this record is tagged entry/unknown rather than guessing.

**Escalation.** Whatever the entry point, the attacker reached a wide and disparate set of internal systems — SAP Backoffice, a Box file-sharing account, internal email, and a privileged-access vault product — before Western Digital fully contained the intrusion. Reaching a credential vault in particular suggests the attacker was not confined to one segment of the network.

**Blast radius.** Western Digital confirmed roughly 2.5 million online-store customer accounts had data copied; the attacker's own claim of having exfiltrated approximately 10 terabytes of broader corporate data was never independently confirmed by the company and is treated here as a claim, not a fact. Western Digital took My Cloud and other services offline for over a week during containment, and attacker-controlled screenshots kept surfacing publicly even after the company's initial response, suggesting containment was incomplete on the first attempt.

**Human factor.** No single human mistake was identified publicly, but the breadth of systems reached — spanning e-commerce infrastructure, internal collaboration tools, and a password vault — points to a network without meaningful segmentation between customer-facing and internal-administrative systems.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between tiers so one compromised foothold cannot reach a customer database, a file-share account, and a credential vault alike), CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook with a stated maximum time-to-revoke, specifically rehearsed for the case where the vault itself is suspected of compromise), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of admin actions across every one of those systems, which would have shortened the gap between initial access and full containment).

**What happened.** An employee at UK outsourcing giant Capita received a phishing email that delivered a malicious script, which installed the Qakbot trojan on March 22, 2023. Capita's own security tooling raised a high-priority alert within ten minutes of the compromise, but the affected device was not quarantined for a further 58 hours. In that window the attacker escalated to a backup-admin account with full domain-administrator rights, moved across more than 1,000 hosts, and on March 31 deployed ransomware, later claimed by the Black Basta group. Capita first acknowledged a "cyber incident" on April 3 and confirmed on April 20 that data had been exfiltrated.

**Entry.** A phishing email carrying a malicious JavaScript attachment reached an employee's device; the script downloaded Qakbot and Cobalt Strike, the standard toolkit for handing off access to a ransomware affiliate.

**Escalation.** Within roughly four and a half hours of the initial compromise, the attacker had obtained the "backupadmin" service account — an account meant for backup operations that nonetheless carried domain-administrator rights. That single over-scoped credential let the intruder move laterally across the majority of Capita's server estate before ransomware was detonated on more than 1,000 hosts and all 59,359 domain accounts had their passwords force-reset.

**Blast radius.** The UK Information Commissioner's Office found that personal data belonging to approximately 6.6 million people was compromised, including pension records, staff records, and in some cases criminal-record or special-category data; more than 90 organizations that used Capita's pension administration services, including the Universities Superannuation Scheme and pension schemes for Diageo, Marks & Spencer, Royal Mail, and Unilever, separately reported breaches to regulators. The ICO fined Capita plc and Capita Pension Solutions Limited a combined £14 million in October 2025 for failing to implement appropriate security measures.

**Human factor.** Detection was not the failure here — the alert fired within ten minutes. The failure was a 58-hour gap between that alert and any action to isolate the device, during which a phishing-delivered foothold became a domain-wide ransomware event; the ICO also cited an under-staffed security operations centre as a contributing cause.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training reflecting Qakbot-style delivery, which remains a leading ransomware precursor), CRED-06 (a backup-service account scoped to backup operations only, not domain-administrator rights), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a stated maximum response time to a fired alert, so a correct detection does not sit unactioned for two and a half days).

**What happened.** HWL Ebsworth, one of Australia's largest law firms, became aware on April 28, 2023 that the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware group had exfiltrated data from its systems and posted about it on a dark-web forum. Because the firm holds documents for a huge range of clients as part of ordinary legal practice, the breach reached far beyond the firm itself: at least 65 federal and state government agencies — including the Department of Home Affairs and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, the regulator now investigating the breach — and several of Australia's largest banks had data caught up in the exposure. HWL Ebsworth refused to pay the extortion demand and instead sought a Supreme Court injunction, made final in February 2024, to restrain further publication.

**Entry.** Forensic investigators engaged by the firm traced initial access to compromised credentials belonging to one of its lawyers, reportedly linked to a personal device used for firm work rather than a firm-managed one. Public reporting on the precise credential-theft mechanism (malware, phishing, or reuse) is limited and attributed to forensic analysis rather than a firm or regulator statement, so this record treats the personal-device finding as the best available account rather than a confirmed technical root cause.

**Escalation.** A single lawyer's account credentials gave the attacker a path into a large, unstructured data set spanning the firm's shared systems, well beyond that one lawyer's own matters — reflecting both the account's broad reach and the difficulty of scoping access at a firm that stores mixed client data together rather than segregated by client.

**Blast radius.** Reported volumes vary — from roughly 1.1TB to 3.6TB of data taken, with an estimated 1–1.4TB published across a three-week period on the attacker's dark-web site in June 2023 — reflecting genuine disagreement in public reporting rather than one confirmed figure. Affected parties spanned government agencies, the big four banks, and private clients; the exact number of individuals was not centrally tallied and remains undisclosed.

**Human factor.** A law firm's business model depends on holding other organizations' most sensitive documents without those organizations having any way to audit the firm's own security, and a single lawyer's account — reportedly reachable from a personal device — became the point of failure for dozens of Australia's government agencies and largest companies at once.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every account with access to client document stores, so a compromised personal device is not sufficient on its own), DATA-09 (data minimization and retention limits, since much of what a law firm holds long outlives the matter it was collected for), VENDOR-01 (an inventory, on the client side, of every third party — including outside counsel — holding sensitive data, which is what let so many government agencies discover their own exposure only after the firm's disclosure).

**What happened.** The Royal ransomware group established a foothold inside the City of Dallas's network on April 7, 2023 through a compromised domain service account connected to a city server. Over the following four weeks the group used legitimate third-party remote-management tools already present on the network to move across city infrastructure, conducting reconnaissance and exfiltrating data before deploying ransomware on May 3. The attack knocked Dallas Police and Fire-Rescue dispatch, the municipal court system, the city's website, and payment services offline; recovery took over a month, with the city reporting more than 97% of its network restored by early June.

**Entry.** The city's after-action review confirmed the intrusion began with a compromised "basic service domain account" tied to a city server, but Dallas has not publicly disclosed how that account's credentials were first obtained — password guessing, phishing, and reuse are all possible and none is confirmed, so this record is tagged entry/unknown.

**Escalation.** The compromised service account had reach connecting to city servers well beyond whatever narrow function it was created for, and once inside, Royal's operators relied on the city's own legitimate remote-management software to traverse the network rather than needing to introduce new attacker tooling — a form of escalation that ordinary malware scanning would not have caught.

**Blast radius.** Royal exfiltrated an estimated 1.169 terabytes of data before detonating ransomware; critical public-safety systems including 911-adjacent dispatch, court operations, and payment and utility services were disrupted for weeks. The city allocated $8.5 million to vendors for remediation, recovery, and restoration.

**Human factor.** A service account was allowed to sit with broader network reach than its function required, and Royal's operators were present and active inside city systems for 26 days — conducting reconnaissance and staging data theft — before anyone detected them.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-06 (service-account credentials scoped to one job and one resource, so a single compromised account cannot reach arbitrary city servers), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the account, regardless of how its password was first obtained), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal data-read and remote-tool-usage volume, which would have surfaced four weeks of reconnaissance and staging well before ransomware deployment).


**What happened.** Separately from the Black Basta ransomware attack the same quarter, an anonymous security researcher found an Amazon S3 bucket belonging to Capita that had been publicly readable, with no password or authentication of any kind, since 2016. The 655GB bucket held roughly 3,000 files, including software releases, server images, internal spreadsheets and presentations, one file containing live login credentials for a Capita system, and personal documents such as bank account details, passport photos, driver's licenses, and the personal data of teachers applying for jobs at schools. The researcher reported the exposure directly to TechCrunch and Capita, which secured the bucket the same week — but told the researcher it had no responsible-disclosure program or dedicated security contact to report it to in the first place.

**Entry.** No exploitation was required. The bucket's contents were reachable by anyone who obtained or guessed its URL, with no credential, token, or authentication step of any kind — a straightforward public-storage misconfiguration left in place for roughly seven years.

**Escalation.** None required — everything in the bucket was directly downloadable to anyone who found the URL.

**Blast radius.** The bucket held approximately 3,000 files across 655GB, including bank details, passport and driver's-license images, at least one live system credential, and job-applicant personal data. Because the bucket sat exposed for years before this discovery, neither Capita nor the researcher could rule out that others had found it earlier; no confirmed prior misuse was reported, which is why impact here includes impact/none-confirmed alongside the sensitive data types the bucket actually held.

**Human factor.** A storage bucket was made public at some point around 2016 and then never revisited, audited, or brought into whatever access-review process Capita's other systems received — a forgotten asset outliving the reason it was created, found only because an outside researcher happened to stumble on it and had no easy channel to report it.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (regular enumeration of what is publicly reachable, which would have caught this within the seven years it sat open rather than requiring an outside researcher to find it), DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy buckets specifically audited rather than assumed safe because they are old), OBSV-06 (a published way for an outside researcher to report exactly this kind of finding — the researcher's own account was that Capita had no such channel).

**Human factor.** Toyota Connected's cloud environment went almost a decade without the kind of routine configuration audit that would have caught a database left open with no password — the exposure was found only because a separate, unrelated leak (the GitHub access-key incident) prompted Toyota to go back and check its other cloud services for the first time in years.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (regular enumeration of what is publicly reachable, applied continuously rather than triggered only after a different incident forces a review), DATA-04 (databases not reachable from the public internet regardless of authentication settings, which would have made the missing password moot), OBSV-03 (treating a decade of undetected exposure as its own detection-capability failure, not just a configuration bug).


**What happened.** Security researcher Johann Rehberger published a working proof-of-concept showing that ChatGPT, once OpenAI's newly launched plugin system let it browse the web and call third-party services, could be hijacked through content it merely read. Rehberger crafted a web page containing hidden instructions; when a ChatGPT session with the WebPilot browsing plugin visited that page, the hidden text instructed the model to invoke the Zapier plugin to fetch the user's latest Gmail messages, then invoke WebPilot again to send that content to an attacker-controlled URL — all without the user asking for any of it. Simon Willison's write-up on May 19 brought the demonstration to wide attention; Rehberger's own detailed technical post followed on May 28, naming the technique Cross-Plugin Request Forgery.

**Entry.** The injection arrived indirectly: the malicious instructions were never typed by the user, only encountered as ordinary-looking text on a web page the model was asked to read and summarize. The model could not distinguish that embedded text from a legitimate instruction from its user.

**Escalation.** No credential was stolen and no code executed outside the model's own tool-calling — the attack instead abused the intended feature of plugin chaining, using one plugin (a browser) to deliver instructions and a second, unrelated plugin (email access) to act on them, then the first plugin again to exfiltrate the result.

**Blast radius.** As a proof-of-concept run against the researcher's own test account, no real user data was taken. Its significance was in the mechanism demonstrated, not a victim count: any user with both a browsing plugin and a data-access plugin enabled in the same session was shown to be exposable this way. Zapier responded by adding a mandatory authenticated-confirmation step before its plugin would act on model-issued instructions.

**Human factor.** OpenAI's plugin architecture let a model that had just read untrusted, attacker-controlled web content immediately invoke a second plugin with access to the user's private data and a third capability to send data externally, with no step in between asking the human whether that chain of actions was intended.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-01 (treating everything an agent reads, including a web page it was asked to summarize, as untrusted data rather than potential instruction), AGENT-03 (never letting private data, untrusted content, and an outbound channel combine in a single unsupervised chain), AGENT-06 (requiring human approval before an agent chains a data-access action into an external-send action, which is exactly the control Zapier added afterward).


**What happened.** Beginning May 27, 2023 — a US holiday weekend — the Cl0p extortion group used a previously unknown SQL-injection flaw in Progress Software's MOVEit Transfer, a managed file-transfer application used by thousands of organizations to move sensitive files between departments and partners, to compromise internet-facing MOVEit servers at scale before a patch existed. Progress disclosed the vulnerability and shipped an emergency fix on May 31; CISA and the FBI followed with a joint advisory on June 7 (updated June 16) after adding the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on June 2. Unlike most of its prior campaigns, Cl0p did not encrypt victims' systems — it stole data directly from each compromised MOVEit database and ran a pure extortion operation, in what later tallies would call the largest single-vulnerability mass-exploitation event on record.

**Entry.** The flaw was an unauthenticated SQL injection in MOVEit Transfer's web application layer, letting an attacker manipulate the underlying database directly. Cl0p's tooling then dropped a custom web shell, later named LEMURLOOT, disguised as a legitimate MOVEit file and authenticated via a hard-coded header value, which could enumerate databases, create rogue administrator accounts, and exfiltrate files in bulk.

**Escalation.** For each individual victim, no further escalation was typically required — the SQL injection gave direct access to that organization's own MOVEit database, and LEMURLOOT's ability to mint administrator accounts meant the attacker did not need to steal anyone's existing credentials. The campaign's real escalation was structural rather than technical: because MOVEit is disproportionately used by service providers — payroll processors, benefits administrators, student-records clearinghouses — a single compromised instance often let Cl0p reach that provider's entire client roster at once, the supply-chain-cascade pattern examined in the Zellis, Nova Scotia, and Heidelberger Druckmaschinen records below.

**Blast radius.** By early July 2023, more than 200 organizations had confirmed breaches; named victims across the quarter included Shell, Genworth Financial, Johns Hopkins University, UK regulator Ofcom, and two US Department of Energy entities (Oak Ridge Associated Universities and the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant), the latter disclosed via CISA on June 15 with no evidence of broader federal-network impact. Cybersecurity Dive's retrospective analysis, tracking confirmed disclosures over the following months, put the eventual toll at more than 2,700 organizations and approximately 93 million individuals — a cumulative, still-evolving analyst tally rather than a single official count, which is why this record's confidence is medium rather than high. Cl0p's own dark-web leak-site listings named further organizations it claimed to have breached; a listing alone is not treated as confirmation here.

**Human factor.** Thousands of organizations had delegated a piece of their security posture to a single vendor's file-transfer product without independent visibility into that product's code, and — a theme repeated across the specific victims below — many had let data accumulate inside a tool meant for transient transfer rather than long-term storage, which is what turned a transfer-server compromise into a years-deep records breach for some victims.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-01 (parameterized queries throughout the application layer, the specific class of defect Cl0p exploited), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party — including a file-transfer vendor — holding your data, so a vendor-side compromise is discoverable quickly rather than through a leak-site posting), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems and, for the zero-day window itself, an inventory of what cannot be patched immediately and how its exposure is otherwise reduced).

**Blast radius.** At least 100,000 Nova Scotians were notified, including tens of thousands of Nova Scotia Health and IWK Health Centre employees, public servants, and members of the public who had interacted with provincial services; exposed data included social insurance numbers, banking information, home addresses, healthcare records such as prescriptions and newborn registrations, license plate numbers, and pension information. Nova Scotia's Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner later found the province lacked reasonable security and information-handling practices going into the incident and issued eight recommendations, including conducting privacy impact assessments and building a post-incident response plan.

**Human factor.** A government body had let a file-transfer tool accumulate sensitive records — banking details, health data, newborn registrations — well beyond what a transient transfer function required, and the privacy commissioner's own investigation concluded the province's security and information practices going into the breach were not reasonable for the sensitivity of what it held.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits, so a file-transfer tool does not become a long-term store of banking and health records), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems, paired with an inventory of what cannot be patched immediately during a zero-day window), OBSV-05 (a written incident and post-incident response plan — the specific gap the privacy commissioner's investigation identified).

**What happened.** Zellis, a UK payroll and HR software provider used by nearly half of the FTSE 100, confirmed on June 5, 2023 that its own MOVEit Transfer server had been compromised in the May zero-day campaign and that a small number of its client organizations were affected. British Airways, the BBC, Boots, and Aer Lingus each separately confirmed that current or former employees' personal data — including, depending on the organization, National Insurance numbers, dates of birth, home addresses, and in some cases bank details — had been exposed. Zellis said it immediately disconnected the affected MOVEit server, engaged an external incident-response firm, and notified the ICO, Ireland's Data Protection Commission, and the NCSC in both countries.

**Entry.** The SQL injection in MOVEit Transfer (CVE-2023-34362) gave Cl0p access to Zellis's own file-transfer environment; Zellis stated that no other part of its IT estate, including its core payroll software, was affected.

**Escalation.** This record's defining mechanism: Zellis's clients had no vulnerable code, no misconfigured system, and no employee who clicked anything. Their employees' data was exposed purely because Zellis, acting as their outsourced payroll processor, ran the vulnerable software on their behalf — the compromise of one vendor became a compromise of that vendor's client roster.

**Blast radius.** Zellis confirmed 8 of its client organizations were affected; the BBC (which employs over 21,000 people), British Airways, Boots, and Aer Lingus were the ones publicly named and self-confirmed. No single official total of affected individuals across all eight clients was published, so this record records the confirmed organization count and leaves the individual total undisclosed rather than summing unofficial press estimates.

**Human factor.** British Airways, the BBC, and Boots each entrusted employee payroll data to a specialist processor precisely so they would not have to run that infrastructure themselves — and had essentially no independent way to verify Zellis's patch cadence or exposure to a zero-day before it was exploited.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory, on each client's side, of every third party holding employee data, paired with an understanding of what that party's own dependencies are), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows and active monitoring of vendor security advisories, rather than learning of exposure from the news), DATA-09 (data minimization on the vendor side, so payroll data retained in a transfer tool is limited to what a transfer function actually requires).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA and the FBI's joint #StopRansomware advisory (AA23-158A, published June 7 and updated June 16, 2023) shipped YARA and indicator-of-compromise rules for the LEMURLOOT web shell and ordered federal civilian agencies to remediate by June 23 after adding CVE-2023-34362 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on June 2.
- Progress Software's own follow-up code review, prompted by the mass exploitation, surfaced two further SQL-injection flaws in MOVEit Transfer within three weeks of the first patch (CVE-2023-35036, disclosed June 9, and CVE-2023-35708, disclosed June 15) — a reminder that one exploited flaw is a reason to audit the surrounding code, not just patch the one CVE.
- Australia appointed its first National Cyber Security Coordinator on June 23, 2023, with the government explicitly citing the HWL Ebsworth breach and its cascading effect on dozens of government agencies as part of the rationale.
- Zapier added a mandatory authenticated-confirmation step before acting on model-issued instructions after Johann Rehberger's chained-plugin disclosure — one of the earliest concrete product fixes driven by LLM-agent security research, months before "prompt injection" entered mainstream security vocabulary.
- Zellis's response — disconnecting its MOVEit server and notifying UK and Irish regulators within days of discovery — was repeatedly held up in press coverage as the contrast case to Capita's 58-hour containment delay the same quarter.
- Toyota committed to continuous, automated auditing of its cloud service configurations after acknowledging that a customer database had gone almost a decade without a routine access review.
- The UK Pensions Regulator's and the ICO's scrutiny of Capita established that a fast alert is not the same as a fast response — the 58-hour gap between detection and containment became a widely cited benchmark for what "high priority" should actually mean operationally.
