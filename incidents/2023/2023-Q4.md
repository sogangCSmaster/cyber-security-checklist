  - https://www.philstar.com/headlines/2023/10/19/2304835/philhealth-13-million-members-affected-data-breach
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/lack-of-antivirus-software-behind-philhealth-ransomware-attack/
```

**What happened.** PhilHealth, the Philippines' state-run national health insurer, was hit by the Medusa ransomware gang on September 22, 2023. Medusa demanded $300,000 to withhold and decrypt the stolen data; PhilHealth refused, in coordination with government cybersecurity agencies, and Medusa began publishing member records on the dark web on October 3, spreading to Telegram and other channels within days. At an October 19 Senate hearing, PhilHealth confirmed roughly 13 million affected members; some press estimates ran as high as 42 million, close to PhilHealth's full membership, though PhilHealth never confirmed that higher figure.

**Entry.** PhilHealth did not publicly identify how Medusa's operators first reached its network. What did surface, through the Senate hearing into the incident, was that the antivirus license covering the affected systems had expired on April 15, 2023 — more than five months before the attack — and had not been renewed because of delays in PhilHealth's own procurement process, leaving the ransomware free to run without the detection layer that should have flagged it.

**Escalation.** Encryption and exfiltration reached PhilHealth's Member Portal, eClaims (electronic hospital-claims submission), and EPRS (employer remittance system) — three separate public-facing applications — indicating the intruders moved beyond a single initial foothold to touch multiple systems before ransomware was deployed.

**Blast radius.** Medusa claimed to have stolen almost 750 GB of data. PhilHealth's own confirmed count, given at the October 19 Senate hearing, was approximately 13 million members whose names, addresses, birthdates, sex, mobile numbers, and PhilHealth ID numbers were exposed; other reporting cited estimates as high as 42 million, though PhilHealth did not itself confirm a figure that high, which is why this record carries a disputed range and medium confidence. Public-facing systems were restored by October 6.

**Human factor.** A five-month gap in antivirus coverage on a system holding tens of millions of citizens' health-insurance records, caused by an internal licensing and procurement lapse rather than any attacker sophistication, is what let Medusa operate largely unimpeded once inside.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-03 (detection is itself monitored — an expired antivirus license is a broken sensor and should itself have triggered an alert), CLOUD-10 (a patch and maintenance SLA with an inventory of what security tooling is supposed to cover which systems), HUMAN-07 (a checklist, with a named owner, for renewing critical security software rather than leaving it to a general procurement queue).

sources:
  - https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/CA%20AG%20-%20CA%20Notification%20Letters.pdf
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/6-9-million-23andme-users-affected-by-data-breach/
  - https://www.techtarget.com/healthtechsecurity/feature/What-the-23andMe-Data-Breach-Reveals-About-Credential-Stuffing
```

**What happened.** Between April and September 2023, attackers used email/password pairs leaked in unrelated prior breaches to log into roughly 14,000 23andMe accounts whose owners had reused those credentials — 23andMe's own systems and password store were never compromised. 23andMe said it began investigating only after stolen data was advertised for sale on a hacking forum, confirmed the breach in early October 2023, and on December 1, 2023 told regulators the true reach was 6.9 million profiles.

**Entry.** Credential stuffing — automated login attempts using previously breached username/password combinations from other services — against 23andMe's own login page, which had no mandatory multi-factor authentication and no rate limiting or breached-password screening strong enough to stop a sustained, low-and-slow campaign running from April through September.

**Escalation.** 23andMe's opt-in "DNA Relatives" feature, which lets a user see profile and relationship information for other users who match as genetic relatives, meant each of the roughly 14,000 directly compromised accounts exposed data belonging to everyone in that account's DNA-Relatives network — turning 14,000 stolen passwords into 6.9 million exposed profiles with no additional credential theft required.

**Blast radius.** 23andMe confirmed that 5.5 million users who had opted into DNA Relatives had profile and some account information accessed, and a further 1.4 million DNA Relatives users had their family-tree profile information accessed — a combined 6.9 million people, including names, birth years, relationship labels, self-reported location, and in many cases ancestry reports. Raw genetic/DNA data was not reported stolen.

**Human factor.** End users reused passwords, but 23andMe's decision not to require MFA by default on a platform holding genetic and family-relationship data, combined with a feature that fanned one compromised account out to potentially hundreds of genetic relatives, is what converted an ordinary credential-stuffing campaign into one of the year's largest exposures of sensitive personal data.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (MFA required, not optional, given the sensitivity of the data), AUTH-06 (rate limiting and breached-password checks that would have slowed or blocked months of stuffing attempts), DATA-10 (a feature that fans one account's data out to others — like DNA Relatives — needs to be rate-limited and have its blast radius capped regardless of opt-in status).

**What happened.** On October 9, 2023, a seller using the handle "pwn0001" listed a database on the cybercrime forum BreachForums claiming to hold 815 million Indian residents' COVID-19 testing records — including full names, ages, genders, addresses, Aadhaar national ID numbers, and passport numbers where applicable — sourced from the Indian Council of Medical Research, the government body that ran India's national COVID-19 testing program. The listing asked $80,000 for the full dataset; the security firm Resecurity was the first to publicly identify and report it.

Sample records posted as proof were partially cross-checked by researchers against the government's own "Verify Aadhaar" portal and appeared authentic, but the true scale of the underlying breach, as opposed to the scale of the claim, remains unconfirmed — which is why this record carries a low-confidence, attacker-claimed figure rather than a verified one.

**Human factor.** A national COVID-testing database that retained full, unmasked Aadhaar and passport numbers years after the acute pandemic-testing period had ended was reachable enough for a criminal seller to export at population scale — a retention decision, not attacker sophistication, is what made a breach of this dataset so damaging if the claim is accurate.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits — pandemic-era testing data tied to national ID numbers should not remain exportable indefinitely), DATA-04 (databases holding identity-linked health data should not be reachable from the public internet under any credential), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal bulk-read or export volume, which an 815-million-row export should have triggered somewhere).

```yaml

escalation: [escalation/overscoped-credential]

sources:
  - https://sec.okta.com/articles/2023/11/unauthorized-access-oktas-support-case-management-system-root-cause/
  - https://sec.okta.com/articles/october-security-incident-recommended-actions/
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2023/11/okta-breach-affected-all-customer-support-users/
```

**What happened.** Between September 28 and October 17, 2023, a threat actor used a compromised service account to access Okta's customer support case management system, reading files — including HTTP Archive (HAR) diagnostic files that customers upload so Okta support can reproduce login problems — for at least 134 customer organizations. Okta first disclosed the breach on October 20 describing limited scope; a November 3 root-cause report and a further November 29 update progressively revealed a much larger picture, including that the names and email addresses of substantially all (99.6%) users of Okta's support system had also been accessed.

**Entry.** An Okta employee had signed into a personal Google account on the Chrome browser of their Okta-managed laptop, and the username and password of a service account — with permission to view and update customer support cases — had been saved into that personal account, most likely through browser profile sync. Okta's own report states the "most likely avenue for exposure of this credential is the compromise of the employee's personal Google account or personal device," but Okta never disclosed how that personal account itself was compromised.

**Escalation.** The service account's permissions were broader than any single support interaction required — full read/update access across the case management system — so once the attacker had its credentials, no further privilege escalation was needed to browse and download support files and, eventually, to run a report enumerating every customer support user's name and email address.

**Blast radius.** HAR files from at least 134 organizations were accessed; within those, session tokens were live enough that the attacker hijacked active sessions at five Okta customers. 1Password and BeyondTrust each publicly described being targeted within the October window — 1Password first flagged suspicious activity to Okta on September 29, and BeyondTrust supplied a specific IOC on October 13 that let Okta finally identify the compromised service account on October 19, a 19-day gap from first customer warning to root-cause identification. Okta's November 29 update disclosed that names and email addresses for essentially its entire base of roughly 18,400 customer organizations' support-system contacts had also been downloaded. Cloudflare's credentials were exposed in this same breach but were not immediately misused; Cloudflare's own intrusion came weeks later, in November, when it failed to rotate some of the exposed credentials in time (recorded in `../2024/2024-Q1.md`, filed by its February 2024 disclosure date).

**Human factor.** No policy or technical control stopped an employee from signing into a personal Google account on a company-managed laptop and syncing a privileged service account's password into it — one ordinary browser habit on one machine became the entry point for a chain that reached at least three other major security and infrastructure vendors.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (managed developer machines that block personal credential sync, full stop), VENDOR-04 (support artifacts like HAR files should be sanitized of live session tokens before they are stored or shared, or those tokens invalidated the moment a diagnostic file is generated), CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook with a stated maximum time-to-revoke — Okta's own gap between first warning and containment ran three weeks).

```

**What happened.** At the end of October 2023, the Akira ransomware group breached Südwestfalen IT (SIT), a shared IT service provider running core systems for 72 towns, cities, and districts across the South Westphalia region of North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. The attack took down the shared Windows domain and forced citizen-facing services — vehicle registration, ID card and birth certificate applications, and more — offline for weeks across all 72 affected municipalities, serving roughly 1.6 million residents. Südwestfalen IT refused to pay, in agreement with the affected municipalities.

**Entry.** A forensic report later commissioned by Südwestfalen IT found that attackers reached SIT's internal network through a VPN gateway running a vulnerable version of Cisco's ASA/FTD software (CVE-2023-20269), which allowed an unauthenticated remote attacker to brute-force VPN credentials because the affected feature enforced no account lockout and no multi-factor authentication. Investigators concluded the attackers simply guessed a working password through repeated attempts.

**Escalation.** Once inside the VPN, the attackers reached SIT's central Windows domain (intra.lan) and moved through Active Directory to systems shared across all connected municipalities, consistent with Akira's documented playbook of pivoting from an exposed VPN foothold to the domain controller before encrypting broadly; investigators later found files bearing the ".akira" extension confirming the ransomware family.

**Blast radius.** All 72 member municipalities lost access to shared applications — including vehicle registration, ID and passport processing, social-services systems, and email — for weeks, with some services still limited more than two weeks after the attack. Südwestfalen IT pursued recovery from backups rather than paying the ransom.

**Human factor.** A single VPN gateway, reachable from the internet, protected by a password alone with no MFA and no lockout on repeated failed attempts, was the only thing standing between the public internet and the shared systems of 72 local governments — a single-factor, brute-forceable front door for over a million and a half residents' government services.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the VPN, which alone would have made a guessed password useless), AUTH-06 (rate limiting and lockout on the authentication surface, which a patched, correctly configured VPN gateway should have enforced), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing infrastructure — Cisco guidance for CVE-2023-20269 was available before this attack).

  - https://grcsolutions.io/the-week-in-cyber-security-and-data-privacy-13-19-november-2023
```

**What happened.** Beginning in May 2023, attackers ran a sustained campaign against Danish energy-sector operators by exploiting a command-injection vulnerability in internet-facing Zyxel firewall appliances used to manage plant and grid-connection equipment. Denmark's SektorCERT, the country's energy-sector CERT, documented 22 separate energy companies compromised across multiple waves through the year, publishing its findings in November 2023 in what press coverage described as the largest recorded cyberattack against Danish infrastructure.

**Entry.** A known, patchable command-injection flaw in Zyxel firewall products, sitting at the internet-facing edge of each victim's operational network, gave attackers a foothold at 22 separate organizations without needing to compromise any one of them first — each victim was reached independently through the same vulnerability class rather than through a single point of entry that then spread.

**Escalation.** Once past the firewall, attackers reached the operational systems those appliances were meant to protect, consistent with the firewalls sitting directly between the internet and plant-control networks with no further segmentation behind them — a flat-network pattern repeated at multiple, independently operated companies rather than one especially deep intrusion at a single site.

**Blast radius.** SektorCERT and subsequent press reporting describe 22 energy companies affected; this record does not carry a specific figure for any physical service disruption, as that level of detail was not present in the sources reviewed for it, though the campaign's scale and SektorCERT's own characterization as Denmark's largest recorded cyberattack indicate a materially significant intrusion into critical-infrastructure operational networks rather than a contained, single-site event.

**Human factor.** Twenty-two separately operated companies sharing the same unpatched, internet-facing firewall model is a sector-wide exposure and patching problem, not a single organization's lapse — a pattern that a sector CERT is specifically positioned to catch across many operators at once, which is what let this campaign be documented as one coordinated finding rather than 22 unrelated incidents.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing OT-adjacent appliances, with an inventory of what security tooling covers which sites, applied consistently across a sector and not just within one company), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between an internet-facing firewall and the operational network behind it, so a single vulnerability class does not translate directly into plant-network access), OBSV-01 (centralized logging and cross-sector information sharing of the kind SektorCERT itself provided, which is what allowed 22 separate, otherwise-isolated intrusions to be recognized as one campaign).

```

**What happened.** In November 2023, Australia's federal Department of Finance uploaded government procurement information to the wrong location, exposing details of bids and suppliers involved in department tenders. No attacker was involved; this was a self-inflicted exposure caused by a handling error during a routine procurement-administration task. Coverage of the incident is limited to a brief mention in trade-press roundups rather than a detailed departmental statement, so several details, including the exact number of suppliers affected, were not available for this record.

**Entry.** Procurement information — material that would ordinarily be visible only to the department and, on a need-to-know basis, to the specific bidder it concerned — was instead uploaded somewhere other organizations or the public could see it, exposing competing suppliers' bid and contract details to each other and to anyone able to reach the wrong location.

**Escalation.** None required: the mistake itself placed the information somewhere it should not have been, and no further access, credential, or technical step was needed for it to be exposed.

**Blast radius.** The department did not publicly disclose how many suppliers or bids were affected, so this record's scale field is undisclosed rather than a specific figure; the exposed data category — competitive bid and supplier information — is nonetheless commercially sensitive to the businesses involved. This sits inside a broader pattern documented by Australia's privacy regulator, the OAIC, whose July–December 2023 Notifiable Data Breaches report found human error responsible for 30% of all reported breaches that half-year (144 of 483), with information sent or published to the wrong recipient the single most common human-error cause (49% of human-error cases) — including, separately, at least 13 misdirection incidents reported by Australian public-sector agencies in that same six-month window.

**Human factor.** A routine step in the procurement-administration workflow — uploading a document or dataset to its intended location — went wrong with no review or check in place to catch that the destination was incorrect before the information became reachable by parties who should never have seen a competitor's bid details.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-10 (recipients and destinations confirmed before data leaves the organization or is published — the single control that would have caught this before publication), DATA-09 (data minimization, so only a specific supplier's own bid material is packaged for any single handling step rather than a bundle covering multiple bidders at once), HUMAN-07 (a checklist for procurement-data handling and publication, rather than relying on an individual operator to get placement right every time).

  - https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/boeing-files-leaked-claim/699579/
```

**What happened.** LockBit 3.0 affiliates exploited the Citrix Bleed vulnerability (CVE-2023-4966) to gain initial access to Boeing Distribution Inc., Boeing's parts-and-distribution business, which runs on a network environment separate from Boeing's main corporate and manufacturing systems. LockBit added Boeing to its extortion leak site on October 27, 2023; Boeing confirmed the intrusion in early November, and when Boeing did not pay, LockBit published roughly 43-45 GB of files on November 15, 2023.

**Entry.** CVE-2023-4966 ("Citrix Bleed"), a critical (CVSS 9.4) information-disclosure flaw in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway appliances, let an unauthenticated remote attacker send a crafted request that leaked valid session tokens directly from the appliance's memory; because those tokens belonged to already-authenticated sessions, presenting one bypassed both password and MFA checks entirely. Citrix shipped a patch on October 10, 2023; LockBit affiliates were exploiting the flaw against Boeing and others within about two and a half weeks of that patch, before Boeing had applied it, per CISA's joint advisory on the campaign.

**Escalation.** A stolen session token on the internet-facing NetScaler appliance gave the attacker an authenticated foothold with no further credential needed; from there, LockBit's affiliates moved laterally within Boeing Distribution Inc.'s separate network environment to stage data and deploy ransomware broadly across it.

**Blast radius.** LockBit published roughly 43-45 GB of files; reporting on the leak's contents describes system backups and internal business and operational documents, and CISA's advisory notes the intrusion also involved theft of employee personal data. Boeing has not published its own detailed accounting of exactly what was in the leaked set, which is why the scale figure here carries a range rather than a single number.

**Human factor.** Boeing had roughly two and a half weeks between Citrix's patch release and confirmed exploitation to patch an internet-facing remote-access appliance, in the middle of a vulnerability CISA and international partners were actively warning about — the gap between patch availability and patch application, not any novel attacker technique, is what let LockBit in.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing appliances, with Citrix Bleed patched within days rather than weeks), AUTH-04 (sessions that are short-lived and revocable so a leaked token has a narrow window of use), CLOUD-05 (segmentation strong enough that a breach of one business unit's "separate" environment does not still let ransomware spread broadly within it).

**What happened.** On November 10, 2023, DP World Australia — which operates four container terminals handling about 40% of the country's shipping-container trade — detected unauthorized network access and disconnected its systems from the internet, shutting down landside freight operations at ports in Sydney, Melbourne, Fremantle, and Brisbane. **Human factor.** A nine-day gap between a critical, actively exploited vulnerability's patch release and Xfinity's own systems being compromised — caught only through a routine security exercise rather than targeted monitoring for the flaw — shows how a single unpatched internet-facing appliance can expose tens of millions of people who had no direct relationship with the vulnerable product itself.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing appliances measured in days, not weeks, especially once a CVE is under active mass exploitation), AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions limiting what a single leaked token can reach), DATA-07 (sensitive fields like partial SSNs and security answers encrypted at rest, limiting what a successful intrusion can actually read even after it reaches the data layer).

**What happened.** A California Chevrolet dealership had deployed a ChatGPT-powered customer-service chatbot, built by vendor Fullpath, on its public website. On December 17, 2023, a user named Chris Bakke typed instructions telling the bot to agree with anything he said and to end every reply with a claim that the statement was "a legally binding offer, no takesies backsies," then asked it to sell him a 2024 Chevrolet Tahoe — normally priced around $76,000–81,000 — for $1. The bot complied; the screenshot went viral within days, and the dealership pulled the chatbot down.

**Entry.** No credential, vulnerability, or system access was needed: the entire technique was a crafted natural-language prompt sent through the same public chat box every ordinary visitor used, which overrode the bot's intended sales-assistant behavior and turned it into an agent that would agree to and "confirm" any deal typed at it.

**Escalation.** None required — the chatbot itself was the target and the full extent of the exploit, extracting an unauthorized commitment directly from a single conversation with no further access needed.

**Blast radius.** No car was actually sold; the dealership and Fullpath stated the chatbot had no authority to bind the business to a sale, and the $1 offer was never honored. The impact was reputational and cautionary rather than financial: the screenshot was viewed tens of millions of times and became one of the most widely cited public demonstrations that quarter of prompt injection against a real, customer-facing deployed system.

**Human factor.** Nobody at the dealership or its chatbot vendor appears to have tested what an adversarial customer could talk the bot into before putting it in front of the public with implicit authority to make representations about price and deal terms — a live, unconstrained language model was trusted with a role that needed a hard, non-model-based backstop.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-NEW (a control requiring that business-critical commitments — price, discounts, contract terms — be enforced by code outside the model, never by asking the model itself to behave, since a system prompt is not a security boundary), AGENT-01 (everything a customer types into a public-facing bot is untrusted input, not instruction, and the model should never have been able to redefine its own operating rules from user text), HUMAN-07 (a pre-launch checklist for any public-facing AI deployment that includes adversarial-prompt testing before go-live).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA, the FBI, and international partners issued a joint #StopRansomware advisory (AA23-325A, November 21, 2023) on LockBit affiliates exploiting Citrix Bleed, naming Boeing among the victims and prompting accelerated NetScaler patching worldwide — the same flaw independently hit DP World Australia and Comcast's Xfinity within weeks.
- Cloudflare's public "Code Red" post-mortem (published February 1, 2024) became a widely cited template for treating a vendor breach's credential-exposure list as incomplete by default, and for rehearsing an "assume everything touched is compromised" rotation rather than a best-guess one.
- Okta published its own root-cause analysis and progressively expanded its disclosed impact three times (October 20, November 3, November 29), later restricting session-token reuse across new IP/device combinations and revising customer guidance on redacting HAR files before uploading them to support.
- 23andMe made MFA mandatory for all users in the weeks following disclosure, having previously left it optional, and added extra authentication for accounts participating in DNA Relatives.
- Germany's IT-security community used the Südwestfalen IT incident as a public case study for municipal shared-service providers to require MFA universally on remote-access VPNs rather than relying on password strength alone.
- Australia's government cited the DP World Australia incident, among others, in accelerating reforms under its critical-infrastructure security legislation.
- OpenAI patched the specific repeated-token prompt pattern used in the ChatGPT training-data extraction research and updated its usage policies to explicitly prohibit attempts to extract training data, while the researchers publicly noted that a prompt-level fix does not address the underlying memorization.
