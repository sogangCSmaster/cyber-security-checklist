sources:
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/april-2025-healthcare-data-breach-report/
```

**What happened.** A mailing vendor working for Gilead Sciences sent IRS Form 1095-C — the annual statement of employer-provided health coverage — to 12,224 current and former employees in envelopes that printed each recipient's own Social Security number, encoded in an 18-digit string, on the outside of the envelope. Anyone who handled a piece of that mail before it was opened — a mailroom, a household member, a delivery contractor — could read the number without opening anything. Gilead reported the incident as required, and it appeared among the healthcare sector's April 2025 breach disclosures.

**Entry.** Inadvertent disclosure through a printing and mailing process, not an intrusion. The vendor's mail-merge template placed a code containing the Social Security number on the envelope's exterior — a decision made once, in a template, that then repeated identically across every one of 12,224 pieces of mail.

**Escalation.** None required. The data was legible without opening the envelope, so there was no second step and nothing to escalate.

**Blast radius.** 12,224 individuals had their Social Security number exposed to anyone who saw the envelope before delivery or before it was opened. Because the coding error compounds identically across a print run, this is the mailing equivalent of a single misconfiguration reaching every downstream record: one template mistake, twelve thousand identical copies of it.

**Human factor.** A one-time decision at a vendor — how to format a mail-merge field, which code goes on the envelope face and which stays inside — was never proofed by anyone downstream who would have recognised the SSN pattern before the print run shipped. Gilead outsourced the mailing but not the consequence: the recipients whose numbers ended up on the outside of an envelope do not distinguish between Gilead and its vendor.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-10 (recipients and destinations confirmed, and the printed artifact itself checked, before data leaves the organization — here, before a print vendor's template ships), HUMAN-07 (a checklist for any mailing that carries an identifier, rather than reliance on a vendor's competence alone), HUMAN-02 (a second person reviews the physical proof of a high-impact mailing before thousands of copies are produced).

  - https://www.fsa.go.jp/en/conference/minister/2025/20250513.html
```

**What happened.** Through the first four months of 2025, criminals logged into Japanese retail brokerage accounts with credentials harvested from phishing sites that cloned real securities firms, then used the victims' own accounts to buy thin, illiquid stocks the attackers already held, sold their own position into the inflated price, and left the victim holding the stock. Japan's Financial Services Agency began publishing running totals on 18 April 2025; by its 9 May report the campaign had produced roughly ¥300 billion in combined buy and sell transactions between January and April. The monthly curve is the striking part: 65 unauthorized-access cases in January became 4,852 in April, and 39 fraudulent trades became 2,746, across nine securities firms.

**Entry.** Credential phishing. Victims reached lookalike login pages for real brokerages — served through search ads, mail and messaging — entered their trading credentials, and the attackers replayed them against the genuine platform. Several of the affected firms authenticated online trading with a password and, at most, an optional second factor; nothing in the login path distinguished the attacker's session from the customer's.

**Escalation.** None required. A brokerage login is the crown jewel by construction: it authorizes trades. The attackers needed no privilege beyond the account they had already taken over, and the market manipulation was executed entirely through intended product functionality.

**Blast radius.** The FSA's April figures alone cover 4,852 unauthorized-access cases and 2,746 fraudulent trades at nine firms; the January–April transaction value it reported is about ¥300 billion (roughly $2 billion) in gross buys and sells, not a net loss figure, and no consolidated customer-loss total was published. The running totals moved substantially between releases — an FSA figure reported on 18 April put the running total nearer $700 million, before the 9 May report restated the four-month picture — which is why this record carries medium confidence and gross transaction value rather than a loss number. Major brokerages including Nomura, Rakuten Securities, SBI and SMBC Nikko were among those reporting cases, and several announced they would compensate affected customers.

**Human factor.** Retail brokerages in Japan had treated multi-factor authentication as a customer convenience setting rather than a condition of trading. Firms competing on frictionless onboarding left the strongest control optional, and customers who had never been asked to turn it on did not.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA as a precondition for placing a trade, not an opt-in preference), AUTH-09 (a factor that a cloned login page cannot relay — number matching or a hardware key, not an OTP the victim types into the attacker's site), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal trading behaviour per account, since buying a single illiquid small-cap at market is not what most retail accounts do).

sources:
  - https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/08/a-timeline-of-south-korean-telco-giant-skts-data-breach/
  - https://securityaffairs.com/176802/data-breach/sk-telecom-data-breach.html
```

**What happened.** South Korea's largest mobile operator detected abnormal activity in billing-monitoring equipment late on 18 April 2025, traced it the next day to its Home Subscriber Server in Seoul — the database that holds SIM authentication material for every subscriber — reported the incident to the Korea Internet & Security Agency on 20 April, and confirmed it publicly on 22 April. The subsequent government investigation by Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT searched SK Telecom's estate of roughly 42,600 servers and found 28 of them infected with 33 malware samples, including 27 variants of the BPFDoor Linux backdoor, three TinyShell implants, a web shell and two open-source command-and-control frameworks.

**Entry.** Never conclusively established and not disclosed by SK Telecom. Press reporting during the investigation pointed at an Ivanti Connect Secure VPN appliance as the likely first foothold, but neither the company nor MSIT published a confirmed initial access vector, so this record tags `entry/unknown` rather than adopting the reporting.

**Escalation.** BPFDoor hides its command channel inside Berkeley Packet Filter rules on Linux hosts, so operator traffic never appears as an inbound connection to ordinary network monitoring; that is what allowed the intruders to hold and extend access across dozens of servers for years. The investigation's other finding is the plainer one: SK Telecom had stored critical subscriber authentication material without encryption, and its credential management was judged inadequate, so reaching the Home Subscriber Server was the same thing as reading it.

**Blast radius.** MSIT's investigation concluded that 25 categories of USIM data totalling about 9.82 GB left the network, corresponding to roughly 26.96 million IMSI records; SK Telecom itself described around 23 million affected subscribers, in a country of about 52 million people. The stolen material — IMSI identifiers, phone numbers and USIM authentication keys — is precisely what is needed to clone a SIM, making every affected subscriber a SIM-swap candidate. SK Telecom began free SIM replacement on 28 April and ran into supply shortages; it brought a SIM-protection service online by 14 May, and told investors that waiving cancellation fees could cost it up to several billion dollars over three years — a projection, not a booked cost, which is why `cost_usd` here is `undisclosed`. MSIT dated the earliest infection to 2022, roughly three years before detection; because the precise first-access date was not published, `dwell_days` stays `unknown` while the three-year figure is the point.

**Human factor.** SK Telecom had seen indications of compromise as early as 2022 and did not report or fully remediate them. Three years of an unread signal, unencrypted authentication keys, and log retention that could not reconstruct the intrusion are all the same decision made repeatedly: treat the core network as trusted because it is internal.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-07 (encrypt subscriber authentication keys at rest so that reading the HSS is not the same as stealing every SIM identity), OBSV-03 (a detection capability that is itself monitored — three years of BPFDoor presence is a broken sensor, and a broken sensor is an incident), OBSV-08 (log retention exceeding realistic dwell time, which for a telecom core is measured in years rather than weeks), CLOUD-09 and OBSV-01 (an inventory of 42,600 servers with centrally logged admin activity, so a compromise is enumerable rather than discoverable only by forensic sweep).

  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/co-op-confirms-data-of-65-million-members-stolen-in-cyberattack/
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/four-arrested-in-uk-over-mands-co-op-harrods-cyberattacks/
```

**What happened.** Threat actors linked to Scattered Spider broke into Marks & Spencer on 17 April 2025, deployed the DragonForce ransomware encryptor against VMware ESXi hosts, and forced the retailer to shut down systems across 1,400 stores and suspend online ordering entirely. Within two weeks the same crew hit Co-op, which shut down parts of its IT estate and had to rebuild its Windows domain controllers, and attempted Harrods, which restricted internet access to its sites to keep them out. M&S chairman Archie Norman described the entry to a UK Parliament Business and Trade Sub-Committee hearing; four suspects aged 17 to 20 were arrested by the National Crime Agency on 10 July.

**Entry.** A phone call. Norman told MPs the attackers impersonated one of the 50,000 people who work with M&S, "appeared as somebody with their details," and persuaded a third party to reset that employee's password. The third party was M&S's outsourced IT help desk, operated by Tata Consultancy Services, which began its own investigation into whether it had been the point tricked. No software vulnerability was exploited to get in; the reset was performed by a human following a normal process for what looked like a normal request.

**Escalation.** Once holding a valid employee identity, the attackers moved into Active Directory, obtained domain-level privilege, and reached the VMware ESXi hypervisors hosting the estate. Co-op's response — rebuilding its Windows domain controllers from scratch — is the clearest statement of how deep directory compromise went in these intrusions. At M&S, encryption of the ESXi layer meant that shutting down systems to stop the spread was already too late; data had also been staged out, with roughly 150 GB reported stolen.

**Blast radius.** M&S paused online ordering for around six weeks, from late April into July, and told the London Stock Exchange it expected a group operating profit impact of around £300 million ($402 million) for 2025/26 before mitigation through cost management, insurance and trading actions. Co-op's interim results put its hit at £80 million ($107 million) of operating profit — £20 million of one-off costs plus £60 million of lost sales — against a £206 million revenue reduction, with a further £20 million expected in the second half; it later confirmed the personal data of all 6.5 million members was stolen, names and contact details rather than payment data. DragonForce never posted M&S to its leak site, which M&S has not explained; Norman told MPs that nobody at M&S dealt with the attackers directly.

**Human factor.** The help desk was doing its job. An outsourced service desk serving 50,000 people is measured on resolution time, has no independent way to verify that the voice on the line is the employee whose details it can recite, and sits organizationally outside the retailer that bears the consequence of getting it wrong. That gap — accountability in one company, the reset button in another — is the vulnerability.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential or MFA reset that does not depend on what the caller can recite — a manager callback, an in-person check, or a device-bound attestation), AUTH-05 (make the recovery path as strong as the login path, because attackers will always take the weaker of the two), HUMAN-04 (hold an outsourced service desk to the same reset controls as an internal one, and audit that they are followed), CLOUD-05 (segmentation so that one recovered employee account cannot reach the hypervisor layer that runs the whole estate).

MTN said its core network, billing systems and financial services infrastructure, including its mobile-money platform, showed no evidence of compromise and remained fully operational, and it notified the South African Police Service, the Hawks priority-crime unit, and authorities in the affected countries.

**What happened.** Hunter Health Clinic, a community health centre serving the Wichita, Kansas area, reported in May 2025 that an unauthorised party had accessed one employee's email account and, through it, patient information belonging to 28,431 individuals. The clinic disclosed the breach as required under U.S. health-privacy law; no further detail about the intrusion has been made public.

```

**What happened.** Criminals paid cash to a group of Coinbase customer-support contractors working outside the United States, in India, to copy customer records out of internal support tooling. The theft began on 26 December 2024. Coinbase detected and fired individual agents as they were caught accessing records without a business reason from January onward, but only learned the full scope on 11 May 2025, when the buyers emailed a $20 million extortion demand threatening to publish what they had. Coinbase refused to pay, disclosed publicly on 15 May, and announced a $20 million reward fund for information leading to arrests instead.

Ordinary support-agent access was sufficient to read names, addresses, phone numbers and emails, the last four digits of Social Security numbers, masked bank account numbers and some bank identifiers, images of government ID documents including driving licences and passports, account balance snapshots and transaction histories, plus internal corporate documents, training material and communications visible to support staff. Passwords, private keys and Coinbase Prime accounts were not reachable, and no wallets were drained directly.

**Blast radius.** Coinbase notified 69,461 individuals, under 1% of its monthly transacting users. The stolen identity-document images and balance snapshots were the actual product: they let the buyers run convincing impersonation calls against wealthy customers and social-engineer them into sending crypto to attacker wallets, losses Coinbase committed to reimburse. In its SEC filing Coinbase estimated $180–400 million in remediation and voluntary customer reimbursement; this record carries the upper bound, with the range stated here because the final figure was not settled at disclosure.

**Human factor.** An outsourced support workforce paid far less than the value of the data it can read, in a jurisdiction where a bribe goes further, with per-record lookup access to identity documents and balances — and detection that caught agents one at a time over four months without anyone connecting them into a pattern. Coinbase had the signal; what it lacked was the aggregation.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-09 (monitor insider risk by behaviour — lookup volume, off-ticket access, off-hours patterns — rather than by trust, and correlate across agents instead of firing them individually), DATA-12 and DATA-11 (cap what a single support session can reach, and make bulk or repeated identity-document retrieval a privileged, alerting action), HUMAN-04 (contractors and subprocessors get the same controls, monitoring and separation of duties as employees), AUTH-07 (support and impersonation tooling behind separate authentication with full audit, so that reading a passport scan is an event and not a page view).

**What happened.** Asana launched a Model Context Protocol server on 1 May 2025, letting customers connect LLM tools to their workspaces for summarisation, natural-language queries and smart replies. A logic flaw in that server's tenant scoping meant data from one customer's Asana instance could surface in another customer's MCP responses. Asana found the flaw itself on 4 June, took the feature offline from 5 June to 17 June to fix it, and notified affected organisations directly; the incident became public on 18 June through UpGuard and press coverage. Asana did not publish a statement of its own.

**Entry.** The agent tooling itself. There was no attacker and no intrusion — Asana was explicit that this was not the result of a hack or malicious activity. The MCP server, a newly built integration surface bolted onto a mature multi-tenant product, did not carry that product's tenant boundary into its own query path.

**Escalation.** None required. Data crossed the tenant boundary inside the MCP server's own responses. Exposure was still bounded by each user's access scope, so no organisation's entire workspace became public; what leaked to another tenant was what a user at that level could see — task-level detail, project metadata, team information, comments and discussions, and uploaded files.

**Blast radius.** Asana assessed approximately 1,000 customers as potentially exposed, out of a base of over 130,000 paying customers. There is no indication any user actually viewed another organisation's data, and no confirmed abuse, which is why `impact/none-confirmed` stands alone here. The window from feature launch to discovery was 34 days, recorded in `dwell_days` — with the caveat that nothing was dwelling except the defect. Asana advised administrators to review MCP access logs, re-read AI-generated summaries for content that did not belong to them, and pause bot pipelines and auto-reconnections until trust was re-established.

**Human factor.** Speed. An MCP server was shipped six months into the protocol's existence, into a market moving fast enough that being late felt more dangerous than being wrong, and the cross-tenant negative test that Asana's core product certainly has was not extended to the new surface. The pattern is not specific to Asana: an AI integration is treated as a feature rather than as a new data-access path requiring the same isolation proof as the API it wraps.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-02 (a negative test proving tenant A cannot read tenant B's data, run against the MCP surface before launch and not only against the REST API), AGENT-05 (treat an MCP endpoint as an authenticated, authorization-enforcing data interface, not a convenience wrapper), AGENT-07 (log agent actions with their originating prompt, which is what made "review your MCP access logs" a usable instruction to customers rather than an empty one), OBSV-01 (central logging of authorization outcomes on the new surface from day one).

controls: [AUTH-01, AUTH-06, CRED-03, OBSV-07]
sources:
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/no-the-16-billion-credentials-leak-is-not-a-new-data-breach/
```

**What happened.** In mid-June 2025, headlines worldwide described "one of the largest data breaches in history": 16 billion credentials exposed, with warnings to change every password for Apple, Google and Facebook accounts. It was not a breach. Cybernews had observed briefly exposed datasets containing compiled credentials stored in a format associated with infostealer malware. The credentials were old — stolen over years from individual infected devices, from prior breaches and from credential-stuffing collections — repackaged into one large file and left on an open host. No named company was newly compromised, and no evidence was presented that the compilation contained previously unseen data.

**Entry.** Infostealer malware on end-user devices, accumulated over years. When such malware runs on a machine it dumps every credential saved in browsers, files and applications into a "log" — plain lines of `URL:username:password` — which is uploaded to the operator and then sold, traded, or given away free on Telegram, Pastebin and Discord to build reputation. A single 1.26 GB file circulating publicly held over 64,000 credential pairs; thousands of such archives exist, and concatenating them produces billions of lines quickly.

**Escalation.** None required and none involved. There was no intrusion into any service to escalate from. The "16 billion" figure is the line count of a concatenation, and it is not deduplicated: the same credential appears many times across many source logs, and many entries are long dead.

**Blast radius.** Genuinely unknown, and the honest answer is that nobody can say. The `scale` field records 16,000,000,000 records because that is the figure that was reported, and `confidence: low` records that it is a headline number for an unverified, undeduplicated aggregation whose samples were never shared — not a count of affected people, not a count of valid credentials, and not attributable to any breach event. Prior compilations behaved the same way: RockYou2024 claimed over 9 billion records, and Collection #1 resolved to about 22 million genuinely unique passwords.

**Human factor.** Two. The one that created the data is password reuse — an infostealer log is only dangerous because the credentials in it work somewhere else. The one that created the story is the incentive structure of breach reporting: a large number with no denominator, no deduplication and no named victim travels faster than a correction, and organisations spent real response hours on an event that had not occurred. That is why this record sits in the corpus at all.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA, which makes a leaked password insufficient regardless of how many copies of it exist), AUTH-06 (breached-password checks and rate limiting at every auth surface, so stuffing a compilation against your login page fails), CRED-03 (rotate on exposure as a standing habit rather than a reaction to headlines), OBSV-07 (rehearse the "our credentials are already public" scenario, so that the answer to a story like this is a known procedure and not a fire drill).

**What happened.** On 18 June 2025, during the twelve-day Israel-Iran war, the pro-Israel hacktivist group Gonjeshke Darande ("Predatory Sparrow") drained roughly $90 million from the hot wallets of Nobitex, Iran's largest cryptocurrency exchange, reported to serve around 7 million users. Rather than keep or launder the funds, the attackers sent them to vanity cryptocurrency addresses generated to spell out anti-IRGC slogans — addresses whose private keys nobody holds, making the money permanently unspendable by anyone, attacker included. Two days later the same group published Nobitex's entire source code, infrastructure documentation and internal privacy-engineering research online. The group had also claimed an attack on the state-owned Bank Sepah the day before, disrupting its services.

**Entry.** Not confirmed. Blockchain-analytics writeups describe the likely root cause as compromised hot-wallet private keys, but this is analyst inference from on-chain behaviour, not a technical finding Nobitex or an independent forensic report has confirmed publicly, so this record tags `entry/unknown`.

**Escalation.** None required — the hot wallets were the objective, and whatever access was obtained reached them directly. Nothing indicates movement through additional systems before the funds moved.

**Blast radius.** Approximately $90 million in bitcoin, ether, dogecoin, ripple, solana, tron and ton, destroyed rather than stolen for profit — evidence that a state-linked financial target can be attacked for pure disruption, with no expectation of monetary gain to the attacker. The follow-on leak of Nobitex's source code and internal architecture is arguably the larger long-term exposure: it hands every future attacker, and every other exchange running similar code, a detailed map of how an exchange under sanctions evades blockchain analytics and integrates with domestic banking rails.

**Human factor.** Without a confirmed technical mechanism, the visible fact is structural: an exchange serving millions of users under international sanctions held tens of millions of dollars in wallets connected closely enough to day-to-day operations that a single compromise could empty them in one action, and nothing intercepted the drain before it completed. Operating under sanctions and cut off from mainstream custody and insurance providers narrows an exchange's options, but the resulting concentration of value in operationally accessible wallets is still a choice, not an inevitability.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-12 (cap what a single compromise can reach, in value and in volume — the core argument for cold storage holding the large majority of any exchange's reserves), CRED-06 (signing credentials scoped to one job, ideally requiring multiple independent approvals rather than a single key or session being sufficient to move funds), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal outbound transaction volume, so a $90 million drain triggers a circuit breaker before it completes rather than after).

**What happened.** Aflac, the largest supplemental insurance provider in the United States, disclosed on 20 June 2025 that its network had been breached on 12 June as part of a campaign against US insurers. It was the third in nine days: Erie Insurance on 7 June, Philadelphia Insurance Companies on 9 June, Aflac on 12 June. Aflac said it stopped the intrusion within hours, that no ransomware affected its systems, and that business operations continued. It told the SEC the files potentially accessed contained claims information, health information, Social Security numbers and other personal data belonging to customers, beneficiaries, employees and agents.

**Entry.** Aflac's own statement said only that a sophisticated cybercrime group used social engineering. The campaign it belonged to was characterised by phone calls to corporate IT help desks in which the caller, posing as an employee, persuaded staff to reset a password or re-enrol multi-factor authentication to a device the attacker controlled — after which a self-service password reset in Okta or Entra ID completed the takeover. This record tags `entry/help-desk` on that campaign-level evidence; Aflac itself never specified the mechanism.

**Escalation.** One reset account reached document stores holding claims files, health information and Social Security numbers across customers, beneficiaries, employees and agents alike. Aflac has not described lateral movement or privilege escalation and says it contained the intrusion within hours of initial access, which is why `dwell_days` is 0 — the fastest detection in this quarter's file, and it still was not fast enough, because the account it stopped could already read everything.

**Blast radius.** Aflac gave no figure at disclosure. In December 2025 it confirmed the personal and health data of approximately 22.65 million people; separate HHS breach filings account for at least 13.9 million individuals whose protected health information was involved. The 22.65 million figure is recorded here with medium confidence because it was established six months after the quarter in which the incident was disclosed, and the two counts measure different populations rather than conflicting.

**Human factor.** The same failure as M&S in April, in a different industry, two months later, after public warnings that the group had moved from retail to insurance. A help-desk agent authenticated a caller on information the caller could recite. What distinguishes Aflac is that detection worked — hours, not weeks — and it still did not prevent the loss, because the blast radius of one ordinary account was 22 million people's health and identity records. On attribution: Aflac never named the group, saying only that the actor "may be affiliated with a known cyber-criminal organization" and that law enforcement and outside experts indicated the insurance industry was being targeted broadly; the `actor/scattered-spider` tag reflects the attribution made by security vendors and press covering the June campaign, not a statement by Aflac.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential or MFA reset that does not rely on what the caller knows), AUTH-09 and AUTH-05 (MFA that cannot be re-enrolled by a phone call, and a recovery path no weaker than the login path), DATA-12 (cap what a single compromised account can reach, in value and in volume — 22 million records should not be reachable from one reset session), DATA-11 (bulk retrieval of claims and health records as a privileged, logged, alerting action).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Microsoft fixed CVE-2025-32711 server-side in the June 2025 Patch Tuesday release, with no customer action required, and stated it had no evidence of customer impact — establishing the pattern that AI-surface vulnerabilities in a hosted assistant are remediated by the vendor rather than patched by the tenant, and that the tenant therefore cannot verify the fix.
- Aim Security's EchoLeak write-up named "LLM scope violation" as a vulnerability class: untrusted input, privileged context and an outbound rendering channel combined in one model window. Every mitigation in this quarter's AI records — EchoLeak, GitHub MCP, Asana, Lovable — reduces to removing one of those three legs.
- Invariant Labs published mitigations alongside the GitHub MCP research: per-repository token scoping enforced at a runtime permission layer rather than by the model, and continuous scanning of MCP traffic. Their explicit warning that model alignment alone cannot prevent indirect prompt injection is the durable guidance.
- The UK NCSC issued statements and practical guidance to UK organisations following the retail attacks, calling the M&S, Co-op and Harrods wave a "wake-up call" and directing attention specifically at service-desk identity verification and password-reset procedures — the first national-level advisory in this period aimed at the help desk rather than at software.
- Asana took its MCP server offline for thirteen days (5–17 June) rather than patching it in place, notified affected organisations individually, and advised administrators to audit MCP access logs and re-read AI-generated summaries for content belonging to other tenants — a usable template for withdrawing an AI integration once tenant isolation is in question.
- Coinbase declined the $20 million extortion demand and announced a $20 million reward fund for information leading to arrests, added identity-verification requirements for large withdrawals, and committed to reimburse customers socially engineered into transferring funds — a public post-incident posture that other extortion targets were subsequently measured against.
- Japan's Financial Services Agency published running monthly figures on hijacked brokerage accounts through the quarter and moved toward requiring stronger authentication for online trading logins, turning multi-factor authentication from a customer preference into a regulatory expectation for the sector.
- Lovable shipped a "security scan" feature in version 2.0 on 24 April 2025 that checked whether a Row Level Security policy existed but not whether it was correct. It is included here as a countermeasure that did not work: a check that returns green for a policy permitting everything is worse than no check, because it converts an open question into false assurance.
