# 2025 Q4 — Trusted Toolchains Turned Against Developers

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-dependency — the defining chains of the quarter ran through developer infrastructure: npm packages, Open VSX and VS Code extensions, and a SaaS vendor's OAuth tokens
> - **New technique this quarter:** payloads hidden in Unicode variation selectors that render as nothing in an editor (GlassWorm); an npm worm that destroys the victim's home directory when it cannot exfiltrate (Shai-Hulud 2.0); and Anthropic's report of an intrusion campaign in which an agentic coding tool performed an estimated 80–90% of the operational work
> - **Recurring failure:** six of the fourteen records here never had an entry vector disclosed at all, including two whose victims were regulated financial institutions' data processors
> - **Records in this file:** 14

## Incidents

```yaml
  - https://www.securityweek.com/nearly-30-alleged-victims-of-oracle-ebs-hack-named-on-cl0p-ransomware-site/
  - https://www.theregister.com/2025/11/25/clop_dartmouth_college/
```

**What happened.** The Cl0p extortion crew exploited an unauthenticated remote-code-execution chain in Oracle E-Business Suite, later assigned CVE-2025-61882 (CVSS 9.8), to steal data in bulk from EBS customers before any patch existed. Google Threat Intelligence dated the mass exploitation to around 9 August 2025, with earlier suspicious activity from 10 July; the extortion emails went out from compromised third-party accounts on 29 September, and Oracle shipped an out-of-band emergency patch with indicators of compromise in early October. Victim disclosures then ran through the rest of the quarter.

**Entry.** The chain targeted the Concurrent Processing component of the EBS web tier and required no username or password. Researchers described it as a chain of server-side request forgery, CRLF injection, an authentication bypass, and XSL template injection, ending in remote code execution and a reverse shell on the application host. Some steps touched issues Oracle had fixed in its July 2025 critical patch update; the final link was not patched until October.

**Escalation.** None required. E-Business Suite *is* the finance, HR, and procurement system of record, so code execution in the application tier put the attacker directly on top of the data worth extorting. Cl0p did not need domain admin or lateral movement to complete its objective.

**Blast radius.** Google put the victim count at "dozens" of organizations; Cl0p subsequently named roughly 29 on its leak site, which is a claim rather than a confirmation, and Cl0p historically lists victims weeks after the theft. Organizations that themselves confirmed compromise include Harvard University, The Washington Post, Logitech, Envoy Air (an American Airlines subsidiary), and Dartmouth College. Logitech confirmed in a securities filing that an attacker exploited CVE-2025-61882 to copy internal data; Envoy Air said only limited business and contact information was taken and no customer data.

**Human factor.** Enterprise ERP is patched on a quarterly ceremony, sits behind change-control boards, and is widely treated as too load-bearing to touch quickly — so a class of system that holds an organization's most sensitive records is routinely months behind on fixes and, in many of these deployments, was directly reachable from the internet. The two-month window between first exploitation and the emergency patch was survivable only for organizations that had already removed the EBS web tier from public exposure.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (an internet-facing EBS tier should be a deliberate, enumerated decision, not an accident of the original 2000s deployment), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems plus an inventory of what cannot be patched, so an out-of-band Oracle alert is actionable in hours), DATA-11 and OBSV-02 (bulk export from an ERP is a privileged, alerting event; the quiet removal of gigabytes over weeks is exactly what volume alerting exists to catch).


**What happened.** F5 disclosed in an SEC filing on 15 October 2025 that a nation-state actor had long-term access to its BIG-IP product development environment and its engineering knowledge-management platform, and had exfiltrated portions of BIG-IP source code together with information about vulnerabilities F5 was still working to fix. F5 learned of the intrusion on 9 August 2025 but delayed disclosure at the request of the U.S. Department of Justice. CISA issued Emergency Directive ED 26-01 the same day the disclosure landed.

**Entry.** F5 has not published how the actor first got in. Bloomberg reported the attackers had been inside for at least twelve months before detection, and subsequently identified them as the China-nexus espionage group UNC5221, associated with the BRICKSTORM backdoor — attribution that comes from press reporting, not from F5's own filing.

**Escalation.** F5 named two distinct environments the actor reached: the BIG-IP build and development environment, and the internal knowledge base holding configuration and implementation details for a small percentage of customers. Reaching both implies movement between engineering systems; F5 did not describe the path.

**Blast radius.** The direct loss was intellectual property: source code and a working list of not-yet-fixed flaws, which CISA warned "could enable the threat actor to identify logical flaws and zero-day vulnerabilities" and build exploits for devices that sit in front of government and enterprise networks. The secondary blast radius fell on customers. ED 26-01 required federal agencies to inventory F5 BIG-IP hardware and software, check whether management interfaces were publicly reachable, apply updates by 22 October, disconnect public-facing end-of-support devices, report by 29 October, patch remaining devices by 31 October, and submit a full inventory by 3 December. F5 said it had seen no indication the stolen vulnerability information had been exploited.

**Human factor.** A vendor's own pre-release vulnerability tracker is, by construction, a list of working exploits against its entire installed base — yet it lived in an engineering knowledge system that went a year without anyone noticing a resident intruder. The decision that matters here is an organizational one: the build environment for a security appliance was not monitored to the standard of the production estate it protects.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-08 (treat the build and product-development environment as production and monitor it as such), OBSV-03 and OBSV-08 (detection that is itself monitored, and log retention that exceeds a realistic dwell time — a year of access is not reconstructable from 90 days of logs), VENDOR-06 (on the customer side, monitoring vendor advisories and having a path to act on an emergency directive within a week).

```yaml
scale: 14 extensions

dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [AGENT-09, AGENT-04, DEPS-03, CRED-03]
sources:
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/self-spreading-glassworm-infects-vs.html
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/11/glassworm-malware-discovered-in-three.html
```

**What happened.** Koi Security documented a campaign it named GlassWorm in which fourteen editor extensions — thirteen on the Open VSX Registry, one on Microsoft's VS Code Marketplace — were published with malicious code that does not render in a code editor. The first infection wave dated to 17 October 2025. A second batch of three infected extensions with a few thousand installs each was reported on 10 November, showing the campaign was not finished when the first report landed.

**Entry.** The payload was written using Unicode variation selectors, characters that are valid Unicode but produce no visual output. As researcher Idan Dardikman put it, the malicious code "literally disappears" in the editor: a reviewer scrolling the extension's source sees whitespace where the payload is. How the publishing accounts themselves were hijacked was never established. Because VS Code extensions auto-update by default, the malicious version reached installed machines without any user action.

**Escalation.** The payload harvested npm, Open VSX, GitHub, and Git credentials from the developer's machine and used them to publish further infected extensions and packages — the credential theft *was* the propagation mechanism. It also targeted 49 cryptocurrency wallet extensions for direct theft, installed a SOCKS proxy to relay traffic through the victim's machine, and deployed hidden VNC for interactive access. Command and control ran over Solana blockchain transaction memo fields, with Google Calendar events holding base64-encoded fallback addresses — infrastructure that cannot be taken down by seizing a domain.

**Blast radius.** The registries recorded roughly 35,800 downloads across the affected extensions; the Eclipse Foundation later argued that figure overstates real users because much of that traffic was bot-generated, which is why this record carries the extension count rather than a user count. Confirmed losses are developer credentials and crypto wallet funds; no downstream customer breach was tied to GlassWorm within the quarter.

**Human factor.** Every control in the extension ecosystem assumes a human or a scanner can read the code. A developer who does the responsible thing — opening the extension source before installing — sees nothing, and the auto-update default means most never get that choice at all.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-09 (scan for invisible and homoglyph characters in anything that will execute — this is the control this incident exists to justify, and it costs one lint rule), AGENT-04 (pin and version-diff IDE extensions rather than letting them auto-update), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before adopting a newly published version), CRED-03 (rotate npm, registry, and Git tokens on any suspicion that a developer machine ran untrusted code).

```yaml
controls: [CRED-01, CRED-05, CRED-07, CICD-05]
sources:
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/eclipse-foundation-revokes-leaked-open.html
```

**What happened.** Wiz researchers found that extension developers on both Microsoft's VS Code Marketplace and Open VSX had committed their publisher access tokens into public repositories. The Eclipse Foundation, which operates Open VSX, revoked the affected tokens and published its response on 31 October 2025 — in the middle of the GlassWorm cleanup, which made the finding considerably more pointed.

**Entry.** Nobody was attacked. The tokens were sitting in public source control, and a researcher looked. Eclipse's security lead Mikaël Barbero was explicit that the exposures "resulted from developer mistakes, not a compromise of the Open VSX infrastructure."

**Escalation.** None required. A publisher token is the whole authorization: anyone holding one can publish or modify that publisher's extensions, which auto-update onto every installed machine. As Wiz put it, an attacker with these tokens could "seize control and distribute malware, effectively poisoning the extension supply chain."

**Blast radius.** No confirmed abuse. This is the most instructive category of record in the corpus: the same access that GlassWorm's operators obtained by unknown means was, for a small number of publishers, simply lying in public — found by a researcher first, and revoked before anyone used it.

**Human factor.** A developer publishing a free editor extension in their spare time has no secret-management process, no CI separation, and no reason to think of their publish token as production credentials for tens of thousands of machines. The registry's defaults — long-lived tokens with no distinguishable format — left that mistake invisible to automated scanning until Eclipse and Microsoft's MSRC agreed on the `ovsxat_` prefix.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-05 and CRED-01 (secret file patterns ignored before the first commit; no live token in a repository, public or private), CRED-07 (tokens that expire by default — Eclipse's response was precisely to cut default token lifetime), CICD-05 (publish credentials issued per-repository through CI rather than held on a laptop and pasted into a config file).


**What happened.** In early November 2025 a trove reported at more than 12,000 internal documents belonging to Knownsec, a Chinese security contractor, was posted to GitHub and, around 7 November, offered on a dark-web forum by an actor using the alias "t1g3r." GitHub removed the repository. The material described offensive tooling and target lists; analysts drew the obvious comparison to the 2024 i-Soon leak, and treated this as a second data point on the same contractor ecosystem rather than an isolated event.

**Entry.** Unknown. No intrusion was ever described. Resecurity's analysis leans toward an insider leak rather than an external compromise, quoting an observer who read it as "a tactic to stir up an internal power struggle" — a hypothesis, not a finding, which is why this record is tagged `entry/unknown` and carries low confidence. Knownsec and the Chinese foreign ministry issued blanket denials.

**Escalation.** None required if the insider theory holds: the documents were already inside the perimeter of someone authorized to read them, and publishing required nothing but an upload.

**Blast radius.** Reporting on the contents described cross-platform remote access tools for Windows, Linux, macOS, iOS, and Android; an email interception platform; a network reconnaissance capability; and surveillance target lists spanning more than twenty countries. The files also reportedly contained data already taken from foreign targets, including some 95 GB of Indian immigration records, roughly 3 TB of South Korean call logs, and about 459 GB of Taiwanese transport data. Every figure here is from the leaked material as read by outside analysts; none is independently confirmed, and the company disputes the whole account.

**Human factor.** An offensive contractor that retains the product of past operations — bulk foreign datasets, sitting on internal shares years after collection — converts one disgruntled or careless person into a strategic disclosure. The retention decision, not the leak, is what set the size of this loss.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-09 (insider risk monitored by behaviour, not by clearance or trust), DATA-09 (minimization and enforced retention, so old operational data is not still sitting there to be taken), DATA-11 and OBSV-02 (bulk access to document stores logged and alerting on volume, which is the only sensor that fires on an authorized user acting abnormally).


**What happened.** On 12 November 2025 the payment processor Checkout.com published a statement by its CTO saying that the ShinyHunters extortion group had obtained data from "a legacy third-party cloud file storage system, used in 2020 and prior years" which, in the company's own words, "was not decommissioned properly." Checkout.com refused to pay and donated the demanded sum to Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Oxford's cyber security centre instead.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. Checkout.com described what the attackers reached but not how they reached it; the system was a third-party file store the company had stopped using years earlier and had left standing.

Checkout.com put the affected population at under a quarter of its current merchant base, and stated that no merchant funds and no card numbers were accessed and that live payment processing was unaffected. The identity documents are the durable loss: a passport scan from 2014 is still a passport scan.

**Human factor.** Someone migrated off a file store and never finished the job, and no inventory ever asked the question again. Nine years of KYC identity documents survived because deletion was nobody's task; meanwhile the company had clearly rehearsed the other half of the problem, because its disclosure and refusal-to-pay decision shipped as a single public statement with a named executive behind it.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (enforced retention — KYC documents from 2010 to 2019 should not have existed in 2025), CLOUD-09 (an asset inventory that finds and removes unowned, forgotten systems; a decommissioning that is verified rather than assumed), DATA-03 (legacy object storage audited and private by default), OBSV-05 (the one control Checkout.com visibly had: a plan naming the decider and the communicator before the ransom note arrived).

**What happened.** Anthropic reported on 13 November 2025 that it had detected, in mid-September, a group it assessed with high confidence to be Chinese state-sponsored using its agentic coding tool to run intrusion operations against roughly thirty organizations, with a small number of successful compromises. After a ten-day investigation it banned the accounts, notified affected parties, and published the report. Anthropic's estimate — and it is the vendor's estimate, from its own telemetry, with no external corroboration — is that the model performed 80–90% of the campaign, with humans intervening at perhaps four to six decision points per operation.

**Entry.** Into the AI system, not into the victims: the operators decomposed the intrusion into small tasks stripped of context, each innocuous in isolation, and told the model it was an employee of a legitimate cybersecurity firm doing authorized defensive testing. Guardrails that evaluate a single request cannot see a campaign assembled from individually reasonable steps. Against the victims themselves, the model performed reconnaissance, identified high-value databases, and researched and wrote exploit code.

**Escalation.** The agent harvested credentials, tested them for privilege, moved between systems, extracted and categorized stolen data by intelligence value, created backdoors for re-entry, and wrote documentation of the operation for reuse. The bottleneck that normally limits an espionage crew — skilled operator hours — was substantially removed.

**Blast radius.** Around thirty targets across technology companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers, and government agencies; Anthropic said a small subset were successfully infiltrated and did not name them. A useful limit was also reported: the model hallucinated credentials and claimed to have extracted secrets that turned out to be public, so the output required human verification and the campaign was not as clean as the automation figure suggests.

**Human factor.** On the defender's side, the decision that matters is speed. Detection tuned to human-paced intrusions — an operator working a shift, pausing, coming back — has no answer for reconnaissance and exfiltration that run continuously at machine speed against thirty targets at once. On the provider's side, the failure was architectural: safety review scoped to one request at a time.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-06 and AGENT-07 (human approval before an agent acts against production, and logging of agent actions with the originating prompt, so an operator's intent is reconstructable), OBSV-02 (per-account volume alerting, the control that fires when a machine enumerates a database faster than any human analyst would), and AGENT-NEW — a control this checklist does not yet carry: an agent platform must evaluate the *sequence* of requests from an account, not each request in isolation, because task decomposition is now the standard jailbreak.

**Escalation.** The ticket system was the escalation. Support tickets and their attachments held API keys, cloud access tokens, SSH private keys, VPN configuration files, SQL backups, internal scripts and source code, network inventories, and identity-document scans — material that turns a read of a helpdesk database into a credential set for the infrastructure the helpdesk supports. The passwords themselves were bcrypt-hashed; the keys pasted into tickets were not.

**Blast radius.** Eurofiber France confirmed customer data was exfiltrated and said the incident was limited to its French customers and regional brands, with Belgian, German, and Dutch entities unaffected. The verified leak is about 10,000 unique email addresses with a smaller number of names and phone numbers; the attacker claims a much larger and more sensitive set including screenshots, credentials, certificates, and backups, which has not been independently confirmed.

**Human factor.** Engineers paste what they need into tickets to solve the customer's problem today, and nobody sweeps them afterwards. A ticket system accumulates, over years, an unmanaged copy of the organization's secrets — held in a third-party application that is patched on the application owner's schedule rather than the security team's.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-01 (parameterized queries in the ticketing application — this is a 1998 vulnerability class holding a telecom's credentials in 2025), VENDOR-04 (support artifacts sanitized: keys, configs, and ID scans stripped or referenced rather than attached), CRED-03 and CRED-09 (a rotation runbook with a stated maximum time-to-revoke, because every secret ever pasted into that platform had to be treated as public on 13 November).

The failure is not that a vendor was breached — it is that the same class of blast radius was available, unchanged, twelve weeks later.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding a token to your systems, which is the precondition for reacting in hours), VENDOR-02 (granted OAuth tokens scoped, expiring, and reviewed; unused integrations revoked), VENDOR-03 (authorizing a new connected application is a reviewed decision, not a checkbox a team lead clicks), DATA-11 (bulk export through an integration's API alerts, whoever is holding the token).


**What happened.** A second, substantially evolved wave of the self-replicating npm worm Shai-Hulud was identified on 24 November 2025, beginning with a compromise in the AsyncAPI repository and peaking in the early hours of that morning UTC before npm applied containment measures around 18:00 UTC. Datadog Security Labs counted 796 unique npm packages across 1,092 versions, together accounting for more than 20 million weekly downloads. Cleanup of republished versions and rotated credentials continued into December.

**Entry.** A developer or CI job installing a compromised version ran a `preinstall` hook, which dropped `setup_bun.js` and `bun_environment.js` and installed the Bun JavaScript runtime to execute an obfuscated payload — a deliberate choice to run outside the Node.js process monitoring most teams had in place. No exploit, no phishing: the standard, expected behaviour of a package manager was the execution vector.

**Escalation.** The worm harvested credentials from the local filesystem (GitHub and npm tokens), temporary cloud credentials for AWS, GCP, and Azure, and secrets from AWS Secrets Manager, Azure Key Vault, and Google Cloud Secret Manager. It exfiltrated them into public GitHub repositories bearing the description "Sha1-Hulud: The Second Coming," then used stolen npm credentials to republish up to 100 backdoored versions of that maintainer's packages — the credential theft and the propagation were the same action. If the victim's own GitHub credentials were missing, it reused credentials harvested from other compromised accounts to keep spreading.

**Blast radius.** More than 500 GitHub users across more than 150 organizations had credentials exfiltrated into public repositories; affected maintainers included Zapier, ENS Domains, PostHog, Postman, and AsyncAPI. The new behaviour that distinguishes this wave from the first: when the worm could neither exfiltrate nor self-replicate, it attempted to destroy every file in the user's home directory. A supply-chain worm that had been a credential-theft problem became a data-destruction problem on developer and CI machines that happened to be well defended at the network layer.

**Human factor.** The ecosystem's defaults did this. Install scripts run by default, installs are not pinned, newly published versions are adopted within minutes, and maintainer tokens do not expire — so one compromised maintainer account converts into 796 packages inside a few hours. Every one of those defaults was a decision someone could have changed years ago at the cost of minor inconvenience.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-02 (install scripts disabled by default on developer machines and in CI — this single setting breaks the entire chain), DEPS-01 and DEPS-03 (frozen, lockfile-pinned installs and a cooldown before adopting a newly published version, so a four-hour window of malicious versions is never installed), CRED-07 (expiring registry and cloud tokens, which limits what a harvest is worth), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed response for "a package we depend on was compromised," rehearsed specifically for the case where the answer is rotate everything within the hour).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **CISA Emergency Directive ED 26-01 (15 October 2025)** required federal agencies to inventory every F5 BIG-IP instance, determine whether management interfaces were internet-reachable, patch by 22 October, disconnect public-facing end-of-support devices, report by 29 October, complete remaining updates by 31 October, and submit a full inventory by 3 December — issued on the strength of stolen vulnerability research rather than any observed exploitation, which is a notable shift in what triggers an emergency directive.
- **Oracle shipped an out-of-band emergency patch for CVE-2025-61882 with published indicators of compromise**, breaking its own quarterly cycle; the accompanying IoCs and reverse-shell command signatures let customers hunt retroactively for the two months of exploitation that preceded the fix.
- **The Eclipse Foundation hardened Open VSX** after the GlassWorm and leaked-token episodes: a distinguishable `ovsxat_` token prefix agreed with Microsoft's Security Response Center so scanners can spot a leaked publisher token, reduced default token lifetimes, removal of the flagged extensions, and automated scanning for malicious code patterns. The token prefix is the transferable idea — make your credentials recognizable so that someone else's secret scanner can find them for you.
- **npm applied registry-level containment of Shai-Hulud 2.0 within roughly a day** of the 24 November peak, and Datadog Security Labs published package-level indicators and the "Sha1-Hulud: The Second Coming" repository-description signature, which let organizations search GitHub for their own exfiltrated credentials rather than waiting to be told.
- **Salesforce demonstrated a third-party OAuth kill switch**, revoking all access and refresh tokens for the Gainsight applications and pulling them from AppExchange within a day; Zendesk, Gong, and HubSpot suspended their own Gainsight integrations in parallel. The capability worth copying is the ability to revoke one vendor's grants across every tenant at once — and to know, beforehand, which tenants have them.
- **Checkout.com published its refusal to pay** alongside the technical facts, naming the legacy system it had failed to decommission and donating the demanded sum to Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Oxford's cyber security centre. As a disclosure template it is unusually complete: what was taken, what was not, which customers, and the decision with an executive's name on it.
- **Anthropic published its own incident report**, expanded classifiers for malicious agentic activity, banned the identified accounts, notified affected entities, and coordinated with authorities — the first vendor post-mortem of its own product being used to run an intrusion campaign, and the source of the 80–90% automation estimate that framed the rest of the quarter's AI-security debate.
