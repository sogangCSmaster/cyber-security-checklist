# 2025 Q4 — Trusted Toolchains Turned Against Developers

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-dependency — the defining chains of the quarter ran through developer infrastructure: npm packages, Open VSX and VS Code extensions, and a SaaS vendor's OAuth tokens
> - **New technique this quarter:** payloads hidden in Unicode variation selectors that render as nothing in an editor (GlassWorm); an npm worm that destroys the victim's home directory when it cannot exfiltrate (Shai-Hulud 2.0); and Anthropic's report of an intrusion campaign in which an agentic coding tool performed an estimated 80–90% of the operational work
> - **Recurring failure:** six of the fourteen records here never had an entry vector disclosed at all, including two whose victims were regulated financial institutions' data processors
> - **Records in this file:** 28

## Incidents

```yaml
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/cl0p-linked-hackers-breach-dozens-of.html
  - https://www.securityweek.com/nearly-30-alleged-victims-of-oracle-ebs-hack-named-on-cl0p-ransomware-site/
  - https://www.theregister.com/2025/11/25/clop_dartmouth_college/
```

**What happened.** The Cl0p extortion crew exploited an unauthenticated remote-code-execution chain in Oracle E-Business Suite, later assigned CVE-2025-61882 (CVSS 9.8), to steal data in bulk from EBS customers before any patch existed. Google Threat Intelligence dated the mass exploitation to around 9 August 2025, with earlier suspicious activity from 10 July; the extortion emails went out from compromised third-party accounts on 29 September, and Oracle shipped an out-of-band emergency patch with indicators of compromise in early October. Victim disclosures then ran through the rest of the quarter.

**Entry.** The chain targeted the Concurrent Processing component of the EBS web tier and required no username or password. Researchers described it as a chain of server-side request forgery, CRLF injection, an authentication bypass, and XSL template injection, ending in remote code execution and a reverse shell on the application host. Some steps touched issues Oracle had fixed in its July 2025 critical patch update; the final link was not patched until October.

**Escalation.** None required. E-Business Suite *is* the finance, HR, and procurement system of record, so code execution in the application tier put the attacker directly on top of the data worth extorting. Cl0p did not need domain admin or lateral movement to complete its objective.

**Blast radius.** Google put the victim count at "dozens" of organizations; Cl0p subsequently named roughly 29 on its leak site, which is a claim rather than a confirmation, and Cl0p historically lists victims weeks after the theft. Organizations that themselves confirmed compromise include Harvard University, The Washington Post, Logitech, Envoy Air (an American Airlines subsidiary), and Dartmouth College. Logitech confirmed in a securities filing that an attacker exploited CVE-2025-61882 to copy internal data; Envoy Air said only limited business and contact information was taken and no customer data.

**Human factor.** Enterprise ERP is patched on a quarterly ceremony, sits behind change-control boards, and is widely treated as too load-bearing to touch quickly — so a class of system that holds an organization's most sensitive records is routinely months behind on fixes and, in many of these deployments, was directly reachable from the internet. The two-month window between first exploitation and the emergency patch was survivable only for organizations that had already removed the EBS web tier from public exposure.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (an internet-facing EBS tier should be a deliberate, enumerated decision, not an accident of the original 2000s deployment), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems plus an inventory of what cannot be patched, so an out-of-band Oracle alert is actionable in hours), DATA-11 and OBSV-02 (bulk export from an ERP is a privileged, alerting event; the quiet removal of gigabytes over weeks is exactly what volume alerting exists to catch).

```yaml
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/ed-26-01-mitigate-vulnerabilities-f5-devices
```

**What happened.** F5 disclosed in an SEC filing on 15 October 2025 that a nation-state actor had long-term access to its BIG-IP product development environment and its engineering knowledge-management platform, and had exfiltrated portions of BIG-IP source code together with information about vulnerabilities F5 was still working to fix. F5 learned of the intrusion on 9 August 2025 but delayed disclosure at the request of the U.S. Department of Justice. CISA issued Emergency Directive ED 26-01 the same day the disclosure landed.

**Entry.** F5 has not published how the actor first got in. Bloomberg reported the attackers had been inside for at least twelve months before detection, and subsequently identified them as the China-nexus espionage group UNC5221, associated with the BRICKSTORM backdoor — attribution that comes from press reporting, not from F5's own filing.

**Escalation.** F5 named two distinct environments the actor reached: the BIG-IP build and development environment, and the internal knowledge base holding configuration and implementation details for a small percentage of customers. Reaching both implies movement between engineering systems; F5 did not describe the path.

**Blast radius.** The direct loss was intellectual property: source code and a working list of not-yet-fixed flaws, which CISA warned "could enable the threat actor to identify logical flaws and zero-day vulnerabilities" and build exploits for devices that sit in front of government and enterprise networks. The secondary blast radius fell on customers. ED 26-01 required federal agencies to inventory F5 BIG-IP hardware and software, check whether management interfaces were publicly reachable, apply updates by 22 October, disconnect public-facing end-of-support devices, report by 29 October, patch remaining devices by 31 October, and submit a full inventory by 3 December. F5 said it had seen no indication the stolen vulnerability information had been exploited.

**Human factor.** A vendor's own pre-release vulnerability tracker is, by construction, a list of working exploits against its entire installed base — yet it lived in an engineering knowledge system that went a year without anyone noticing a resident intruder. The decision that matters here is an organizational one: the build environment for a security appliance was not monitored to the standard of the production estate it protects.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-08 (treat the build and product-development environment as production and monitor it as such), OBSV-03 and OBSV-08 (detection that is itself monitored, and log retention that exceeds a realistic dwell time — a year of access is not reconstructable from 90 days of logs), VENDOR-06 (on the customer side, monitoring vendor advisories and having a path to act on an emergency directive within a week).

**What happened.** The University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg confirmed in mid-October 2025 that its Oracle E-Business Suite installation was among those compromised in the global campaign recorded at I2025Q4-03. Wits said it had notified South Africa's Information Regulator under the Protection of Personal Information Act and was assessing what data relating to staff, students and suppliers had been reached. It is one of the few named victims in the campaign outside North America and Western Europe, and one of the first to say publicly which regulator it had told.

**Entry.** The same pre-authentication remote-code-execution chain in Oracle E-Business Suite that Cl0p had been using since early August — no credential, no phish, no insider. The relevant EBS components were reachable from the public internet, which is the condition that turned a vendor's zero-day into this university's incident.

**Escalation.** None required. E-Business Suite is the finance, HR and procurement system; whoever reaches it has already reached the payroll, vendor-master and student-billing records. There was no second step to take.

**Blast radius.** Wits did not publish a record count. The campaign's operators listed the university on their leak site alongside roughly two dozen other organizations and applied the usual pressure sequence of executive extortion mail followed by a countdown page. What is documented is the regulatory notification and a months-long assessment of an ERP system that touches every employee and every registered student.

**Human factor.** A university runs its ERP the way most large institutions do: internet-reachable because staff, contractors and campuses need it, patched on the vendor's quarterly cadence because that is what the maintenance contract describes. Neither decision is careless in isolation. Together they mean that the interval between a vendor's zero-day and its out-of-band fix is an interval in which the institution has no defence but detection — and the exploitation here ran for roughly two months before anyone noticed.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-08 (the EBS administrative and servlet surfaces do not belong on the public internet; a VPN or identity-aware proxy in front of them removes the entire unauthenticated path), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA that can absorb an out-of-band emergency release rather than waiting for the next quarterly window), DATA-11 and OBSV-02 (bulk export from an ERP is a privileged, logged, alerting action — two months of extraction from a finance system should not be invisible), OBSV-05 (the written plan that produced a prompt regulator notification is the part of this response that worked).

**What happened.** Askul Corporation, a large Japanese office-supplies and consumer e-commerce operator that also runs order fulfilment for other retailers, detected a ransomware infection on the evening of Sunday 19 October 2025 and suspended ordering and shipping the following day. Its own sites — Askul, Lohaco and Soloel Arena — stopped taking orders, and so did the online stores of Muji, Loft and Sogo & Seibu, which depend on Askul for logistics. No group publicly claimed the attack in the days that followed, and Askul said it was investigating whether personal and customer data had been taken.

Muji suspended domestic online shopping and app functions with, in its own words, no determined resumption date; Loft halted online orders and shipments; Sogo & Seibu reported disruption to Askul-dependent logistics. The retailers were not themselves breached — they simply had no second way to get a parcel to a customer.

**Human factor.** Three retail chains had outsourced fulfilment to one operator and had no fallback for the case where that operator's systems stopped. Concentration was never a decision anyone made explicitly; it accumulated one efficiency at a time. The lesson is not that outsourcing is wrong but that a dependency capable of stopping your revenue is a dependency you should have rehearsed losing.

**What would have stopped it.** For Askul: DATA-08 (backups that exist, are restore-tested, and cannot be written by production credentials, so recovery is measured in hours), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between order intake, warehouse control and corporate IT so one infection cannot take all three). For Muji, Loft and Sogo & Seibu: VENDOR-01 and VENDOR-06 (know which vendors can halt your revenue, hold a contractual notification window, and hold a documented manual or secondary fulfilment path). OBSV-05 applies to everyone in the chain — the customer-facing statements in the first 48 hours were noticeably better than the internal clarity behind them.

```yaml
controls: [AGENT-09, AGENT-04, DEPS-03, CRED-03]
sources:
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/self-spreading-glassworm-infects-vs.html
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/11/glassworm-malware-discovered-in-three.html
```

**What happened.** Koi Security documented a campaign it named GlassWorm in which fourteen editor extensions — thirteen on the Open VSX Registry, one on Microsoft's VS Code Marketplace — were published with malicious code that does not render in a code editor. The first infection wave dated to 17 October 2025. A second batch of three infected extensions with a few thousand installs each was reported on 10 November, showing the campaign was not finished when the first report landed.

**Entry.** The payload was written using Unicode variation selectors, characters that are valid Unicode but produce no visual output. As researcher Idan Dardikman put it, the malicious code "literally disappears" in the editor: a reviewer scrolling the extension's source sees whitespace where the payload is. How the publishing accounts themselves were hijacked was never established. Because VS Code extensions auto-update by default, the malicious version reached installed machines without any user action.

**Escalation.** The payload harvested npm, Open VSX, GitHub, and Git credentials from the developer's machine and used them to publish further infected extensions and packages — the credential theft *was* the propagation mechanism. It also targeted 49 cryptocurrency wallet extensions for direct theft, installed a SOCKS proxy to relay traffic through the victim's machine, and deployed hidden VNC for interactive access. Command and control ran over Solana blockchain transaction memo fields, with Google Calendar events holding base64-encoded fallback addresses — infrastructure that cannot be taken down by seizing a domain.

**Blast radius.** The registries recorded roughly 35,800 downloads across the affected extensions; the Eclipse Foundation later argued that figure overstates real users because much of that traffic was bot-generated, which is why this record carries the extension count rather than a user count. Confirmed losses are developer credentials and crypto wallet funds; no downstream customer breach was tied to GlassWorm within the quarter.

**Human factor.** Every control in the extension ecosystem assumes a human or a scanner can read the code. A developer who does the responsible thing — opening the extension source before installing — sees nothing, and the auto-update default means most never get that choice at all.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-09 (scan for invisible and homoglyph characters in anything that will execute — this is the control this incident exists to justify, and it costs one lint rule), AGENT-04 (pin and version-diff IDE extensions rather than letting them auto-update), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before adopting a newly published version), CRED-03 (rotate npm, registry, and Git tokens on any suspicion that a developer machine ran untrusted code).


**What happened.** On 25 October 2025 the Everest extortion group listed Svenska kraftnät, the state-owned operator of Sweden's electricity transmission network, on its leak site and threatened to publish roughly 280 gigabytes of data. Svenska kraftnät confirmed a breach the next day, saying it was limited to "a limited external file transfer solution" and that electricity supply and the operator's mission-critical systems were unaffected. It reported the incident to Swedish police and to the national cyber security authorities.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. The operator named the affected system — an externally facing file-transfer service — but not how it was reached, and no vendor advisory has been tied to it publicly. Recorded as unknown.

**Escalation.** None reached. The operator's account is that the compromise stayed inside the file-transfer environment and did not extend into the systems that actually run the grid. If that holds, the segmentation between an internet-facing document exchange and the control estate did the job it exists to do — the opposite finding to most records in this corpus, and worth recording as such.

**Blast radius.** The 280 GB figure is the attacker's claim and has not been confirmed; the operator said only that it was investigating the scope and nature of the data. The material at risk is whatever an external file-transfer service accumulates when nobody prunes it: contractor correspondence, drawings, procurement documents, and personal data of counterparties. Chief information security officer Cem Göcgören said the organization took the breach "very seriously and have taken immediate action", and there has been no reported effect on power supply.

**Human factor.** External file-transfer systems are where organizations put things temporarily and then leave them. They sit outside the perimeter by design, they are shared with parties outside the organization by design, and no one owns deleting what is on them. A grid operator with excellent control-system segregation can still lose a decade of documents from the one box everybody is allowed to upload to.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 and CLOUD-05 (the file-transfer service was correctly kept off the operational network — the control that limited this incident, and the one to verify rather than assume), DATA-09 (enforced retention on the exchange platform: a file that has been collected should not still be there a year later), DATA-11 and OBSV-02-style volume alerting (280 GB leaving a document-exchange service is a detectable event), OBSV-05 (confirming within a day of the leak-site post, and saying plainly what was and was not affected, is the disclosure behaviour worth copying).

```yaml
  - https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/canadian-warn-hacktivists-exposed-ics-devices/804244/
```

**What happened.** On 30 October 2025 the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security and the RCMP issued alert AL25-016 describing several recent incidents in which hacktivists reached internet-accessible industrial control systems in Canada and changed their settings. At a municipal water facility they altered pressure values, degrading service to the community. At an oil and gas company they manipulated an automated tank gauge, triggering false alarms. On a farm they changed the temperature and humidity setpoints of a grain-drying silo, creating unsafe storage conditions. The Cyber Centre's framing is the instructive part: the victims were not targets but "victims of opportunity", and the intrusions required neither custom malware nor insider access.

**Entry.** Control devices — PLCs, remote terminal units, human-machine interfaces, SCADA front-ends, safety controllers, building-management systems and industrial IoT gear — that answered directly from the public internet. Many such devices authenticate weakly or not at all because they were designed for a serial cable on a plant floor, and were later given an IP address and a modem for remote support.

**Escalation.** None required. The exposed interface was the control interface. There was no privilege to escalate because the device's operating interface grants setpoint changes to whoever can reach it.

**Blast radius.** Three confirmed operational effects across three sectors: water service to a community, false alarms consuming an energy operator's response capacity, and grain at risk of spoilage or combustion. No data was the objective. The Cyber Centre noted that the exposure concentrates in local utilities, agriculture and smaller manufacturers, where the operational technology is real but the security budget is not.

**Human factor.** Someone connected a controller to the internet to avoid a two-hour drive for a setpoint change, and the arrangement outlived the person, the project and the memory of it. Nobody has an inventory of which devices are reachable, because the devices were installed by operations rather than IT and were never added to any asset list.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 and CLOUD-09 (enumerate what is actually reachable from the internet, including equipment IT never commissioned — this is the single highest-yield exercise for a small utility or farm), CLOUD-08 (remote access to control equipment through a VPN with multi-factor authentication rather than a directly exposed interface, which is precisely what the alert recommends), AUTH-11 (every state-changing interface authenticates its caller, including non-HTTP industrial protocols that assume the network is trusted), OBSV-01 (setpoint changes are logged and someone reads them — the grain-silo case was discovered by its physical consequences).

A benign agent later reads that record as part of its work, treats the text as instruction, and then uses the platform's agent-to-agent discovery feature to recruit other agents on its team, including agents running with more privilege than the user who planted the text. ServiceNow's own prompt-injection protection was enabled throughout.

**Entry.** Indirect prompt injection through data the agent was expected to read. Nothing was exploited in the conventional sense; the injected text arrived through a normal business workflow and was interpreted as a command because a language model cannot distinguish the two.

**Escalation.** Product features did the escalation. Three Now Assist defaults combine: the default language model supports agent discovery; agents published to the same virtual agent are automatically grouped into one team; and agents are marked discoverable when published. The result is that any agent can enlist any teammate, and the teammate executes under its own privileges rather than the requester's. AppOmni demonstrated record creation, reading and modification, copying sensitive data into other tickets, sending email containing confidential information, and privilege escalation.

**Blast radius.** None confirmed in the wild. This is a research finding with no reported exploitation. ServiceNow, after responsible disclosure, said the behaviours were intended and subsequently updated its documentation to describe the configuration risk more clearly — which makes every customer running the defaults the party that has to act.

**Human factor.** The defaults were chosen to make multi-agent workflows work on first use, and they do. The unstated assumption is that anything an agent reads was written by someone trusted, which is false for every ticketing system ever built — the entire purpose of a ticket queue is to accept text from people you do not control. A vendor saying "working as intended" is not the end of the question; it transfers the decision to the customer, who has to decide whether the intent matches their threat model.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-01 (everything an agent reads is data, never instruction — ticket bodies above all), AGENT-02 (agents hold scoped credentials so a recruited agent cannot exceed the requesting user's authority), AGENT-03 (untrusted content, privileged data and an outbound channel such as email must not combine unsupervised), AGENT-06 (human approval before an agent changes records, access or sends external mail), AGENT-07 (log every agent action with its originating prompt, so a chain of agents remains attributable to the text that started it). Operationally: turn off automatic team grouping and discoverability, and publish agents as discoverable only where that is deliberate.

**What happened.** A second, substantially evolved wave of the self-replicating npm worm Shai-Hulud was identified on 24 November 2025, beginning with a compromise in the AsyncAPI repository and peaking in the early hours of that morning UTC before npm applied containment measures around 18:00 UTC. The company retained Mandiant, Palo Alto Networks, and Ernst & Young for forensics and announced a compensation programme of 1.685 trillion won (about $1.17 billion) in vouchers to all 33.7 million affected customers from 15 January 2026. That figure is an announced remediation spend, not an audited loss. Coupang's Korea CEO resigned in the aftermath.

**Human factor.** Two failures compounded. A credential issued to an employee outlived the employment by months, because revocation was a manual step in a process nobody owned — the ordinary joiner-mover-leaver gap, at the scale of a company serving most of a country. Then the first public number was wrong by four orders of magnitude, which cost the company more trust in a week than the access itself did in five months.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-03 (a tracked leaver process with a maximum revocation time that covers machine credentials, not just the SSO account), CRED-07 and CRED-09 (internal keys that expire on their own, plus a rotation and revocation runbook with a named owner — a key issued to a person should not outlive that person's badge), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal read volume per credential: 33 million account reads from overseas infrastructure over five months is the single most detectable pattern in this file).

**What happened.** Korean Air disclosed on 29 December 2025 that personal data on roughly 30,000 of its employees had been stolen — not from the airline, but from Korean Air Catering & Duty-Free, the in-flight catering and duty-free business spun off from the airline in 2020 and still holding employee records. Reporting ties the intrusion to the Oracle E-Business Suite zero-day chain (CVE-2025-61882) against KC&D's ERP server in November 2025, with Cl0p claiming the theft and publishing data. The airline said no customer data was involved and that it had notified the authorities; vice chairman Woo Kee-hong said the company was working to establish the full scope and who was affected.

**Entry.** The same Oracle E-Business Suite remote-code-execution chain used across the campaign, at the supplier rather than at the airline. By November the fix had been available since Oracle's 4 October out-of-band release, which makes this an unpatched known vulnerability rather than a zero-day from the victim's point of view.

**Escalation.** None required. The supplier's ERP held the employee records directly.

**Blast radius.** Names and account numbers for approximately 30,000 Korean Air employees, published on the extortion group's leak site. No customer data was reported as affected. The airline's exposure came entirely through a corporate separation that moved the systems but not the data — five years after the spin-off, the subsidiary still held the parent's workforce records.

**Human factor.** When a business unit is carved out, the data goes with it and the security expectations usually do not. The parent keeps a vendor relationship where it once had a department, and the assurance that used to be internal policy becomes a contract clause nobody tests. Meanwhile the ERP that the subsidiary inherited is patched on the subsidiary's budget, which is smaller than the airline's.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA at the supplier that treats an out-of-band release for an internet-facing ERP as a days-not-weeks obligation — the fix existed for roughly a month before this intrusion), CLOUD-08 (EBS not reachable from the public internet), VENDOR-01 and VENDOR-06 (an inventory of who holds your employee data after a divestment, with contractual notification windows and evidence of patching, not assurances), DATA-09 (the parent's employee records should have been minimized or repatriated at the point of separation rather than left in the spun-off entity for five years).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **CISA Emergency Directive ED 26-01 (15 October 2025)** required federal agencies to inventory every F5 BIG-IP instance, determine whether management interfaces were internet-reachable, patch by 22 October, disconnect public-facing end-of-support devices, report by 29 October, complete remaining updates by 31 October, and submit a full inventory by 3 December — issued on the strength of stolen vulnerability research rather than any observed exploitation, which is a notable shift in what triggers an emergency directive.
- **Oracle shipped an out-of-band emergency patch for CVE-2025-61882 with published indicators of compromise**, breaking its own quarterly cycle; the accompanying IoCs and reverse-shell command signatures let customers hunt retroactively for the two months of exploitation that preceded the fix.
- **The Eclipse Foundation hardened Open VSX** after the GlassWorm and leaked-token episodes: a distinguishable `ovsxat_` token prefix agreed with Microsoft's Security Response Center so scanners can spot a leaked publisher token, reduced default token lifetimes, removal of the flagged extensions, and automated scanning for malicious code patterns. The token prefix is the transferable idea — make your credentials recognizable so that someone else's secret scanner can find them for you.
- **npm applied registry-level containment of Shai-Hulud 2.0 within roughly a day** of the 24 November peak, and Datadog Security Labs published package-level indicators and the "Sha1-Hulud: The Second Coming" repository-description signature, which let organizations search GitHub for their own exfiltrated credentials rather than waiting to be told.
- **Salesforce demonstrated a third-party OAuth kill switch**, revoking all access and refresh tokens for the Gainsight applications and pulling them from AppExchange within a day; Zendesk, Gong, and HubSpot suspended their own Gainsight integrations in parallel. The capability worth copying is the ability to revoke one vendor's grants across every tenant at once — and to know, beforehand, which tenants have them.
- **Checkout.com published its refusal to pay** alongside the technical facts, naming the legacy system it had failed to decommission and donating the demanded sum to Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Oxford's cyber security centre. As a disclosure template it is unusually complete: what was taken, what was not, which customers, and the decision with an executive's name on it.
- **Anthropic published its own incident report**, expanded classifiers for malicious agentic activity, banned the identified accounts, notified affected entities, and coordinated with authorities — the first vendor post-mortem of its own product being used to run an intrusion campaign, and the source of the 80–90% automation estimate that framed the rest of the quarter's AI-security debate.
