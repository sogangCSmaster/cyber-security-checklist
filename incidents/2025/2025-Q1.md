# 2025 Q1 — The Toolchain Becomes The Target

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — four records began with a valid credential in someone else's hands (a support portal login, infostealer-harvested Jira accounts, a maintainer's GitHub token, a customer's Cerner credential), not with an exploit
> - **New technique this quarter:** tampering with the *signing interface* rather than the system being signed for — malicious JavaScript in Safe{Wallet}'s web app showed Bybit's signers a normal transfer while they approved a different one; in the same quarter, poisoned AI-assistant "rules files" used invisible Unicode to make a code assistant emit backdoors that a human reviewer could not see
> - **Recurring failure:** the tooling around production — support portals, CI runners, IDE assistants, legacy migration servers — held far more reach than the accounts using it, and almost nothing logged or capped that reach
> - **Records in this file:** 11

## Incidents

dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [AUTH-01, AUTH-07, DATA-11, OBSV-02]
sources:
  - https://www.powerschool.com/security/sis-incident/
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/powerschool-hacker-claims-they-stole-data-of-62-million-students/
```

**What happened.** PowerSchool, the K-12 student information system used by thousands of school districts in the US, Canada and elsewhere, became aware on 28 December 2024 of unauthorized exfiltration of personal information from customer SIS environments, and notified customers on 7 January 2025. The company paid a ransom in the days after discovery, on the strength of a video in which the threat actor claimed to have deleted the stolen data. PowerSchool never published a count of affected individuals; the figures in circulation come from the extortion demand itself.

**Entry.** A threat actor used stolen credentials to log into PowerSource, PowerSchool's community-facing customer support portal. No vulnerability was exploited and no district was breached directly — the credential simply worked, and the path it opened was not protected by a second factor.

**Escalation.** None of the usual kind was needed. From inside PowerSource the actor used a customer-support maintenance access tool that could reach districts' PowerSIS databases and pull student and teacher records out of them — support tooling built to let a handful of staff help any customer, reachable by anyone holding one portal login.

**Blast radius.** Data types varied by district because districts choose what the SIS stores: names, contact details and demographic fields for most, with Social Security numbers, medical alerts and grades for a subset. PowerSchool said it expected more than three quarters of involved individuals not to have had an SSN exfiltrated, and offered two years of identity protection and credit monitoring to all involved students and educators regardless of entitlement. The attacker's extortion demand claimed 62,488,628 students and 9,506,624 teachers across 6,505 districts — a claim, not a confirmed figure, which is why this record's scale is recorded as the attacker's number and its confidence is medium. In May 2025 individual districts were separately extorted with the same data, confirming the paid ransom bought nothing.

**Human factor.** Two decisions compounded: leaving a customer-facing support portal without mandatory MFA while it fronted a tool that could export any customer's database, and then paying an extortionist for a promise of deletion that was worth exactly as much as such promises usually are.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on any portal with production reach, including the "just support" one), AUTH-07 (support and impersonation tooling behind separate authentication and full audit), DATA-11 (bulk export of a customer database as a privileged, logged, alerting action), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal export volume per account, which is the only thing that catches a legitimate credential doing an illegitimate thing).

impact: [impact/pii, impact/intellectual-property]

tech: [tech/saas]

dwell_days: 1
confidence: medium
controls: [AUTH-01, CRED-03, OBSV-02]
sources:
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/telefonica-confirms-internal-ticketing-system-breach-after-data-leak/
  - https://www.securityweek.com/infostealer-infections-lead-to-telefonica-internal-ticketing-system-breach/
```

**What happened.** Telefónica confirmed on 10 January 2025 that an internal ticketing system had been accessed without authorization, a day after a dump of its internal Jira data appeared on a hacking forum. Four people using the handles DNA, Grep, Pryx and Rey claimed the intrusion; three of them belong to the HellCat extortion crew, which had used the same pattern against Schneider Electric's Jira server weeks earlier. The company learned of the breach from the leak, not from its own monitoring.

**Entry.** Commodity infostealer malware on employee machines harvested Jira credentials; Hudson Rock's analysis counted more than fifteen infected Telefónica employees. The credentials were simply replayed against the internal Jira development and ticketing server: no exploit, and the harvested logins alone were sufficient. Telefonica did not say whether any second factor was available on that server, which is the basis for the missing-MFA tag here.

**Escalation.** Once inside Jira, the attackers targeted two employees with administrative privileges and socially engineered them into naming the correct server, which the attackers then brute-forced over SSH — a foothold in a ticketing system converted into host access through a conversation rather than a vulnerability.

**Blast radius.** The attackers claim roughly 2.3 GB of documents and tickets: 236,493 lines of customer data, 469,724 lines of internal ticketing data and over 5,000 internal documents. Telefónica confirmed the unauthorized access and reset passwords on affected accounts the following day but did not confirm any of those counts, which is why they are attributed here and the record carries medium confidence. Tickets labelled as customer data had been opened from @telefonica.com addresses, so some of it may be staff acting for customers rather than customer records directly.

**Human factor.** Internal ticketing systems accumulate everything — customer details, credentials pasted into tickets, architecture notes — while being treated as an internal convenience rather than a crown-jewel system, so they end up single-factor and unmonitored. The follow-on step is the more uncomfortable one: two administrators helped the intruders by answering a plausible question.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on internal collaboration systems, which makes an infostealer's password dump worthless on its own), CRED-03 (treat every credential seen by an infostealer-infected endpoint as burned and rotate it), OBSV-02 (alert on one account scraping hundreds of thousands of ticket rows).

### I2025Q1-03 · DeepSeek exposed ClickHouse database

```yaml

entry: [entry/public-database]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/messages, impact/credentials, impact/none-confirmed]
factor: [factor/misconfiguration, factor/human-error]
tech: [tech/llm, tech/api]
scale: "over 1000000 log entries"
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: high
controls: [DATA-04, CLOUD-01, AUTH-03, OBSV-04]
sources:
  - https://www.wiz.io/blog/wiz-research-uncovers-exposed-deepseek-database-leak
```

**What happened.** Wiz Research, mapping DeepSeek's external attack surface as the Chinese AI company's assistant topped app-store charts, found two unusual open ports within minutes of starting. Behind them sat a ClickHouse database, publicly reachable and requiring no authentication at all. Wiz disclosed it to DeepSeek, which secured the exposure promptly, and published on 29 January 2025.

**Entry.** ClickHouse's HTTP interface was listening on oauth2callback.deepseek.com and dev.deepseek.com on ports 8123 and 9000, open to the internet with authentication disabled. Its /play path accepted arbitrary SQL, so anyone who found the host had full read access to the database and, depending on server settings, a path toward reading local files.

**Escalation.** None required — the entry point was the data. A `log_stream` table held over a million rows dating from 6 January 2025 containing plaintext chat history, API secrets, backend operational details and service metadata.

**Blast radius.** No malicious access was confirmed; the exposure was found by researchers and closed after responsible disclosure, so the recorded impact is the exposure itself rather than a confirmed loss. What was exposed matters more than the count: user conversations with an AI assistant are among the most intimate data a consumer service holds, and the API secrets alongside them were operational keys, not test values.

**Human factor.** A company scaling from obscurity to global traffic in weeks shipped infrastructure faster than it enumerated what that infrastructure exposed. This is the AI-era version of the open S3 bucket — no attacker, no exploit, no sophistication, just a default that was never closed. It is adjacent to `factor/ai-generated-default` without being it: nothing indicates an AI tool chose the setting, so the tag is deliberately not applied.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and search indexes never reachable from the public internet), CLOUD-01 (regularly enumerate what is publicly reachable and require a decision for each item), AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data), OBSV-04 (chat content and API secrets should never have been sitting in a log stream in the first place).

### I2025Q1-04 · nullifAI malicious models on Hugging Face

```yaml
id: I2025Q1-04
name: nullifAI malicious models on Hugging Face
org: Hugging Face (platform); glockr1/ballr7 and who-r-u0000 model repositories
date_occurred: 2025-01
date_disclosed: 2025-02
region: global
sector: [sector/tech]
actor: [actor/unknown, actor/researcher]
entry: [entry/model-artifact]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/none-confirmed]
factor: [factor/third-party-trust, factor/supply-chain-blindness]
tech: [tech/ml-model]
scale: "2 models"
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [INPUT-06, DEPS-07, DEPS-04, AGENT-NEW]
sources:
  - https://www.reversinglabs.com/blog/rl-identifies-malware-ml-model-hosted-on-hugging-face
```

**What happened.** ReversingLabs researchers found two PyTorch models on Hugging Face — `glockr1/ballr7` and `who-r-u0000/0000000000000000000000000000000000000` — carrying malicious pickle payloads that the platform's own scanner had passed as clean. They named the technique nullifAI, notified Hugging Face on 20 January 2025, and published the analysis the following month.

**Entry.** A model file is not inert data. PyTorch models are Python pickles, and unpickling executes opcodes; both models opened a platform-aware reverse shell to a hardcoded address (107.173.7.141) the moment they were loaded.

**Escalation.** None required for the payload itself, but the evasion is the finding: the models were stored as 7z archives rather than PyTorch's default ZIP, which broke Picklescan's pre-scan validation while leaving the pickle stream perfectly loadable. Deserialization executes opcodes sequentially and does not need a well-formed archive; the security scanner did. Detection and execution disagreed about what counted as a valid file, and the attacker lived in the gap.

**Blast radius.** No downstream victims were confirmed. Hugging Face removed both models within 24 hours of notification and subsequently changed Picklescan to detect malicious code inside "broken" pickle files. The instructive part is the class, not the count: every organization pulling models from a public hub was, at that moment, relying on a scanner that could be walked past with a change of container format.

**Human factor.** Teams adopted "scan the model before loading it" as sufficient assurance, in the same way an earlier generation trusted an antivirus verdict on an executable. Nobody decided to run untrusted code — they decided to trust a checkmark.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-06 (no deserialization of untrusted data — prefer safetensors and refuse pickle-format weights from public sources), DEPS-07 (scanning in CI, with the standing assumption that a passing scan is a weak signal rather than a guarantee), DEPS-04 (verify the artifact is the one you intended, from the publisher you intended). The gap this record exposes has no control yet: AGENT-NEW should require that model artifacts from public hubs are loaded only in a sandbox with no network egress and no credentials, so that a reverse shell in a pickle reaches nothing.

  - https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/26/hackers-publish-sensitive-patient-data-allegedly-stolen-from-australian-ivf-provider-genea
```

**What happened.** Genea, an Australian IVF provider running 22 fertility clinics and one of three companies accounting for over 80% of the national market, disclosed on 19 February 2025 that it was investigating suspicious activity on its network. A week later it confirmed data had been stolen and published online, and the Termite ransomware group claimed the attack on its leak site. A redacted court order obtained by Genea set out the intrusion timeline.

**Entry.** The court order states the attackers reached Genea's network on 31 January 2025 through a Citrix server. Neither Genea nor the order made public whether that was an unpatched vulnerability, a stolen credential or something else, so the entry vector is recorded as unknown rather than guessed.

**Escalation.** From the Citrix foothold the intruders reached Genea's primary file server, its domain controller, its backup software and BabySentry, the clinical patient-management system — the full estate, with no evident boundary between a remote-access appliance and the systems holding patient records.

**Blast radius.** On 14 February the attackers exfiltrated 940.7 GB to a DigitalOcean server they controlled; Termite's leak-site post claimed roughly 700 GB and published screenshots of identity documents and patient files. The data spans full names, contact details, dates of birth, next of kin, Medicare card numbers, private health insurance and Defence DA numbers, medical histories, diagnoses, medications, pathology results and clinicians' notes. Genea said there was no evidence financial data was involved, obtained a court injunction against further sharing of the leaked data, and notified the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner and the Australian Cyber Security Centre.

**Human factor.** A mid-sized clinical provider held fertility-treatment records — among the most sensitive categories that exist — on a network where a remote-access appliance could reach the domain controller and the patient database. Two weeks passed between intrusion and a near-terabyte export, and the exfiltration was not what raised the alarm.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing appliances such as Citrix gateways, which are the single most reliably attacked class of device), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on remote access), CLOUD-05 (segmentation, so an appliance compromise does not reach the domain controller and the clinical database), OBSV-02 (alerting on a 940 GB outbound transfer to a cloud host nobody uses).

  - https://www.elastic.co/security-labs/bit-bybit
  - https://thehackernews.com/2025/02/bybit-hack-traced-to-safewallet-supply.html
```

**What happened.** On 21 February 2025 at approximately 12:30 UTC, Bybit executed a routine transfer from an Ethereum multisig cold wallet to a hot wallet. The transaction its signers approved was not the transaction that executed: roughly 400,000 ETH and stETH, worth over $1.5 billion, went to an attacker-controlled address in the largest cryptocurrency theft on record. The FBI attributed the theft to North Korea, tracking the activity as TraderTraitor, and published 50 Ethereum addresses on 26 February asking exchanges, bridges and analytics firms to block related transactions. Forensic investigations by Sygnia and Verichains found the compromise originated at Safe{Wallet}, the multisig wallet provider — not at Bybit, whose own infrastructure showed no evidence of compromise.

**Entry.** Nothing was exploited at the victim. On 4 February a Safe{Wallet} developer's macOS workstation was compromised after they ran a malicious Python application referencing Docker logic and the domain getstockprice[.]com, which the attacker had registered two days earlier; the file path and behaviour indicate social engineering, though the exact lure and channel were never established. The next day, the attackers used that developer's active AWS session tokens to enter Safe{Wallet}'s AWS environment — session tokens that were already authenticated, so the MFA on the account never came into play. An attempt to register their own virtual MFA device against the developer's IAM user failed, but by then it was unnecessary.

**Escalation.** Two weeks of reconnaissance inside the AWS environment led to the thing that mattered: the S3 bucket serving app.safe.global, a statically hosted web app. On 19 February the JavaScript bundle was replaced with a version containing a payload that activated only for a specific Bybit multisig transaction and rewrote the transaction's logic and destination on the fly, leaving every other user's session untouched and the backdoor invisible to ordinary traffic. Two minutes after the theft executed, clean JavaScript was uploaded over it — the cleanup is itself preserved in Wayback Machine snapshots.

**Blast radius.** Over 400,000 ETH and stETH left Bybit's cold wallet in a single manipulated transaction. Bybit restored its ETH reserves and stated it remained solvent, but the funds were laundered rapidly and are not expected to be fully recovered. Safe{Wallet} rebuilt and reconfigured all infrastructure, rotated every credential, and restored service in phases with additional transaction hash, data and signature validation, temporarily removing the native Ledger integration used during the heist. External review found no flaw in Safe's smart contracts or in the source code of its frontend — because the source was never the problem.

**Human factor.** This record exists to retire the phrase "a human reviewed it". Multiple Bybit signers looked at a transaction on a hardware wallet workflow and approved it; what they were shown was rendered by JavaScript the attacker controlled. Any review that reads its input from the system under attack is not a control. Upstream, the ordinary failures are familiar: a developer laptop that could be reached by a social-engineering lure, long-lived cloud session tokens that made MFA irrelevant once stolen, and a static asset bucket whose contents shipped to users with no integrity check between upload and browser.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (managed developer machines, since a wallet provider's engineer workstation is production infrastructure), AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions — the theft rode a stolen session token for 17 days), VENDOR-05 (third-party scripts on sensitive pages minimized, pinned or isolated; a signing interface should not be a freely mutable static bundle), HUMAN-07 (a signing checklist that verifies the decoded transaction hash and destination on a device that does not trust the web UI), VENDOR-09 (verify what a bundled third-party component actually does at runtime, rather than assuming yesterday's bundle is today's).

**What happened.** NTT Communications, the enterprise arm of Japan's largest telecoms group, disclosed on 5 March 2025 that an attacker had accessed internal systems and exfiltrated information belonging to 17,891 corporate customers. The compromised system was the Order Information Distribution System, which handles service orders and changes for business customers.

Whatever access the attacker held reached more than one internal system, which is the clearest published evidence of movement inside the estate.

```yaml

dwell_days: 0
confidence: high
controls: [CICD-04, CICD-01, CRED-03, DEPS-10]
sources:
  - https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/harden-runner-detection-tj-actions-changed-files-action-is-compromised
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2025/03/18/supply-chain-compromise-third-party-github-action-cve-2025-30066
  - https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/github-actions-supply-chain-attack/
```

**What happened.** On 14 March 2025 at around 09:00 PT, StepSecurity's Harden-Runner flagged anomalous network traffic from CI jobs using `tj-actions/changed-files`, an action used in more than 23,000 repositories to determine which files a pull request touched. An attacker holding the maintainer's personal access token had retagged the action's release tags — every tag from v1.0.0 through v44.5.1 — to point at one malicious commit (`0e58ed86…`). GitHub removed the repository on 15 March and restored it cleaned the same day; the flaw was assigned CVE-2025-30066 and added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 26 March.

**Entry.** For the thousands of downstream projects, entry was ordinary dependency trust: a workflow referencing `@v44` or any other tag fetched attacker-controlled code on its next run. For the action itself, entry was a stolen personal access token — no compromise of GitHub, no exploit, just a maintainer credential in the wrong hands (its theft is the subject of record I2025Q1-09).

**Escalation.** None was needed beyond the retagging. A GitHub Action runs inside the job with the job's environment, so the payload — a Python script that dumped the Runner Worker process memory — could read whatever secrets the workflow had been given and print them into the build log. For public repositories, that log is world-readable, so exfiltration infrastructure was unnecessary: the attacker let each victim publish its own secrets, double-base64-encoded.

**Blast radius.** Over 23,000 repositories referenced the action, though only those whose workflows ran during the affected window — 12–15 March UTC, per CISA — executed the payload, and the dumped secrets were readable by anyone only where the repository was public. No count of repositories that actually leaked secrets was established. Exposed material included cloud access keys, GitHub tokens, npm tokens and private RSA keys. CISA directed organizations to audit affected projects, identify exposed secrets in logs, rotate them, and update to v46.0.1 or later. The mutable-tag design that made the attack possible remains the default way most workflows reference actions.

**Human factor.** Every affected team made the same reasonable-looking decision: pin a dependency to a version tag rather than an immutable commit hash, and grant the CI job the secrets it might need rather than the ones this step actually needs. Neither decision looks like a risk until the tag moves under you.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-04 (pin actions to full-length commit SHAs with minimal token permissions — the single control that neutralizes tag retagging entirely), CICD-01 (per-job secrets, so one step's compromise does not expose the whole workflow's credentials), CRED-03 (rotate everything the runner could see, on the assumption it was read), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed answer to "a dependency we run in CI was compromised today").

On 6 December an attacker submitted a malicious pull request to `spotbugs/sonar-findbugs`, whose workflow used the `pull_request_target` trigger — a trigger that runs fork-supplied code with access to repository secrets, the classic poisoned-pipeline setup — and leaked that token. The same maintainer also maintained reviewdog repositories. Using a disposable account, the attacker pushed a further malicious workflow to SpotBugs that leaked a second token, this one belonging to a reviewdog maintainer.

**Escalation.** With the reviewdog maintainer's token, the attacker force-pushed to a fork and manipulated git tags so `v1` pointed at malicious code, inserting a base64-encoded payload directly into the `install.sh` that consuming workflows execute. The payload dumped CI runner memory into workflow logs, the same technique that would appear in tj-actions three days later; at least five other reviewdog actions that depend on action-setup carried it onward, and researchers assess the stolen tj-actions maintainer token was itself captured this way.

**Blast radius.** Not publicly quantified. The compromise window was under two hours, but every workflow that ran a dependent reviewdog action during it printed its secrets to its own log, and public repositories printed them where anyone could read. The four-month gap between the first token theft and its use is the number worth keeping: a credential leaked in December sat unused until March, long after any plausible investigation window had closed.

**Human factor.** A maintainer put a token in a workflow file for convenience, on a project with no reason to think of itself as critical infrastructure; that token turned out to unlock a different, widely used project, which unlocked a third. Open-source maintainer credentials are shared blast radius across every repository the person touches, and nobody's threat model accounts for the other repositories.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no credential in a workflow file — the root of the entire chain), CICD-01 (CI secrets unavailable to untrusted pull-request builds; `pull_request_target` with fork code and secrets is this control's textbook violation), CICD-04 (SHA-pinned actions, which would have stopped the retagged `v1` from reaching a single consumer), CRED-07 (expiring tokens, since a personal access token that is still valid four months after it leaks is a permanent key).

```

**What happened.** Pillar Security disclosed on 18 March 2025 a technique it called the Rules File Backdoor, affecting AI code editors including GitHub Copilot and Cursor. A "rules file" — the project-level configuration that tells an assistant how to write code for this repository — can carry hidden instructions that make the assistant emit vulnerable or backdoored code, in every future generation, for every developer on the project.

**Entry.** Indirect prompt injection through a file nobody treats as executable. The malicious instructions are concealed with zero-width joiners, bidirectional text markers and other invisible Unicode, so a rules file looks empty or innocuous in a diff and in a pull-request review while reading as clear instructions to the model. A developer copying a rules file from a public repository, or accepting a pull request that adds one, adopts it without seeing it.

**Escalation.** None required. The rules file already sits in the trusted context the assistant reads on every request; from then on, the assistant — the part of the workflow developers scrutinize least — produces the attacker's code as its own suggestion, and the poisoning propagates with every fork and clone of the repository.

**Blast radius.** No exploitation in the wild was reported; this is a research finding, and its scale is recorded as undisclosed. Its significance is structural: the artifact people review is the generated code, but the artifact that determines the generated code is the configuration, and the configuration was invisible by design. Pillar disclosed to Cursor on 26 February and to GitHub on 12 March 2025; both initially responded that reviewing and accepting AI suggestions is the user's responsibility, and GitHub later shipped a warning for hidden Unicode text on 1 May 2025.

**Human factor.** Repository-level AI configuration was adopted as documentation — something to copy from a template, share between projects, commit without review. It is not documentation; it is instructions to a code generator with commit access to your project, and the review process treated it as noise.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-08 (repository-level agent configuration reviewed as executable code, with the same gate as a build script), AGENT-09 (scan for invisible and homoglyph characters in anything a model reads or writes, so an "empty" file that is not empty fails the check), AGENT-01 (treat everything the agent reads — including its own config — as untrusted data rather than authoritative instruction), CICD-02 (required review that actually renders what changed, since an invisible diff passes an ordinary approval).

```

**What happened.** Oracle Health, the electronic health records business Oracle acquired as Cerner in 2022, told affected customers it became aware on or around 20 February 2025 of unauthorized access to legacy Cerner data-migration servers that had not yet been moved to Oracle Cloud. Patient data was copied to a remote server. BleepingComputer reported the incident on 28 March 2025 after seeing the customer notification; Oracle did not publicly acknowledge it. This record is low confidence for that reason: it rests on customer notifications and sources relayed by a single publication, with no statement from the company or a regulator.

**Entry.** According to the notification, the attacker used compromised customer credentials to reach the legacy servers sometime after 22 January 2025. How those credentials were obtained was not disclosed.

**Escalation.** The servers were pre-migration Cerner systems — outside the controls of the platform customers believed they were on. Multiple healthcare organizations' data was taken, and the notification did not explain how one customer's credentials could reach several organizations' records; that gap is the most important unanswered question in the record, and it points at shared or overscoped access on a legacy tier.

**Blast radius.** Not quantified publicly. Data "may" have included patient information from electronic health records, though sources told BleepingComputer patient data theft was confirmed. Affected hospitals were then extorted individually by an actor calling themselves "Andrew", demanding millions in cryptocurrency and standing up clearnet sites to pressure them. Oracle Health told hospitals it would not notify patients directly, leaving each organization to determine its own HIPAA notification obligations, while offering templates and help identifying affected individuals.

**Human factor.** Three separate choices made this worse than the intrusion itself: leaving pre-migration copies of patient data on legacy servers outside the destination platform's controls; communicating the incident on unbranded plain paper and by phone rather than in writing on letterhead; and pushing the notification duty to customers who had no visibility into what was taken. The technical failure was ordinary; the response turned it into a disclosure failure.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-09 (an inventory that finds forgotten pre-migration servers and retires them, because a migration that leaves the old copy running has not finished), AUTH-01 (MFA on any credential that can reach patient records, legacy tier included), DATA-12 (cap what a single compromised credential can reach, so one customer's login cannot export several organizations' records), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan naming the decider, the communicator and the disclosure clock — the missing artifact behind every complaint customers made about this response).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **CISA** published an alert on the `tj-actions/changed-files` compromise on 18 March 2025 and added both CVE-2025-30066 and CVE-2025-30154 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 26 March, directing organizations to audit affected projects, search build logs for exposed (and double-base64-encoded) secrets, rotate everything found, and update to a patched version.
- The quarter's consolidated CI guidance, from StepSecurity, Wiz and Unit 42 alike: **pin GitHub Actions to full-length commit SHAs rather than mutable tags**, restrict workflow token permissions, and stop using `pull_request_target` with fork-supplied code and repository secrets. StepSecurity published a drop-in replacement action and the runtime-detection write-up that caught the attack the same morning it began.
- The **FBI** issued a public service announcement on 26 February 2025 attributing the Bybit theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor activity and publishing 50 Ethereum addresses, asking RPC node operators, exchanges, bridges, analytics firms and DeFi services to block associated transactions — an industry-wide blocking request rather than a victim notification.
- **Safe{Wallet}** rebuilt and reconfigured all infrastructure, rotated every credential, and restored service in phases with added validation of transaction hash, data and signatures, temporarily removing the native Ledger integration used in the heist. The broader lesson circulated across the crypto sector all quarter: verify the decoded transaction on a device that does not trust the web interface presenting it.
- **Hugging Face** removed the nullifAI models within 24 hours of ReversingLabs' notification and updated Picklescan to detect malicious code inside "broken" Pickle files — closing the specific gap between what a scanner validates and what a deserializer executes, while leaving the general case (pickle-format model weights are executable code) intact.
- **GitHub** shipped a warning for hidden Unicode characters on 1 May 2025 following Pillar Security's disclosure; Cursor's position was that reviewing and accepting AI-generated suggestions remains the user's responsibility. Repository-level AI configuration entered the quarter as documentation and left it as an artifact that needs code review.
- **PowerSchool** published a final incident update on 7 March 2025, offered two years of identity protection and credit monitoring to all involved students and educators regardless of regulatory requirement, and later acknowledged it had paid a ransom — which did not prevent individual districts being extorted with the same data in May 2025. It is the quarter's clearest data point against paying.
- **Genea** obtained a court-ordered injunction restraining further sharing of the leaked patient data and notified both the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner and the Australian Cyber Security Centre — an increasingly common legal containment step whose practical effect on leak-site distribution remains limited.
