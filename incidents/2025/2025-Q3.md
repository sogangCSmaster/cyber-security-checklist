# 2025 Q3 — When Developer Tooling Became The Payload

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-dependency — four records start with a package the victim installed and never read, and a fifth with a vendor's OAuth token; meanwhile the five largest *operational* incidents (Jaguar Land Rover, Collins Aerospace, Asahi, Orange Belgium, Miljödata) all carry `entry/unknown`, because none of those victims disclosed a vector
> - **New technique this quarter:** two, both firsts — malware that invokes the developer's own installed AI CLI as its reconnaissance engine (s1ngularity, 26 August), and a self-replicating npm worm that republishes itself through every package a compromised maintainer owns (Shai-Hulud, 16 September)
> - **Recurring failure:** one maintainer account, one CI token, or one vendor's OAuth grant was the entire perimeter — TOTP that a phishing page could relay in real time, publish tokens with no cooldown, and connected apps nobody reviewed
> - **Records in this file:** 29

sources:
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/qantas-confirms-data-breach-impacts-57-million-customers/
  - https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/voice-phishing-data-extortion
```

**What happened.** Qantas detected unusual activity on 1 July 2025 on a third-party customer-servicing platform used by one of its contact centres, and made the incident public the following day. The airline initially estimated up to six million customer records were involved and later confirmed the figure at 5.7 million, with a field-by-field breakdown rather than a single headline number. The incident sat inside the broader 2025 campaign against Salesforce-hosted customer data that Google's Threat Intelligence Group tracks as UNC6040, with extortion handled by a partner cluster claiming the ShinyHunters name.

**Entry.** Qantas described the vector as a criminal targeting a call centre and, through it, gaining access to a third-party customer-servicing platform — social engineering of a human operator, not an exploit against Qantas's own systems. The technique GTIG documented for this campaign is a voice call impersonating IT support that walks the employee through authorising the attacker's tooling, which is why this record carries `entry/vishing`; Qantas itself did not use the word "vishing", so the specific channel is reported rather than confirmed by the victim.

**Escalation.** None required. The platform the attacker reached was a customer-servicing system that legitimately held the records; once authenticated as the contact-centre operator, bulk reading was an ordinary function of the tool, not a privilege that had to be won.

**Blast radius.** 5.7 million customers: 4 million records held name, email address and Frequent Flyer details; 1.3 million also held addresses; 1.1 million dates of birth; 900,000 phone numbers; 400,000 gender; 10,000 meal preferences. Qantas stated that no Frequent Flyer account credentials, passwords, PINs, financial information or passport details were taken. The airline obtained a court injunction restraining publication of the stolen data.

**Human factor.** A contact-centre agent — often offshore, often on a platform the airline does not itself operate — was reachable by phone, had bulk access to customer records, and had no independent way to verify that the "IT support" caller was real. The control that failed is identity verification on inbound support contact, not anything in the airline's network.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (verify the identity of anyone requesting access or an authorisation step, independent of what the caller claims), HUMAN-05 (training that reflects current voice-impersonation technique, not just email phishing), DATA-11 (bulk export from a customer-servicing platform should be a privileged, logged, alerting action rather than a routine one).

sources:
  - https://securityaffairs.com/179706/cyber-crime/it-worker-arrested-for-selling-access-in-100m-pix-cyber-heist.html
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_in_Brazil
```

**What happened.** C&M Software is one of the technology providers that connects smaller Brazilian banks and payment institutions to PIX, the Central Bank's instant-payment rail. Over a single night at the start of July 2025, attackers issued fraudulent transfers out of the reserve accounts that at least six institutions hold at the Central Bank, moving more than 540 million reais — roughly 100 million US dollars, with São Paulo investigators later describing a possible total above one billion reais. São Paulo civil police arrested a C&M employee, João Nazareno Roque, on 4 July; the Central Bank suspended part of C&M's connection to the system as a precaution. C&M stated publicly that the breach came from social engineering of a person, not a defect in its systems.

**Entry.** A paid insider. Roque told police he was approached outside a bar in São Paulo, sold his system password for 5,000 reais, and was paid a further 10,000 reais to run code the criminals supplied inside C&M's environment — 15,000 reais in total, about 2,700 US dollars, for access to a national payment rail. He described communicating only by telephone and changing handsets every fifteen days to stay untraceable.

**Escalation.** None was needed in the usual sense, but the shape of the credential is the finding: one operator's password, with no second factor and no second human, was sufficient to reach an interface that could originate transfers from institutional reserve accounts. The attacker did not need to move laterally because the first account already sat next to the money.

**Blast radius.** More than 540 million reais diverted from the reserve accounts of at least six financial institutions in one night, laundered onward partly through cryptocurrency exchanges and over-the-counter brokers. Police froze some 270 million reais, pursued further suspects, and the Central Bank suspended access for institutions suspected of receiving the diverted funds. The incident prompted tighter Central Bank rules for the technology providers that connect institutions to PIX.

**Human factor.** The recruitment price is the lesson. An organisation whose entire business is connecting banks to a payment system treated one of its own engineers as a trusted single point, with a credential valuable enough to be worth a hundred million dollars to a criminal group and cheap enough to buy for the price of a used motorcycle. Nothing in the control set assumed that the person holding the password might be the adversary.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every human account with production reach, so a sold password is not by itself an entry), HUMAN-08 (separation of duties on payment origination, so that no single operator credential can both create and release a transfer), HUMAN-02 (a second person on high-impact actions), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal transfer volume per institution, since the entire loss occurred inside one night).

scale: 64000000 people
cost_usd: undisclosed

confidence: high
controls: [CRED-10, AUTH-02, AUTH-06, INPUT-09, DATA-02, OBSV-06]
sources:
  - https://ian.sh/mcdonalds
```

**What happened.** McHire is the hiring platform Paradox.ai operates for McDonald's franchisees, fronted by a conversational AI assistant called "Olivia" that collects applicants' details and administers a personality questionnaire. Researchers Ian Carroll and Sam Curry found that the staff login accepted a documented-style default account, and that once inside, the application's own API would return any applicant's record on request. They reported it on 30 June 2025, the default account was disabled within about forty-five minutes, Paradox.ai confirmed full remediation the next day, and the research was published on 9 July.

**Entry.** Two ordinary flaws in series. The "Paradox team members" login path accepted the username and password `123456`, a test account that had never been removed, and it granted a restaurant-owner administrative view. That is `entry/default-credentials` in its plainest form: not a weak password chosen by a user, but a placeholder left enabled in production.

**Escalation.** None required, and the second flaw is the more instructive one. The applicant-record endpoint took a numeric `lead_id` parameter and performed no check that the caller was entitled to that record, so decrementing the number walked backwards through the applicant database. The researchers reached records including names, email addresses, phone numbers, addresses, application status and chat transcripts, along with tokens that would have allowed logging in as an individual applicant.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 64 million applicant records were reachable. No abuse was confirmed: this was a researcher-found exposure, reported privately and fixed within a day, and the value of the record is that the same two defects appear constantly in production systems. Job applicants are a population that cannot choose the platform, cannot delete themselves from it, and often have no idea which vendor holds their data.

**Human factor.** A test credential survived into production because nobody owned the question "which accounts exist on this system and why", and the authorisation check on the applicant endpoint was never written because the identifier was assumed to be private. Both are review failures rather than coding failures — the code did exactly what someone wrote, and no one checked that user A could not read user B's record.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-10 (no default or shared credential in anything shipped or deployed; a unique one is forced at first use), AUTH-02 (authorization checked server-side, per object, on every request, so an incremented identifier returns nothing), DATA-02 (a negative test that proves user A cannot read user B's record, run in CI rather than by a visiting researcher), INPUT-09 (identifiers are unguessable, and unguessability is never the only control).

sources:

  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_Response_Route
```

**What happened.** In early 2022 a defence official emailed a spreadsheet outside the intended group. The official believed it contained about 150 rows; it in fact contained more than 33,000, covering over 18,700 Afghans who had applied for relocation to the UK under the Afghan Relocations and Assistance Policy, together with contact details and information about their links to British forces. The Ministry of Defence did not learn of the loss until August 2023, when an excerpt surfaced in a Facebook group. It obtained an unprecedented *contra mundum* superinjunction on 1 September 2023 barring any reporting of the leak's existence, and a secret relocation scheme, later named the Afghanistan Response Route, was run behind it. Mr Justice Chamberlain discharged the injunction on 15 July 2025, at which point the incident, the scheme and four sealed judgments all became public at once.

**Entry.** A person with legitimate access sent data to the wrong place. There was no attacker, no vulnerability and no intrusion: the sender misunderstood the contents of the file being sent — a hidden or unexamined range of rows behind a visible extract — and no process stood between that misunderstanding and an email leaving the department.

**Escalation.** None required. The spreadsheet was itself the complete, unredacted, unencrypted dataset; onward circulation was performed by ordinary human sharing until an extract reached a public Facebook group roughly eighteen months later.

**Blast radius.** Details of more than 18,700 applicants, in a dataset of over 33,000 rows that identified people by their association with British forces in a country then governed by the adversary those forces had fought. Around 6,900 people were relocated under a scheme originally scoped for about 150, at a projected cost of roughly £850 million with wider exposure estimated far higher; more than 665 affected Afghans had brought claims against the Ministry by mid-2025. A classified 2024 review by Paul Rimmer concluded the leak was unlikely to significantly increase individual risk — a judgement reached three years after the fact and contested since.

**Human factor.** Three compounding decisions, none of them technical. Someone extracted a working subset from a master list without severing it from the rest of the data. Someone sent a spreadsheet to external recipients without a check on what it actually contained. And once the loss was known, the institutional response optimised for secrecy over notification, so the people whose safety was at stake were not told for years — the superinjunction protected the programme and the department, and left the data subjects uninformed.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-10 (confirm recipients and destinations before data leaves the organisation, as a required step rather than a habit), HUMAN-07 (a checklist for sensitive data transfers — what is in the file, who is receiving it, has it been reduced to the minimum — because a competent operator working from memory is exactly what failed here), DATA-09 (data minimisation: the working extract should never have carried 33,000 rows when 150 were needed), OBSV-05 (an incident plan whose disclosure clock runs for the benefit of the people affected, not the institution).

  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2025/07/20/microsoft-releases-guidance-on-exploitation-of-sharepoint-vulnerability-cve-2025-53770
```

**What happened.** A chain of SharePoint Server flaws — CVE-2025-49706 and CVE-2025-49704, then the patch bypasses CVE-2025-53770 and CVE-2025-53771, collectively nicknamed "ToolShell" — was exploited against internet-facing on-premises SharePoint farms. Microsoft observed exploitation as early as 7 July 2025, published guidance on 19 July, and on 22 July named three actors: the China state-linked Linen Typhoon and Violet Typhoon, and Storm-2603, which from 18 July began deploying Warlock ransomware through modified Group Policy Objects. SharePoint Online was not affected; this was entirely an on-premises problem.

**Entry.** Unauthenticated remote code execution against the SharePoint web front end, achieved by abusing unsafe deserialization of untrusted data. Attackers dropped a web shell — most commonly `spinstall0.aspx`, with filename variants — onto the server to run commands and read server state.

**Escalation.** This is the part that made ToolShell different from an ordinary RCE. The web shell's purpose was not persistence on disk but theft of the server's ASP.NET `machineKey` material — the `validationKey` and `decryptionKey` used to sign and encrypt `__VIEWSTATE`. With those keys an attacker can forge a valid, correctly signed `__VIEWSTATE` payload at will and get code execution again from an ordinary unauthenticated HTTP request. Patching closes the original hole but does nothing about the stolen keys, so a patched server with unrotated keys stays owned. From there, Storm-2603 moved laterally and pushed ransomware via GPO.

**Blast radius.** Never aggregated into a single authoritative count. Microsoft and CISA both described widespread exploitation across government, defence and education operators of on-premises SharePoint; individual victim counts published by vendors during July varied and are not reproduced here. Confirmed outcomes span credential and key theft, document exfiltration consistent with espionage, and Warlock ransomware deployment.

**Human factor.** SharePoint on-premises is classic retained-legacy infrastructure — kept because a migration was never funded, reachable from the internet because that is how staff used it, and patched on an enterprise cadence rather than an internet-facing one. The second failure was procedural: many operators applied the July update and considered themselves finished, without performing the machine-key rotation that the guidance made a mandatory, ordered step.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems measured in days, plus an inventory of what cannot be patched), CLOUD-08 (a document-collaboration server does not need to be published to the open internet), CRED-03 (rotate anything the attacker may have seen — here, rotate ASP.NET machine keys *before* patching, patch, rotate again, and restart IIS, because key rotation is the only step that actually evicts the attacker).

  - https://github.com/aws/aws-toolkit-vscode/security/advisories/GHSA-7g7f-ff96-5gcw
  - https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/24/amazon_q_ai_prompt/
```

**What happened.** Someone with no special standing opened a pull request against the open-source `aws-toolkit-vscode` repository on 13 July 2025. It was accepted, and the change rode the normal release pipeline into version 1.84.0 of the Amazon Q Developer extension for Visual Studio Code. The change was not conventional malware: it was a natural-language instruction addressed to the coding agent itself, telling it that its goal was to return the system to a near-factory state — clearing local files and directories with shell commands and deleting AWS resources such as S3 buckets, EC2 instances and IAM users through the AWS CLI, running continuously until finished. AWS published a bulletin on 23 July and shipped 1.85.0.

**Entry.** A pull request from an ordinary GitHub account. AWS's own bulletin identifies the root cause as "an inappropriately scoped GitHub token in their CodeBuild configuration" — the token available to the build had far more repository authority than building an extension requires, so a contribution that reached the build could reach the release.

**Escalation.** The overscoped CodeBuild token is the whole escalation. There was no exploit and no privilege chain: contribution access plus a build credential that could commit and publish equalled publishing authority over an extension installed on developer machines that hold live AWS credentials.

**Blast radius.** AWS states that the malicious code "was distributed with the extension but was unsuccessful in executing due to a syntax error", that no customer services or environments were affected, that 1.84.0 was pulled from distribution channels, and that the credentials were revoked and replaced. AWS did not publish an install count for the affected version, so none is given here. The significance of this record is not its damage but its demonstration: the payload was English prose, the execution engine was an AI agent with the user's own permissions, and it passed code review.

**Human factor.** A maintainer merged an external pull request into a repository whose build held a token strong enough to publish, and the release gate did not treat "text that will be read by an agent as instructions" as executable content. Prompt files, agent rules and system-prompt fragments were reviewed as documentation rather than as code.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-05 (deploy credentials scoped per repository, least-privilege and expiring — the bulletin names the opposite as the cause), CICD-09 and CICD-02 (release approval requiring a second human, with required review on anything that reaches users), AGENT-08 (repository-level agent configuration and prompt content reviewed as executable code, because for an agent that is exactly what it is).

```

**What happened.** Tea is a women-only app for sharing safety information about men, and it required new users to submit a selfie and a government-issued ID to prove they were women. Around 25 July 2025 an anonymous poster on 4chan pointed at a Tea Firebase storage bucket that was readable without any authentication, holding roughly 59 GB of data — about 72,000 images, of which approximately 13,000 were the verification selfies and government IDs and about 59,000 were images from posts, comments and messages. Three days later, on 28 July, researcher Kasra Rahjerdi found a second exposure: roughly 1.1 million private direct messages dating from 2023 to that week.

**Entry.** No exploit and no attacker skill. A legacy Firebase storage bucket was configured to allow unauthenticated reads. Tea's own statement describes it as "a legacy data storage system" holding "a dataset from prior to February 2024". The second exposure was different in kind: message history could be retrieved using a user's own API key, meaning the access-control check on message retrieval was effectively absent rather than merely misconfigured.

**Escalation.** None required — a public URL returned the files. The one structural amplifier was the legacy system itself: a store that had been superseded stayed online, stayed populated, and stayed outside whatever controls the current stack had.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 72,000 images including about 13,000 selfies and photographed government IDs — the single most sensitive category a consumer app can hold, since a face plus an ID document is a complete identity-theft and doxxing kit — plus about 1.1 million private messages whose content included discussions of infidelity, abortion, phone numbers and planned meeting locations, and which were linkable to identifiable individuals. Tea took the system offline, said it found no evidence of access to other parts of its environment, offered identity-protection services and notified law enforcement; the messaging feature was withdrawn. Multiple class-action suits followed in August.

**Human factor.** Two decisions made this incident rather than the exposure itself. First, verification documents that existed to answer one question at signup were retained indefinitely rather than checked and deleted. Second, a superseded storage system was left populated and reachable instead of being emptied and decommissioned, because nobody owned the question "what is still running that we stopped using?"

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy buckets specifically audited — this is precisely the legacy-bucket case the control is written for), DATA-07 (identity documents deleted after the verification decision is made, which would have removed the 13,000 worst files from existence), CLOUD-09 (an inventory that surfaces forgotten and unowned assets so a pre-2024 store cannot quietly remain internet-readable).

**What happened.** On 16 July 2025 an attacker reached the third-party, cloud-hosted CRM system that Allianz Life uses for customer and partner records, and the insurer discovered it the following day. Allianz Life disclosed the breach at the end of July, describing the cause as a social-engineering technique used against the third-party platform rather than an intrusion into its own network. **Entry.** Social engineering of a human with access to the CRM. Allianz Life used the general term rather than naming the channel; the campaign it belongs to — the 2025 wave against Salesforce-hosted customer data attributed to ShinyHunters and tracked by Google as UNC6040 — worked by telephoning employees while posing as IT support and talking them through authorising the attacker's connected application. The record carries `entry/vishing` on that basis, reported rather than confirmed by the victim.

**Escalation.** None required. The CRM legitimately held the records; once the attacker held an authorised session or connected app, bulk reading was an ordinary function of the platform.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 1.5 million people, with names, addresses, dates of birth and Social Security numbers among the fields. Extortion followed in the usual pattern for this campaign — the data was later published rather than merely threatened. Notably, detection took one day, which limited the window but not the loss: the export was the first and only action the attacker needed.

**Human factor.** An insurer's most sensitive population data sat in a SaaS CRM where a single employee's cooperation, obtained by telephone, produced a complete export. The employee was not careless in any way the organisation had trained them to recognise; the help-desk impersonation script is convincing precisely because it mirrors a real internal process.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for anyone requesting an authorisation step, independent of the caller's claim), HUMAN-05 (training that reflects current voice-impersonation technique), VENDOR-03 (authorising a new connected application requires review, so one employee cannot grant a third party access alone), DATA-11 (bulk export as a privileged, logged, alerting action).

On victim machines, `telemetry.js` searched for wallets, keystores, `.env` files, SSH private keys, npm credentials and `gh auth token` output — and then ran locally installed AI command-line tools, including the Claude, Gemini and Amazon Q CLIs, with their approval prompts switched off (`--dangerously-skip-permissions`, `--yolo`, `--trust-all-tools`), instructing them to enumerate the filesystem and produce an inventory of sensitive files. The developer's own agent, already trusted and already authorised, performed the reconnaissance; Wiz observed this succeed in hundreds of cases before model-side guardrails refused. Exfiltration went to repositories named `s1ngularity-repository`, `-0` and `-1` under the victim's account, with the payload double- and triple-base64-encoded. The second escalation was ordinary credential reuse: the harvested GitHub tokens were replayed.

**Blast radius.** Phase one ran from about 22:32 UTC on 26 August until npm removed the versions at 02:44 UTC on 27 August, with GitHub disabling the attacker-created repositories around 09:00 UTC. Wiz counted over a thousand valid GitHub tokens, dozens of valid cloud credentials and npm tokens, and roughly twenty thousand further files leaked; counts differ between vendor analyses, which is why this record is medium confidence rather than high. Phase two ran from 16:00 UTC on 28 August to 02:00 UTC on 29 August: using the stolen tokens, automation flipped over 5,500 private repositories to public across more than 400 users and organisations, exposing source code that had never been meant to leave its owner. Affected versions were `nx` 20.9.0, 20.10.0, 20.11.0, 20.12.0, 21.5.0, 21.6.0, 21.7.0 and 21.8.0, plus matching `@nx/devkit`, `@nx/js`, `@nx/node`, `@nx/workspace` and `@nx/eslint` releases and `@nx/enterprise-cloud` and `@nx/key` 3.2.0.

**Human factor.** Three defaults nobody revisited: a permissive workflow token left at the old default, an unquoted variable in a shell step, and a publish pipeline that could be triggered programmatically. Downstream, the decisive habit was running `npm install` with lifecycle scripts enabled on a machine that held cloud credentials and an AI CLI configured to act without asking.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-02 (install scripts disabled by default on developer machines and in CI — the entire victim-side payload was a `postinstall` hook), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before adopting a newly published version, which would have covered the four-hour window almost entirely), CICD-01 and CICD-04 (CI secrets unavailable to untrusted pull-request builds, and pipeline steps with minimal token permissions), AGENT-06 and AGENT-02 (an AI CLI should not be invocable in a mode that skips human approval by any process that can reach a shell, and its credentials should be scoped to its task).

**Entry.** An ordinary dependency install. The worm's post-install payload ran on the developer's or build agent's machine with whatever authority that context already had, which in practice meant npm publish tokens, GitHub Personal Access Tokens and cloud credentials.

**Escalation.** Three mechanisms compounding. First, collection: the payload installed and ran TruffleHog — a legitimate open-source scanner that recognises more than 800 secret formats — against the filesystem, read `.npmrc`, harvested environment variables and configuration files, and queried the cloud instance metadata service for AWS, GCP and Azure keys. Second, exfiltration in plain sight: it created a public GitHub repository named `Shai-Hulud` under the victim's own account and committed the stolen material, double-base64-encoded, in a `data.json` file, also posting to a `webhook.site` endpoint; it installed a GitHub Actions workflow to keep exfiltrating repository secrets on every run, and force-migrated private repositories to public copies carrying a `-migration` suffix. Third, and this is the novel part, propagation: when the payload found a working npm token it authenticated as that maintainer, injected itself into their other packages, and published new versions — no command-and-control server was needed, because the worm read its own content to reproduce. Each newly poisoned package was a fresh infection vector for the next maintainer downstream.

**Blast radius.** CISA's alert puts the September wave at over 500 packages. The credential classes harvested were npm tokens, GitHub PATs and AWS, GCP and Azure API keys, plus whatever else TruffleHog recognised. CISA's mitigation guidance told organisations to pin npm dependencies to versions released before 16 September 2025, rotate all developer credentials, enforce phishing-resistant MFA on GitHub and npm, and block outbound connections to `webhook.site`. Unit 42 assessed from the style of the bash code that the operator used an LLM to help write parts of the malware. A second, larger wave under the same name followed in November 2025 and belongs to Q4, not to this record.

**Human factor.** The ecosystem's structural habit is the failure here: publish tokens that never expire, sitting on developer laptops and in CI environments, with lifecycle scripts enabled so that any install can run arbitrary code with those tokens in reach. That combination is what turned a compromise into a worm — a self-propagating attack needs the victim to hold the credential that infects the next victim, and npm's default posture handed that over.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-02 (install scripts disabled by default in CI and on developer machines — without post-install execution the worm has no body), CRED-07 and CICD-04 (publish tokens that expire and are scoped per job, so a stolen token cannot republish an entire portfolio), DEPS-01 and DEPS-03 (frozen lockfile installs plus a cooldown on new versions), DEPS-10 (a named, rehearsed response for "a package we depend on was compromised", since the practical question in the week of 16 September was how fast an organisation could answer whether it had installed one of 500 packages).

**What happened.** Noma Labs disclosed a vulnerability chain in Salesforce Agentforce, rated CVSS 9.4 and named ForcedLeak, that allowed an outsider to make an organisation's own AI agent read out CRM data. They reported it to Salesforce on 28 July 2025; Salesforce acknowledged it on 31 July, shipped Trusted URLs enforcement for Agentforce and Einstein AI on 8 September, and the research was published on 25 September. No exploitation in the wild was reported.

**Entry.** Web-to-Lead is the Salesforce feature that turns a public web form into a CRM record, so anyone on the internet can write into the database by design. The researchers placed instructions in the lead Description field, which accepts up to 42,000 characters and so comfortably holds a prompt. The injected text sat dormant until an employee later asked Agentforce an ordinary question about the lead, at which point the agent read the attacker's text along with the legitimate record and treated it as instruction rather than data.

**Escalation.** The agent's own guardrail became the exfiltration channel. Agent output was restricted to a content-security-policy allowlist of trusted domains — but one domain on that list, `my-salesforce-cms.com`, had been allowed to expire and was available to register for about five dollars. Buying it converted an allowlist entry into an attacker-controlled endpoint, and the proof of concept then moved data out in an image URL that the browser fetched automatically. The agent never did anything it was not permitted to do.

**Blast radius.** None confirmed. The exposure was potential across organisations running Agentforce with Web-to-Lead enabled, covering lead and contact data, sales pipeline information, internal notes and, through connected integrations, whatever else the agent could reach. Salesforce re-secured the expired domain and made trusted-URL enforcement the default.

**Human factor.** Somebody, years earlier, added a domain to an allowlist and nobody owned it afterwards. Domain expiry is not a security event in any calendar, and an allowlist is exactly the kind of artefact that accretes entries and never loses them. The second human factor is structural: the agent was given private data, untrusted external content and an outbound channel at the same time, with no human in between — the combination that makes prompt injection consequential rather than merely possible.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-01 (everything an agent reads is untrusted data, never instruction — a lead submitted by a stranger most of all), AGENT-03 (private data, untrusted content and an external channel never combine unsupervised), CLOUD-09 (an inventory in which unowned and forgotten assets are found and removed, applied to trusted-domain allowlists as much as to servers — a lapsed domain on an allowlist is a live security control that now belongs to someone else), INPUT-02 (server-side validation and sanitisation of the fields a public form writes into an agent's context).

```

**What happened.** An npm package called `postmark-mcp`, published by a developer using the handle `phanpak`, presented itself as a Model Context Protocol server letting AI assistants send transactional email through Postmark. It was a near-exact copy of Postmark Labs' legitimate open-source MCP code, and for fifteen versions it behaved exactly as advertised. Version 1.0.16, published on 17 September 2025, added one line: a BCC on every outgoing message to `phan@giftshop[.]club`. Koi Security published the finding in late September 2025, and it is the first malicious MCP server documented in the wild.

**Entry.** Ordinary installation of a plausible-looking package. Postmark's own advisory states that the company had never published its MCP server to npm before this incident, so the package was occupying a name that looked official and was not — which is why this record carries `entry/typosquatting` alongside the dependency and agent-tooling tags. Nothing was exploited; developers wired the server into their agent because the name and the code both looked right.

**Escalation.** None required, and that is the lesson. An MCP server sits inside the agent's trust boundary: it is granted broad permissions by design and its outputs and side effects are not independently reviewed. Sending email was the server's sanctioned function, so adding a recipient needed no privilege at all.

**Blast radius.** The package recorded 1,643 downloads before the author removed it from npm. Because the server sat in the path of whatever the agent was asked to send, the exfiltrated material is whatever those installations emailed — password-reset links, invoices, internal memos, customer correspondence — copied silently to the author's domain with no trace in the sender's own outbox. No count of intercepted messages was published. Postmark confirmed that its own API and services were unaffected and advised users to remove the package, review email logs and rotate any credentials that had been sent by email during the exposure period.

**Human factor.** Fifteen honest releases bought trust, and the sixteenth spent it. Developers pinned nothing, diffed nothing, and reviewed an MCP server once at adoption rather than on each update — treating a component that reads and writes on the agent's behalf the way one treats a formatting utility. The single added line would have been visible in a one-minute version diff.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-04 (MCP servers and IDE extensions pinned, reviewed and version-diffed — this control is exactly the one line that was added), AGENT-03 (private data, untrusted components and an external channel should never combine unsupervised; an email-sending tool is by definition an external channel), AGENT-07 (agent actions logged with their originating prompt, so a silently added recipient shows up in the organisation's own records), DEPS-03 and DEPS-09 (a cooldown on new versions, and maintainer provenance treated as a selection criterion rather than an assumption).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **CISA alert, 23 September 2025 — "Widespread Supply Chain Compromise Impacting npm Ecosystem".** The federal response to Shai-Hulud told organisations to audit dependency trees, pin npm packages to versions released before 16 September 2025, rotate all developer credentials, enforce phishing-resistant MFA on GitHub and npm accounts, block outbound connections to `webhook.site`, and turn on branch protection, secret scanning and Dependabot. It is the first CISA alert whose central instruction is effectively "pin your dependencies to a date".
- **Microsoft and CISA on ToolShell: rotate keys, then patch, then rotate again.** The SharePoint guidance made ASP.NET machine-key rotation a mandatory, ordered step around patching, followed by an `iisreset`, because attackers who had already stolen the keys could keep forging valid `__VIEWSTATE` payloads against a fully patched server. It is the quarter's clearest statement that patching is not eviction, and it also warned that malicious modules persist unless manually removed from configuration files.
- **AWS pulled the extension and named its own root cause.** AWS removed Amazon Q Developer extension 1.84.0 from distribution, revoked and replaced the overscoped GitHub token in its CodeBuild configuration, and shipped 1.85.0 — publishing the credential-scoping failure in its own bulletin rather than describing the incident only in terms of the attacker.
- **Nx published a public post-mortem of its own workflow.** Nx named three compounding conditions in one repository — a `pull_request_target` trigger, a `GITHUB_TOKEN` still at the pre-2023 read-and-write default, and an unsanitised PR title echoed into a shell — plus a programmatically dispatchable publish workflow. It is the most directly reusable artefact of the quarter for anyone auditing their own GitHub Actions.
- **Salesloft, Salesforce and Google revoked at the token layer, not the account layer.** Salesloft revoked all Drift-to-Salesforce connections on 20 August 2025, Drift was removed from AppExchange, and Google revoked every Drift Email Workspace OAuth token. The response model worth copying is mass revocation of a third party's grants across an entire customer base, executed by the platform rather than by each customer.
- **Google Threat Intelligence published concrete Salesforce hardening for the vishing campaign:** restrict the "API Enabled", "Customize Application" and "Manage Connected Apps" permissions to a minimal set of administrators, allowlist approved connected applications, apply trusted-IP and login-range restrictions to block VPN and Tor origins, and use Salesforce Shield transaction-security policies and event monitoring to alert on large downloads.
- **ENISA publicly confirmed the Collins Aerospace MUSE incident was ransomware,** which mattered because several national authorities and airports were otherwise describing the same event in incompatible terms; a single agency naming the cause is what let affected airports plan for a recovery timeline rather than an open-ended outage.
- **Postmark disowned the package publicly and precisely,** stating it had never published its MCP server to npm before the incident, that its own API and services were unaffected, and telling users to remove the package, audit email logs and rotate credentials sent by email during the exposure window. The useful precedent is a vendor publishing a disavowal fast enough to be the top result for its own product name.
