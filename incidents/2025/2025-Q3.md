# 2025 Q3 — When Developer Tooling Became The Payload

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-dependency — four records start with a package the victim installed and never read, and a fifth with a vendor's OAuth token; meanwhile the five largest *operational* incidents (Jaguar Land Rover, Collins Aerospace, Asahi, Orange Belgium, Miljödata) all carry `entry/unknown`, because none of those victims disclosed a vector
> - **New technique this quarter:** two, both firsts — malware that invokes the developer's own installed AI CLI as its reconnaissance engine (s1ngularity, 26 August), and a self-replicating npm worm that republishes itself through every package a compromised maintainer owns (Shai-Hulud, 16 September)
> - **Recurring failure:** one maintainer account, one CI token, or one vendor's OAuth grant was the entire perimeter — TOTP that a phishing page could relay in real time, publish tokens with no cooldown, and connected apps nobody reviewed
> - **Records in this file:** 15

## Incidents

```yaml
  - https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/voice-phishing-data-extortion
```

**What happened.** Qantas detected unusual activity on 1 July 2025 on a third-party customer-servicing platform used by one of its contact centres, and made the incident public the following day. The airline initially estimated up to six million customer records were involved and later confirmed the figure at 5.7 million, with a field-by-field breakdown rather than a single headline number. The incident sat inside the broader 2025 campaign against Salesforce-hosted customer data that Google's Threat Intelligence Group tracks as UNC6040, with extortion handled by a partner cluster claiming the ShinyHunters name.

**Entry.** Qantas described the vector as a criminal targeting a call centre and, through it, gaining access to a third-party customer-servicing platform — social engineering of a human operator, not an exploit against Qantas's own systems. The technique GTIG documented for this campaign is a voice call impersonating IT support that walks the employee through authorising the attacker's tooling, which is why this record carries `entry/vishing`; Qantas itself did not use the word "vishing", so the specific channel is reported rather than confirmed by the victim.

**Escalation.** None required. The platform the attacker reached was a customer-servicing system that legitimately held the records; once authenticated as the contact-centre operator, bulk reading was an ordinary function of the tool, not a privilege that had to be won.

**Blast radius.** 5.7 million customers: 4 million records held name, email address and Frequent Flyer details; 1.3 million also held addresses; 1.1 million dates of birth; 900,000 phone numbers; 400,000 gender; 10,000 meal preferences. Qantas stated that no Frequent Flyer account credentials, passwords, PINs, financial information or passport details were taken. The airline obtained a court injunction restraining publication of the stolen data.

**Human factor.** A contact-centre agent — often offshore, often on a platform the airline does not itself operate — was reachable by phone, had bulk access to customer records, and had no independent way to verify that the "IT support" caller was real. The control that failed is identity verification on inbound support contact, not anything in the airline's network.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (verify the identity of anyone requesting access or an authorisation step, independent of what the caller claims), HUMAN-05 (training that reflects current voice-impersonation technique, not just email phishing), DATA-11 (bulk export from a customer-servicing platform should be a privileged, logged, alerting action rather than a routine one).

```yaml
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2025/07/20/microsoft-releases-guidance-on-exploitation-of-sharepoint-vulnerability-cve-2025-53770
```

**What happened.** A chain of SharePoint Server flaws — CVE-2025-49706 and CVE-2025-49704, then the patch bypasses CVE-2025-53770 and CVE-2025-53771, collectively nicknamed "ToolShell" — was exploited against internet-facing on-premises SharePoint farms. Microsoft observed exploitation as early as 7 July 2025, published guidance on 19 July, and on 22 July named three actors: the China state-linked Linen Typhoon and Violet Typhoon, and Storm-2603, which from 18 July began deploying Warlock ransomware through modified Group Policy Objects. SharePoint Online was not affected; this was entirely an on-premises problem.

**Entry.** Unauthenticated remote code execution against the SharePoint web front end, achieved by abusing unsafe deserialization of untrusted data. Attackers dropped a web shell — most commonly `spinstall0.aspx`, with filename variants — onto the server to run commands and read server state.

**Escalation.** This is the part that made ToolShell different from an ordinary RCE. The web shell's purpose was not persistence on disk but theft of the server's ASP.NET `machineKey` material — the `validationKey` and `decryptionKey` used to sign and encrypt `__VIEWSTATE`. With those keys an attacker can forge a valid, correctly signed `__VIEWSTATE` payload at will and get code execution again from an ordinary unauthenticated HTTP request. Patching closes the original hole but does nothing about the stolen keys, so a patched server with unrotated keys stays owned. From there, Storm-2603 moved laterally and pushed ransomware via GPO.

**Blast radius.** Never aggregated into a single authoritative count. Microsoft and CISA both described widespread exploitation across government, defence and education operators of on-premises SharePoint; individual victim counts published by vendors during July varied and are not reproduced here. Confirmed outcomes span credential and key theft, document exfiltration consistent with espionage, and Warlock ransomware deployment.

**Human factor.** SharePoint on-premises is classic retained-legacy infrastructure — kept because a migration was never funded, reachable from the internet because that is how staff used it, and patched on an enterprise cadence rather than an internet-facing one. The second failure was procedural: many operators applied the July update and considered themselves finished, without performing the machine-key rotation that the guidance made a mandatory, ordered step.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems measured in days, plus an inventory of what cannot be patched), CLOUD-08 (a document-collaboration server does not need to be published to the open internet), CRED-03 (rotate anything the attacker may have seen — here, rotate ASP.NET machine keys *before* patching, patch, rotate again, and restart IIS, because key rotation is the only step that actually evicts the attacker).

```yaml
  - https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/24/amazon_q_ai_prompt/
```

**What happened.** Someone with no special standing opened a pull request against the open-source `aws-toolkit-vscode` repository on 13 July 2025. It was accepted, and the change rode the normal release pipeline into version 1.84.0 of the Amazon Q Developer extension for Visual Studio Code. The change was not conventional malware: it was a natural-language instruction addressed to the coding agent itself, telling it that its goal was to return the system to a near-factory state — clearing local files and directories with shell commands and deleting AWS resources such as S3 buckets, EC2 instances and IAM users through the AWS CLI, running continuously until finished. AWS published a bulletin on 23 July and shipped 1.85.0.

**Entry.** A pull request from an ordinary GitHub account. AWS's own bulletin identifies the root cause as "an inappropriately scoped GitHub token in their CodeBuild configuration" — the token available to the build had far more repository authority than building an extension requires, so a contribution that reached the build could reach the release.

**Escalation.** The overscoped CodeBuild token is the whole escalation. There was no exploit and no privilege chain: contribution access plus a build credential that could commit and publish equalled publishing authority over an extension installed on developer machines that hold live AWS credentials.

**Blast radius.** AWS states that the malicious code "was distributed with the extension but was unsuccessful in executing due to a syntax error", that no customer services or environments were affected, that 1.84.0 was pulled from distribution channels, and that the credentials were revoked and replaced. AWS did not publish an install count for the affected version, so none is given here. The significance of this record is not its damage but its demonstration: the payload was English prose, the execution engine was an AI agent with the user's own permissions, and it passed code review.

**Human factor.** A maintainer merged an external pull request into a repository whose build held a token strong enough to publish, and the release gate did not treat "text that will be read by an agent as instructions" as executable content. Prompt files, agent rules and system-prompt fragments were reviewed as documentation rather than as code.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-05 (deploy credentials scoped per repository, least-privilege and expiring — the bulletin names the opposite as the cause), CICD-09 and CICD-02 (release approval requiring a second human, with required review on anything that reaches users), AGENT-08 (repository-level agent configuration and prompt content reviewed as executable code, because for an agent that is exactly what it is).

```yaml
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_(app)
```

**What happened.** Tea is a women-only app for sharing safety information about men, and it required new users to submit a selfie and a government-issued ID to prove they were women. Around 25 July 2025 an anonymous poster on 4chan pointed at a Tea Firebase storage bucket that was readable without any authentication, holding roughly 59 GB of data — about 72,000 images, of which approximately 13,000 were the verification selfies and government IDs and about 59,000 were images from posts, comments and messages. Three days later, on 28 July, researcher Kasra Rahjerdi found a second exposure: roughly 1.1 million private direct messages dating from 2023 to that week.

**Entry.** No exploit and no attacker skill. A legacy Firebase storage bucket was configured to allow unauthenticated reads. Tea's own statement describes it as "a legacy data storage system" holding "a dataset from prior to February 2024". The second exposure was different in kind: message history could be retrieved using a user's own API key, meaning the access-control check on message retrieval was effectively absent rather than merely misconfigured.

**Escalation.** None required — a public URL returned the files. The one structural amplifier was the legacy system itself: a store that had been superseded stayed online, stayed populated, and stayed outside whatever controls the current stack had.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 72,000 images including about 13,000 selfies and photographed government IDs — the single most sensitive category a consumer app can hold, since a face plus an ID document is a complete identity-theft and doxxing kit — plus about 1.1 million private messages whose content included discussions of infidelity, abortion, phone numbers and planned meeting locations, and which were linkable to identifiable individuals. Tea took the system offline, said it found no evidence of access to other parts of its environment, offered identity-protection services and notified law enforcement; the messaging feature was withdrawn. Multiple class-action suits followed in August.

**Human factor.** Two decisions made this incident rather than the exposure itself. First, verification documents that existed to answer one question at signup were retained indefinitely rather than checked and deleted. Second, a superseded storage system was left populated and reachable instead of being emptied and decommissioned, because nobody owned the question "what is still running that we stopped using?"

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy buckets specifically audited — this is precisely the legacy-bucket case the control is written for), DATA-07 (identity documents deleted after the verification decision is made, which would have removed the 13,000 worst files from existence), CLOUD-09 (an inventory that surfaces forgotten and unowned assets so a pre-2024 store cannot quietly remain internet-readable).


**What happened.** Through the middle of 2025 a financially motivated cluster that Google's Threat Intelligence Group tracks as UNC6040 ran a voice-phishing campaign against Salesforce customers, and on 5 August 2025 Google disclosed in an update to its own report that one of its corporate Salesforce instances had been among the victims, compromised in June. The pattern repeated across airlines, insurers, retailers and luxury brands through July and August, and extortion demands — attributed to a partner cluster, UNC6240, which calls itself ShinyHunters — arrived months after the theft.

**Entry.** A phone call. The caller impersonated internal IT support, usually to an English-speaking branch of a multinational, and talked the employee through Salesforce's connected-app setup page, having them enter an eight-digit connection code that the attacker supplied. Entering that code authorised the attacker's own build of Salesforce's Data Loader — rebranded so it did not look out of place — into the victim's org. No password was stolen and no MFA prompt was defeated, because the victim was never asked for either: they were asked to grant consent, and they did. GTIG also observed Okta-themed phishing panels used to harvest credentials and MFA codes in parts of the campaign.

**Escalation.** The connected app carried broad API access by design, because Data Loader exists to move bulk records. Exfiltration ran over Mullvad VPN addresses; later in the campaign the actor moved off Data Loader to custom Python scripts, and in some cases used Salesforce accounts compromised at unrelated organisations rather than trial accounts. One case retrieved only about 10% of the target data before detection; others ran small chunked test queries first and then pulled whole tables.

**Blast radius.** No authoritative total was published, and none is invented here. Google's own instance yielded basic, largely public business contact information for small and medium businesses before access was revoked. The same campaign is the frame around record I2025Q3-01 (Qantas, 5.7 million people) and a run of consumer-brand and insurer disclosures through August. Extortion followed months later by email and phone, demanding Bitcoin within 72 hours.

**Human factor.** Authorising a connected application was a self-service action available to ordinary users. The organisation had no gate between "an employee believes IT asked them to do this" and "a third-party application now holds bulk API access to the CRM", and no review of what was already connected.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-03 (authorising a new connected application requires review, not a user's own click — GTIG's mitigation list is effectively this control, restricting "Customize Application" and "Manage Connected Apps" to administrators and allowlisting approved apps), VENDOR-02 (scope, expire and periodically review granted OAuth tokens), DATA-11 and OBSV-02 (bulk export as a privileged, alerting action, with transaction-security policies that fire on large downloads).

**What happened.** Orange Belgium disclosed in late August 2025 that an intruder had reached one of its IT systems in July and accessed data belonging to approximately 850,000 customer accounts. The operator said it blocked access to the affected system on detection, tightened controls, notified the competent authorities and filed a complaint with the judicial authorities, and would contact affected customers by email or SMS. It declined to name the attacker publicly while saying it knew who was responsible, and stated the incident was unrelated to the separate attack on Orange France in July.

Orange Belgium explicitly stated that passwords, email addresses and bank or financial details were not involved. **Human factor.** With the vector undisclosed, the assessable failure is one of concentration: a single system's compromise produced a clean set of SIM-unlock secrets for 850,000 subscribers. Nothing capped how much one system could give up at once, and PUK codes — a credential — were held in recoverable form alongside ordinary customer records.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-12 (cap what a single compromise can reach, in value and in volume, so one system does not yield 850,000 SIM-unlock codes), DATA-07 (treat PUK codes as the credential they are and protect them at rest accordingly, rather than as an ordinary subscriber attribute), OBSV-02 (alert on abnormal read volume per account or credential, since 850,000 records do not leave a subscriber-management system in the course of normal work).


**What happened.** Drift is an AI chat and conversational-marketing product owned by Salesloft. To do its job it held long-lived OAuth tokens into its customers' Salesforce orgs, and in some cases Google Workspace and Slack. An actor Mandiant tracks as UNC6395 had access to Salesloft's GitHub environment from roughly March to June 2025, obtained those stored customer tokens, and then between 8 and 18 August 2025 used them to bulk-export CRM data from more than 700 organisations. Public disclosure landed on 26 August. Named victims include Cloudflare, Google, Palo Alto Networks, Proofpoint, Zscaler, Tanium and SpyCloud.

**Entry.** For the downstream victims, the entry was a valid OAuth token belonging to a trusted, already-authorised integration — authentication, including MFA, was never in the path, because the token *is* the authorisation. For Drift itself, the entry into its GitHub environment has not been publicly explained; what is documented is the access window (March to June) and what was taken from it.

**Escalation.** This is a textbook supply-chain cascade: one vendor's token store equalled standing API access to 700+ customer tenants, each of which had granted a scope broad enough for a chat product to read and write CRM objects. The tokens were long-lived and unexpiring, so an intrusion that ended in June was still monetisable in August. Reconnaissance was methodical — on 9 August a stolen token was tested with TruffleHog set as the user-agent string; on 12 August the actor enumerated Salesforce objects through `/services/data/v58.0/sobjects/`; on 13 and 14 August it pulled Case schema, counted Accounts, Contacts and Users, and probed API limits; on 16 August it ran `SELECT COUNT() FROM Case` to size the pull; on 17 August it exfiltrated in bulk through Salesforce Bulk API 2.0 and then deleted the job logs minutes later.

**Blast radius.** More than 700 organisations, with Case, Contact, Account and Opportunity objects exported — support-ticket text and metadata plus business contact details; file attachments were not taken. The second-order damage is the important part: the actor then searched the exported support tickets for plaintext secrets that customers had pasted into them, specifically AWS access keys, VPN credentials and Snowflake tokens. Cloudflare confirmed that 104 of its API tokens were found in compromised support cases and rotated them between 26 and 29 August. Salesloft revoked all Drift-to-Salesforce connections on 20 August, Salesforce and Salesloft notified affected organisations on 23 August, and Drift was removed from AppExchange.

**Human factor.** Two habits, both ordinary. Customers granted a chat vendor a permanent, broadly scoped token into their system of record and never revisited it. Their own staff and their own customers pasted live AWS keys and VPN credentials into support tickets, turning a CRM into an unindexed secrets store — which is why a marketing-integration breach became a cloud-credential breach.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 and VENDOR-02 (an inventory of every third party holding a token into your systems, with those grants scoped, expiring and periodically re-reviewed — an unexpiring Salesforce grant to a chat widget is the failure in one line), VENDOR-04 (sanitise support artefacts, because secrets in tickets are what turned 700 CRM exports into a cloud-credential incident), CRED-07 (credentials expire; a token stolen in June should not still work in August).

```yaml
  - https://nx.dev/blog/s1ngularity-postmortem
```

**What happened.** On 26 August 2025 an attacker published malicious versions of `nx`, a widely used JavaScript monorepo build system, and of several `@nx/*` plugins. A `postinstall` hook ran a file called `telemetry.js` on Linux and macOS, which harvested credentials and then — for the first time publicly documented — invoked whatever AI coding CLIs it found on the machine and asked *them* to hunt the filesystem for secrets. Results were base64-encoded and pushed to public GitHub repositories created under each victim's own account. Two days later the attacker used the stolen GitHub tokens to flip thousands of private repositories public.

**Entry.** Nx's own post-mortem names three compounding mistakes in one repository. A workflow that validated pull-request titles used the `pull_request_target` trigger, which runs with the *target* branch's permissions — and the repository still carried the pre-2023 default of read-and-write permissions for the `GITHUB_TOKEN`. That workflow echoed the PR title without sanitising it, so shell metacharacters in a title executed. And the publish workflow had `workflow_dispatch` enabled, so it could be triggered through the API. The attacker put shell commands in a pull-request title, extracted a GitHub token, pushed a branch with their own scripts, triggered the publish workflow, and took the npm publish token.

**Escalation.** Two distinct escalations, and the first is the novel one. On victim machines, `telemetry.js` searched for wallets, keystores, `.env` files, SSH private keys, npm credentials and `gh auth token` output — and then ran locally installed AI command-line tools, including the Claude, Gemini and Amazon Q CLIs, with their approval prompts switched off (`--dangerously-skip-permissions`, `--yolo`, `--trust-all-tools`), instructing them to enumerate the filesystem and produce an inventory of sensitive files. The developer's own agent, already trusted and already authorised, performed the reconnaissance; Wiz observed this succeed in hundreds of cases before model-side guardrails refused. Exfiltration went to repositories named `s1ngularity-repository`, `-0` and `-1` under the victim's account, with the payload double- and triple-base64-encoded. The second escalation was ordinary credential reuse: the harvested GitHub tokens were replayed.

**Blast radius.** Phase one ran from about 22:32 UTC on 26 August until npm removed the versions at 02:44 UTC on 27 August, with GitHub disabling the attacker-created repositories around 09:00 UTC. Wiz counted over a thousand valid GitHub tokens, dozens of valid cloud credentials and npm tokens, and roughly twenty thousand further files leaked; counts differ between vendor analyses, which is why this record is medium confidence rather than high. Phase two ran from 16:00 UTC on 28 August to 02:00 UTC on 29 August: using the stolen tokens, automation flipped over 5,500 private repositories to public across more than 400 users and organisations, exposing source code that had never been meant to leave its owner. Affected versions were `nx` 20.9.0, 20.10.0, 20.11.0, 20.12.0, 21.5.0, 21.6.0, 21.7.0 and 21.8.0, plus matching `@nx/devkit`, `@nx/js`, `@nx/node`, `@nx/workspace` and `@nx/eslint` releases and `@nx/enterprise-cloud` and `@nx/key` 3.2.0.

**Human factor.** Three defaults nobody revisited: a permissive workflow token left at the old default, an unquoted variable in a shell step, and a publish pipeline that could be triggered programmatically. Downstream, the decisive habit was running `npm install` with lifecycle scripts enabled on a machine that held cloud credentials and an AI CLI configured to act without asking.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-02 (install scripts disabled by default on developer machines and in CI — the entire victim-side payload was a `postinstall` hook), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before adopting a newly published version, which would have covered the four-hour window almost entirely), CICD-01 and CICD-04 (CI secrets unavailable to untrusted pull-request builds, and pipeline steps with minimal token permissions), AGENT-06 and AGENT-02 (an AI CLI should not be invocable in a mode that skips human approval by any process that can reach a shell, and its credentials should be scoped to its task).


**What happened.** Miljödata is a small Swedish software supplier whose HR products handle medical certificates, rehabilitation cases and work-injury reporting, and which serves roughly 80% of Sweden's municipalities. Over the weekend of 23 August 2025 it was hit by ransomware, and the outage propagated straight into public administration: more than 200 Swedish municipalities, including Skellefteå, Mönsterås and Kalmar, lost access to sick-leave and occupational-health systems. Swedish police officer Helena Renberg said the attackers demanded 1.5 bitcoin. The Swedish Privacy Protection Authority received around 70 incident reports tied to the attack.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. Miljödata's chief executive Erik Hallén said the company was working intensively with external experts to establish what happened; neither the company nor CERT-SE published a vector. Civil Defence Minister Carl-Oskar Bohlin stated publicly that the full extent had not yet been determined.

**Escalation.** A straightforward supply-chain cascade, and the clearest small-vendor example in this quarter: one mid-sized supplier's environment was the single point of failure for a service used by four fifths of a country's municipalities. No lateral movement into the municipalities themselves was needed, because the municipalities' data already lived in the supplier's systems.

**Blast radius.** Over 200 municipalities disrupted, plus regional employers. The categories involved — sick-leave certificates, rehabilitation records and work-injury reports — are health information about public-sector employees, which is why this record carries `impact/phi` even though the victim is not a healthcare organisation. No authoritative count of affected individuals was published at disclosure and none is asserted here. The reach extended past Sweden's public sector: on 25 September 2025 Volvo Group North America disclosed a breach of its own employee data traced to the Miljödata attack.

**Human factor.** Sweden's municipalities collectively concentrated a sensitive HR function in one supplier because it was the sector-standard choice, without anyone owning the question of what happens when that supplier stops. The supplier, for its part, was a small company holding health data on a national scale — a mismatch between the sensitivity of the data and the security budget of the organisation holding it.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data, with the concentration risk made visible — 80% market share in a sensitive function is a national single point of failure, not a procurement convenience), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows and active monitoring of supplier advisories, so 200 municipalities are not all learning the same thing from the press), DATA-09 (data minimisation and enforced retention, since much of what was exposed was historical case material that no longer needed to be online).


**What happened.** Jaguar Land Rover detected an intrusion beginning around 31 August 2025 and shut its own systems down as a containment measure, pausing production from 1 September. Retail and production activity were severely disrupted within days. Global manufacturing stayed halted for roughly five weeks; a restart planned for 24 September slipped, and production only resumed from 8 October, with partial normalisation continuing into late October. A group styling itself "Scattered Lapsus$ Hunters" — a self-declared combination of the Scattered Spider, Lapsus$ and ShinyHunters brands — claimed responsibility on Telegram; that is a claim, not a confirmed attribution, and later reporting pointed in other directions.

**Entry.** Never disclosed. JLR did not publish a vector, and this record does not guess at one. The claimed actor's documented technique in 2025 was help-desk and voice impersonation, but no public source ties that to this intrusion.

**Escalation.** Also undisclosed. What is observable is the scope of the containment decision: JLR took down systems across a global manufacturing and retail estate rather than isolating a segment, and needed five weeks to bring them back. That is consistent with lateral movement across a flat or weakly segmented environment, but it is an inference from the response, not a published finding.

**Blast radius.** Roughly five weeks of halted production at a manufacturer with about 34,000 direct employees and around 120,000 jobs in its supply chain. JLR's own results booked £196 million (about $220 million) in cyber-related costs in the quarter ending 30 September 2025. The UK government issued a £1.5 billion loan guarantee to stabilise the supplier base, and suppliers laid off workers in the hundreds while the plants were dark. The Bank of England attributed part of a Q3 GDP shortfall to the disruption. The Cyber Monitoring Centre estimated the total economic impact to the UK at around £1.9 billion, which would make it the costliest cyber event on UK record — that is a modelled estimate rather than an audited figure, hence this record's medium confidence. JLR first said there was no evidence customer data had been stolen and later confirmed a data breach without specifying the categories.

**Human factor.** The decisive failure is not at the point of entry, which nobody has published, but at the point of recovery: a manufacturer could not separate the compromised estate from the systems that build cars, and could not restore them in less than five weeks. That is a set of choices about segmentation, backup design and rehearsal made long before the attacker arrived.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between tiers and environments, so that containing an intrusion does not require stopping production for five weeks), DATA-08 (backups that exist, are restore-tested and are not writable by production credentials — the constraint on JLR was restoration speed, not detection), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan naming the decider, the communicator and the disclosure clock, since the supplier base and the government were both improvising alongside JLR).
