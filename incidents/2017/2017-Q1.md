# 2017 Q1 — Open Databases, Forged Cookies, Spoofed CEOs

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/public-database` — CloudPets' unauthenticated MongoDB was one visible casualty of a much larger wave of exposed, unauthenticated databases swept and ransomed across the internet this quarter.
> - **New technique this quarter:** a national financial regulator's own website (Poland's KNF) was compromised and turned into a watering hole to reach over 100 banks and financial institutions worldwide.
> - **Recurring failure:** authentication that was absent entirely (CloudPets' database, Cellebrite's legacy backup), bypassable by design (St. Jude's pacemakers), or trivially forged (Yahoo's session cookies) — the pattern was no real check, not merely a weak one.
> - **Records in this file:** 16

sources:

  - https://www.dicardiology.com/article/fda-confirms-cybersecurity-vulnerabilities-st-judes-implantable-cardiac-devices-merlin
```

**What happened.** On January 9, 2017 the FDA confirmed that St. Jude Medical's radio-frequency-enabled implantable cardiac devices (pacemakers and defibrillators) and the Merlin@home bedside transmitter that relays their data contained cybersecurity vulnerabilities that could let a nearby attacker alter how the device functioned. The finding validated allegations security research firm MedSec had made five months earlier in a joint, adversarial disclosure with short-seller Muddy Waters Capital, which had bet against St. Jude's stock. St. Jude, then being acquired by Abbott, shipped a Merlin@home firmware patch the same day the FDA communication issued.

**Entry.** `entry/auth-bypass` — the device's RF pairing and authentication scheme, later tracked as CVE-2017-12712, could be bypassed or forged by a nearby attacker, letting unauthorized commands reach the implanted device without a legitimate credential.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — bypassing the authentication check gave direct command access to the implant itself; the transmitter-to-device link was the target, so there was nothing further to move laterally into.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed patient harm or in-the-wild exploitation was reported at the time of disclosure or since. The vulnerable population was only quantified later, outside this quarter: an August 2017 firmware recall covering roughly 465,000 U.S. devices.

**Human factor.** St. Jude had no clear, published channel for researchers to report this kind of flaw responsibly, which is part of why the finding reached the public through an adversarial, profit-motivated short-seller relationship rather than a coordinated vulnerability-disclosure program.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant, bypass-resistant authentication on every account or channel with production reach — checklist.md is written for human logins, but it is the closest match to a device pairing/authentication scheme with no meaningful check), OBSV-06 (a published way for an outsider to report a vulnerability, which would have let this reach St. Jude through a coordinated channel instead of a short-seller's report).

  - https://iapp.org/news/a/900-gb-of-cellebrite-data-stolen-and-released/
```

**What happened.** An outside hacker exfiltrated roughly 900 GB of data from an external-facing Cellebrite web server and gave a sample to a journalist, becoming public on January 12, 2017. Cellebrite, an Israeli maker of mobile-phone forensic extraction tools widely used by police and intelligence agencies, confirmed unauthorized access to the server but said the exposed material was limited to a legacy backup of my.Cellebrite, its end-user license-management system.

**Entry.** `entry/exposed-service` — the attacker reached an external web server that still held a legacy database backup rather than the current production system; Cellebrite never publicly disclosed how that server itself was initially compromised.

**Escalation.** `escalation/legacy-system` — a deprecated system (the old license-management backend) sat reachable from the internet and bypassed whatever controls protected Cellebrite's current infrastructure; once the server was reached, the backup file on it was directly readable with no further step needed.

**Blast radius.** The archive contained customer usernames and passwords for Cellebrite's licensing portal and technical product data; some reporting also described evidence-related log data from Cellebrite devices, though that characterization came from the attacker and outlets rather than an independently verified Cellebrite accounting. The attacker told journalists the motive was opposition to Western government surveillance policy and initially sought to sell access rather than publish the data in bulk.

**Human factor.** A legacy system stayed reachable from the internet, with customer credentials sitting in a backup file, long after the license-management platform it served should have been decommissioned or had its old data purged.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-09 (an inventory that finds and removes unowned or forgotten internet-facing assets, like an old license-management server) and DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits, so a legacy backup with live customer credentials does not simply persist indefinitely).

  - https://www.securityweek.com/ukraine-power-company-confirms-hackers-caused-outage/

```

**What happened.** On the night of December 17-18, 2016, a cyberattack caused a power outage affecting part of Kyiv and the surrounding region — Ukraine's second confirmed cyberattack on its power grid in as many years, after a similar December 2015 incident. Ukrenergo, the national transmission operator, initially described the event only as a technical fault; more than a month later, on January 18, 2017, it told Reuters its investigation had confirmed the outage was caused by a cyberattack, with the normal operation of workstations and SCADA servers disrupted by malware that gave the intruders remote control.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — Ukrenergo's confirmation described the effect (malware disrupting workstations and SCADA servers through "external influences" that enabled remote control) but did not specify how the attackers first reached the network; the company said it was still tracing potentially infected computers and the source of the breach.

**Escalation.** `escalation/lateral-movement` — whatever the initial foothold, the intrusion reached from ordinary IT workstations into the SCADA servers able to issue commands to substation equipment, the same IT-to-OT path exploited in Ukraine's December 2015 grid attack a year earlier.

**Blast radius.** Power was restored within just over an hour, and no customer or megawatt count for the affected area was published. Ukrainian officials situated the attack within a much larger pattern: President Poroshenko cited roughly 6,500 cyberattacks against Ukrainian state institutions over the preceding two months.

**Human factor.** The same IT-to-OT reach that let 2015's attackers issue substation commands from compromised workstations was still exploitable a year later, and it took Ukrenergo more than a month after the outage to publicly confirm a cyberattack was the cause rather than a technical fault.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between IT and OT tiers, so a compromised workstation cannot reach SCADA servers) and OBSV-03 (detection itself monitored, so confirming a second grid-affecting intrusion in as many years takes days rather than a month).

cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [DATA-04, CLOUD-01]
sources:
  - https://nsfocusglobal.com/elasticsearch-hit-ransom-attack/
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/database-ransom-attacks-hit-couchdb-and-hadoop-servers/
```

**What happened.** Beginning around January 12, 2017 and escalating sharply by January 18, attackers scanned the internet for Elasticsearch clusters left reachable with no authentication, deleted or encrypted their contents, and left a ransom note demanding Bitcoin for their return — the same pattern already sweeping MongoDB installations (including CloudPets, recorded separately in this file) extended to a second widely deployed database technology. Security researcher Niall Merrigan, tracking the campaign in real time, counted roughly 2,700 attacked Elasticsearch servers by January 18; separately, Shodan founder John Matherly and the security firm NSFOCUS each scanned the wider internet and put the number of exposed, at-risk instances far higher, at roughly 35,000 to nearly 40,000, most hosted on Amazon Web Services.

**Entry.** `entry/public-database` — Elasticsearch instances were bound to public IP addresses with no authentication in front of them, so anyone connecting to the port could call the cluster's own API to delete, modify, or query arbitrary data; no credential or exploit was needed.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — the exposed API already granted full read, write, and delete access, so finding an instance was equivalent to owning it.

**Blast radius.** Victims were concentrated in the United States, with further clusters identified in Europe, China (NSFOCUS separately counted roughly 1,956 vulnerable Chinese instances), and Singapore. No single named organization was identified as a victim in the reporting available for this campaign; the pattern was thousands of independently run installations, mostly small operations that had never put authentication in front of a default install, each individually ransomed.

**Human factor.** Each affected operator had deployed Elasticsearch — which bound to a public interface with no authentication by default in the versions then in wide use — without adding a firewall rule, VPN, or access control in front of it, the same root-cause misconfiguration already driving the parallel MongoDB wave that same month.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and search indexes not reachable from the public internet at all) and CLOUD-01 (treating "nothing is publicly reachable unless it was decided to be" as the default, with regular enumeration to catch instances like these before an attacker's scan does).

```

**What happened.** As the MongoDB and Elasticsearch ransom waves continued, attackers extended the same scan-and-strike pattern to two more database technologies in January 2017. An attacker using the handle NODATA4U scanned for internet-facing Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS) installations reachable through an exposed web-based admin interface and worked through them roughly one host per hour, deleting directories and replacing them with a folder named "NODATA4U_SECUREYOURSHIT" — vandalism, with no ransom demand attached. Separately, a group identifying itself as r3l4x wiped exposed CouchDB installations and left a Bitcoin ransom note; researchers tracking both campaigns alongside the MongoDB and Elasticsearch waves counted 124 vandalized Hadoop instances and 443 targeted CouchDB servers by January 18, 2017.

**Entry.** `entry/exposed-service` for the Hadoop installations — Fidelis Cybersecurity Threat Research found attackers scanning specifically for HDFS's unauthenticated web admin panel on port 50070, estimating 8,000-10,000 such installations were potentially exposed worldwide — and `entry/public-database` for CouchDB, which by default likewise accepted unauthenticated connections. Neither required a credential, exploit, or foothold beyond finding the open port.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — both HDFS's admin interface and CouchDB's default configuration granted whoever connected the ability to delete data directly; Fidelis noted that only basic HDFS familiarity was needed once a target was found.

**Blast radius.** At least 124 Hadoop installations had their data destroyed and replaced with the attacker's vandalism marker, and 443 CouchDB servers were wiped with a 0.2 BTC ransom demand attached, by the time researchers' running counts were published on January 18, 2017; Fidelis's estimate of the total exposed Hadoop population (8,000-10,000 installations) suggests the damage could have grown far larger had the campaign continued at the same pace. No victim organizations were named in the reporting available.

**Human factor.** Operators of both technologies had deployed them with internet-facing defaults — an open admin panel for Hadoop, no authentication for CouchDB — that the MongoDB and Elasticsearch waves earlier the same month had already shown were being actively scanned for and exploited, yet thousands of installations were still unprotected when the campaign reached these two technologies in turn.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and search indexes not reachable from the public internet) and CLOUD-08 (admin interfaces, like HDFS's web panel, kept off the public internet entirely).

factor: [factor/weak-auth, factor/detection-failure]

cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [AUTH-01, CRED-07, OBSV-01]
sources:
  - https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/unit42-second-wave-shamoon-2-attacks-identified/
  - https://www.theregister.com/2017/01/26/shamoon_2_hits_saudi_arabian_targets/
```

**What happened.** A new variant of the Shamoon disk-wiping malware (Shamoon 2, also called Disttrack V2) hit Saudi Arabian government and private-sector networks in three waves between November 2016 and January 2017, overwriting master boot records and files and rendering machines unbootable — echoing the original 2012 Saudi Aramco attack. Reporting and attribution pointed to an Iran-linked actor, possibly APT33 or a group imitating it, though Palo Alto Networks and other researchers stopped short of firm attribution.

**Entry.** `entry/default-credentials` (closest documented match) — Palo Alto Networks stated it had no details of whatever intrusion preceded each wiper deployment, so the true initial foothold is undisclosed. What is documented is that the malware carried hardcoded, organization-specific account credentials, including, in the second wave, default usernames and passwords lifted from Huawei's published virtual-desktop-infrastructure documentation — meaning at least one target had never changed a vendor-default password on its own VDI.

**Escalation.** `escalation/credential-reuse` and `escalation/lateral-movement` — the payload logged into new hosts using stolen, legitimate domain credentials, copied itself across the network, and created a scheduled task so every infected machine detonated in unison, turning ordinary admin tooling into a self-spreading wiper rather than requiring any further privilege-escalation technique.

**Blast radius.** Thousands of workstations were wiped across the affected organizations. Vendor reporting on how many organizations were hit varies considerably — from a handful specifically named up to more than 20 across the full campaign — and no single authoritative aggregate count was ever published; some victims reported days of operational disruption while machines were rebuilt.

**Human factor.** Whatever intrusion harvested the domain credentials used in the attack went undetected long enough for the operators to return weeks later with a wiper, and at least one target had left a documented vendor-default password unchanged on infrastructure with reach across the network.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on accounts with production reach, so a harvested or default password is not enough on its own), CRED-07 (credentials that expire rather than remain valid indefinitely, limiting how long earlier-stolen credentials stay useful), and OBSV-01 (centrally logging authentication and admin actions, so reuse of legitimate domain credentials across many hosts does not go unnoticed for weeks).

  - https://thehill.com/policy/cybersecurity/321973-32-million-yahoo-accounts-hacked-in-forged-cookie-row/
  - https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001011006/000119312517241304/d417550dex991.htm
```

**What happened.** Yahoo disclosed that, separate from the roughly one billion and 500 million accounts covered by its 2016 breach announcements, an attacker had used forged authentication cookies to access approximately 32 million accounts directly, without a username or password, during 2015 and 2016. Yahoo began individually notifying affected account holders around February 2017 and confirmed the finding in its 2016 Form 10-K filed March 1, 2017. The U.S. Justice Department's March 15, 2017 indictment of two Russian FSB officers and two criminal hackers they worked with tied the underlying access back to the same 2014 intrusion that had exposed Yahoo's user database and proprietary source code.

**Entry.** `entry/session-theft` — the attacker did not use Yahoo's authentication flow at all; a forged session cookie, minted using knowledge of Yahoo's own proprietary cookie-generation code, was presented directly to Yahoo's systems and accepted as proof of an already-authenticated session.

**Escalation.** `escalation/token-forgery` — once the attacker could mint a valid-looking cookie for any target account, they had the same access a real login would have granted, repeatable at will across the roughly 32 million accounts.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 32 million Yahoo accounts had at least one forged-cookie access event across 2015-2016. Yahoo described the figure itself as an estimate from forensic investigation ("approximately," "believe"), not a fully enumerated confirmed count.

**Human factor.** The capability to forge valid session cookies traced back to source code the original 2014 intruders had already reached; that exposure was not treated as requiring the cookie-signing mechanism itself to be rotated or invalidated, letting forged cookies keep working for roughly two more years.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-04 (sessions that are short-lived, revocable, and rotated on privilege change, rather than a cookie that keeps working for years) and CRED-03 (rotating any secret material — including a cookie-signing mechanism — the moment source code that touches it is known to have been exposed to an intruder).

**What happened.** In February 2017, India's Unique Identification Authority (UIDAI), which runs the Aadhaar biometric identity system covering over a billion residents, filed a criminal police complaint against three private-sector partners — Axis Bank, a major private bank; Suvidhaa Infoserve, a payments services company; and eMudhra, a digital-signature certification agency — after confirming they had illegally accessed the Aadhaar database, stored residents' personal data without authorization, and used it to impersonate those individuals in later authentication attempts.

**Entry.** `entry/third-party-access` — all three companies held legitimate, scoped access to Aadhaar's authentication service as registered partners, needed to verify customers' identity for banking, payments, and digital-signature services; the violation was not a technical intrusion but retaining and replaying that access beyond its authorized, per-transaction purpose.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-02 (partner access to an identity or authentication service scoped, expiring, and reviewed, rather than trusted to self-limit) and DATA-09 (enforced retention limits and data minimization at the point of integration, so a partner's systems cannot simply keep what they were only authorized to check).

```

**What happened.** Starting in January 2017 and accelerating through the U.S. tax-filing season, criminals ran a nationwide campaign spoofing company executives' email addresses to ask payroll or HR staff for copies of every employee's Form W-2 — names, Social Security numbers, wages, addresses — and, in many cases, following up with a second spoofed email from the same "executive" requesting a wire transfer. The IRS issued an unusual nationwide consumer alert on February 2, 2017 (IR-2017-20), calling it "one of the most dangerous email phishing scams" it had seen and noting that organizations hit in 2016 were being targeted again.

**Entry.** `entry/spear-phishing` — a spoofed or look-alike email address impersonating a real CEO or senior executive, sent directly to an employee in payroll or HR with the authority to export W-2 data; no malware or credential theft was needed to get the data out.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — the entire mechanism is a human decision to reply with an attachment or approve a wire transfer; once that happens, the scheme has succeeded with no technical step of any kind.

**Blast radius.** Confirmed victims that quarter spanned school districts (more than 20 by one K-12 cybersecurity tracker's count), religious broadcaster EWTN Global Catholic Network, financial-services company Moneytree, and hospitals, restaurant chains, staffing agencies, and tribal organizations elsewhere. Full W-2 batches — SSNs and wages for entire workforces — went to attackers in each case, and stolen W-2 data was reported selling for $4–$20 per record on dark-web markets. No aggregate financial loss for this specific wave was ever centrally tallied; the FBI's broader running total for CEO-fraud/BEC losses stood at roughly $3.1 billion across more than 22,000 victims as of mid-2016, for context on the surrounding pattern.

**Human factor.** A single employee, believing a real executive was asking, emailed sensitive payroll data or authorized a wire transfer without verifying the request through a second channel.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-10 (recipients and destinations confirmed before sensitive data leaves the organization), HUMAN-02 (high-impact actions, like releasing bulk employee tax data or wiring funds, require a second person), and HUMAN-05 (training that reflects current technique, including this specific W-2-plus-wire-fraud combination rather than generic phishing awareness).

**What happened.** Around October 7, 2016, attackers modified a JavaScript file on the website of Poland's Financial Supervision Authority (KNF), the country's banking regulator, turning a site every Polish bank's staff routinely visited into a watering hole. Polish security site badcyber.com went public on February 3, 2017; researchers at BAE Systems and Symantec followed within two weeks, tying the tooling to infrastructure used more broadly against banks and other financial institutions well beyond Poland, and to malware previously associated with North Korea's Lazarus Group.

**Human factor.** Bank IT teams extended implicit trust to a government regulator's website and had not ensured every workstation's browser plugins were current on a fix that had already existed for over a year.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (managed, patched endpoints — checklist.md frames this as developer machines, but the same discipline applied to every workstation's browser plugins would have closed a year-old hole) and OBSV-01 (centrally logging authentication and admin actions, which is how the post-infection reconnaissance commands were ultimately identified).

### I2017Q1-13 · Cloudbleed — Cloudflare reverse-proxy memory disclosure

```yaml
id: I2017Q1-13
name: Cloudbleed — Cloudflare reverse-proxy memory disclosure
org: Cloudflare (and its customer websites)
date_occurred: 2016-09-22
date_disclosed: 2017-02-23
region: global
sector: [sector/tech, sector/saas]
actor: [actor/researcher]
entry: [entry/unauth-api]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/credentials, impact/pii]

tech: [tech/cdn]
scale: undisclosed (bug triggered 1,242,071 times; ~150 customers' data confirmed in 80,000+ search-engine-cached pages)

dwell_days: 149
confidence: high
controls: [INPUT-02]
sources:
  - https://blog.cloudflare.com/quantifying-the-impact-of-cloudbleed/
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloudbleed
```

**What happened.** A parsing bug in Cloudflare's reverse-proxy edge servers occasionally read past the end of a memory buffer and pasted the adjacent, uninitialized memory — which could hold another customer's private data — into the HTML of an unrelated page. Google Project Zero researcher Tavis Ormandy found live examples of this leaked data (including what looked like authentication tokens and private messages) around February 17-18, 2017, reported it to Cloudflare, which disabled the responsible features within hours and published a full technical account on February 23-24.

**Entry.** `entry/unauth-api` (closest available tag; TAGS.md has no dedicated tag for a memory-safety parser bug) — no credential or exploitation technique was needed by any attacker. Any ordinary HTTP request to a page using one of three affected Cloudflare features (email obfuscation, Server-Side Excludes, Automatic HTTP Rewrites) could, at random, trigger the parser to over-read and leak whatever was in adjacent memory, with no authentication gating what a requester could see.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — the bug directly placed another customer's memory contents into the HTTP response body; there was no foothold to expand from.

**Blast radius.** Cloudflare calculated the flawed logic was triggered 1,242,071 times between September 22, 2016 and February 18, 2017. The company could confirm only a sample of real-world impact: roughly 150 customers' leaked data was found among more than 80,000 unique pages cached by search engines, which Cloudflare worked with Google, Bing, Yahoo, and others to purge. Cloudflare explicitly declined to rule out that passwords, health records, Social Security numbers, or private encryption keys had been exposed at some point during the nearly five-month window, since most triggered leaks were never captured or reviewed by anyone.

**Human factor.** A new HTML-parsing feature shipped without the kind of memory-safety testing — fuzzing, bounds checking — that would have caught a buffer over-read before it reached production traffic serving a large share of the web.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-02 (server-side validation of what a parser is allowed to touch and return — checklist.md has no control specific to fuzzing or memory-safety-testing parser code before shipping it, and INPUT-02 is the closest existing match to that gap).

**What happened.** CloudPets, internet-connected stuffed animals made by Spiral Toys that let family members exchange recorded voice messages with a child through the toy, stored its entire user database in a MongoDB instance with no authentication required and no firewall restricting who could connect. Security researcher Troy Hunt detailed the exposure on February 27, 2017, after learning the database — and a separate, similarly open Amazon S3 bucket holding the actual voice recordings — had already been found, copied, and in at least three cases wiped and replaced with a ransom demand by opportunistic attackers before he ever looked at it, part of a broader wave of attacks against unauthenticated MongoDB instances that had been sweeping the internet since late December 2016.

**Blast radius.** Account records for more than 820,000 registered CloudPets users were exposed, including email addresses and weakly protected password data, alongside more than two million recorded voice messages — many of them children's — stored in the open S3 bucket.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases not reachable from the public internet at all), DATA-03 (object storage private by default, which would have covered the S3 bucket holding the recordings), and AUTH-06 (rate limiting and breached-password checks, which would have limited how useful the weakly protected account passwords were even if the database itself had required a login).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Cloudflare published a detailed public post-mortem within days of Tavis Ormandy's report, coordinated with Google, Bing, Yahoo, and other search engines to purge more than 80,000 cached pages that had captured leaked memory, and disabled the responsible parsing features — a widely cited model for fast, transparent incident handling that renewed industry scrutiny of memory-safety in edge/CDN parsing code.
- The FDA's January 9, 2017 safety communication and St. Jude Medical's same-day Merlin@home firmware patch was one of the FDA's first device-specific cybersecurity actions for implanted medical hardware, foreshadowing the formal recall and broader postmarket medical-device cybersecurity guidance that followed later in 2017.
- The IRS's February 2, 2017 nationwide alert (IR-2017-20) gave employers a dedicated address (phishing@irs.gov) to forward W-2 phishing emails and explicitly recommended verbal, second-channel verification before honoring any executive request for bulk employee tax data.
- The Justice Department's March 15, 2017 indictment of two Russian FSB officers and two criminal hackers over the Yahoo intrusions was the first U.S. criminal cyber case against Russian government officials, and named cookie forgery as chargeable conduct rather than an unattributable technique.
- A wave of ransomed, unauthenticated MongoDB (and, soon after, Elasticsearch) instances that began in late December 2016 and was still growing through this quarter — of which CloudPets was one high-profile casualty — pushed MongoDB Inc. and cloud providers toward more aggressive secure-by-default warnings for self-hosted deployments.
- Microsoft's Silverlight patch (MS16-006), already a year old by the time the KNF watering-hole campaign exploited the same flaw, underscored the industry's accelerating move to deprecate browser plugins entirely rather than rely on every organization to keep them current.
