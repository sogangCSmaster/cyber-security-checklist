# 2017 Q3 — Equifax, CCleaner, and unpatched trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/unpatched-cve` — a patch that had existed for months went unapplied on an internet-facing system (Equifax's Apache Struts flaw), the same vulnerability class being mass-exploited elsewhere that quarter.
> - **New technique this quarter:** `entry/supply-chain-build` — CCleaner's own official, signed updater became the delivery mechanism, reaching 2.27 million machines before selectively backdooring roughly 40 chosen targets.
> - **Recurring failure:** detection depended on someone outside the victim organization — a researcher, a card network, a lapsed certificate's renewal, a business partner's tip — far more often than on the victim's own monitoring.
> - **Records in this file:** 18

```yaml
  - https://securityaffairs.com/60702/cyber-crime/bithumb-hacked.html
  - https://www.esecurityplanet.com/endpoint/byod-blamed-for-massive-cryptocurrency-breach/
  - https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/major-cryptocurrency-exchange-hacked-customers-bitcoin-and-ethereum-accounts-plundered
```

**What happened.** South Korean cryptocurrency exchange Bithumb confirmed around July 5, 2017 that an employee's home personal computer — not Bithumb's own servers or network — had been compromised, exposing personal data for roughly 31,800 customers (about 3% of its user base at the time): names, email addresses, and mobile numbers. Passwords were not among the exposed fields. In the days that followed, attackers used the leaked contact details to target individual customers with follow-up phishing and voice-phishing calls impersonating Bithumb, tricking some into revealing one-time transaction passwords; victim reports of cryptocurrency drained this way ranged from roughly 10 million to over 1 billion Korean won per account, figures that were never reconciled into an official aggregate loss total.

**Entry.** `entry/stolen-credentials` (closest fit — the exact malware or technique used against the machine was never publicly detailed) — the compromised computer belonged to an employee but was not part of Bithumb's own IT infrastructure; a personal device that had access to a customer-data export was infected and its contents taken.

**Escalation.** None required. The employee's PC apparently already held a working set of customer contact records, so no further movement inside Bithumb's network was needed — because that machine was never inside Bithumb's network to begin with.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 31,800 customers' contact details reached the attacker directly. The larger, harder-to-measure damage came second-hand: attackers weaponized that contact list in follow-on phishing and vishing calls against Bithumb customers, and a number of victims — reported by South Korean media as around 100 accounts — lost cryptocurrency this way, though Bithumb never published a reconciled total and attributed some losses to customers' own reuse of transaction passwords.

**Human factor.** Customer contact data was reachable from an employee's personal, presumably less-controlled home computer rather than only from managed corporate systems, and once that data was stolen, ordinary customers had no way to distinguish Bithumb's real communications from an attacker who now had their name, email, and phone number to work with.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-12 (cap what a single compromised device can reach, in value and volume — a personal computer should not have been able to hold tens of thousands of live customer records), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on customer accounts, so a phished one-time password alone couldn't drain funds), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal export volume, which should have flagged a personal machine pulling a bulk customer list in the first place).


**What happened.** On July 9, 2017, an independent website called magicapk.com began letting anyone type in an Indian Reliance Jio mobile number and retrieve that subscriber's first and last name, email address, SIM activation date and circle, and — the site claimed — their Aadhaar national ID number. Multiple journalists tested numbers they controlled and got back real, matching personal details, while other queries returned nothing, an inconsistency neither side ever resolved. Jio, then India's largest mobile carrier with over 120 million subscribers, publicly called the leaked data "unauthentic" and denied any breach of its own systems; the hosting provider suspended magicapk.com the same night, and Jio filed a police report the next day.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — no investigation, journalist, or regulator ever established how magicapk.com's operator obtained the underlying subscriber records, and Jio disputed that the data came from its systems at all. Consistent with the rule against guessing an entry vector, this is recorded as unknown rather than attributed to a specific mechanism.

**Escalation.** None required, as observed. Whatever the data's origin, the exposure mechanism itself required no escalation: it was a public web form that returned full subscriber records the moment any 10-digit Jio number was entered.

**Blast radius.** Unclear and disputed. The site's design implied any of Jio's then-120-million subscriber base could be looked up, but no reliable count exists of how many records were actually queried, scraped, or viewed before the site was taken down within hours of the story breaking. The presence of Aadhaar numbers in some results was particularly sensitive, since Aadhaar is linked to banking, tax, and welfare systems across India.

**Human factor.** Whether the fault lay with Jio's own systems, a retailer or point-of-sale partner used for SIM registration and Aadhaar-linked know-your-customer checks, or another intermediary, some process handling subscriber identity data left it reachable by an outside party well enough to power a working public lookup tool — and the dispute over authenticity itself shows nobody, including Jio, could point to a clean audit trail proving where the data could or could not have come from.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data — the core failure, regardless of which system originated it), INPUT-09 (identifiers like a 10-digit phone number are inherently guessable and enumerable, so unguessability alone was never a valid control), DATA-04 (databases and lookup indexes holding subscriber PII should not be reachable from the public internet without authentication, wherever in the vendor chain that index actually lived).


**What happened.** UpGuard researcher Chris Vickery found an Amazon S3 bucket, owned and run by NICE Systems — an Israel-based vendor that handles Verizon call-center operations — configured to allow public, unauthenticated download. The bucket held months of Verizon customer service logs: names, addresses, account numbers, and account PINs. Vickery notified Verizon on June 13, 2017; the bucket wasn't secured until around June 22. UpGuard published its findings July 12; UpGuard estimated up to 14 million records were exposed, while Verizon said the true figure was 6 million.

**Entry.** `entry/public-storage` — the bucket required no authentication and was reachable at its AWS URL; anyone who found or guessed the address could list and download its full contents.

**Blast radius.** Between 6 and 14 million Verizon customer accounts — the two organizations never reconciled the figure. Data sat exposed for at least nine days between notification and remediation, and for an unknown period before Vickery found it.

**Human factor.** A vendor handling Verizon's call-center operations set a bucket containing live customer data to public instead of private, and no routine review by either company caught it before an outside researcher did.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage private by default, legacy buckets audited), CLOUD-01 (enumerate what's publicly reachable on a schedule), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data, so its controls get audited too).

```yaml
  - https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2017-07-24/swedish-government-tries-to-stem-damage-done-by-security-breach
  - https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450423272/Big-data-means-big-risk-Swedish-Transport-Agency-leak-shows
```

**What happened.** Swedish press revealed on and after July 17, 2017 that Transportstyrelsen, the government agency responsible for Sweden's vehicle and driving-license registers, had mishandled its own outsourcing of IT operations to IBM under an April 2015 contract. The agency uploaded its full databases — vehicle registrations, driving-license photos and details, and government fleet and road data, including records tied to fighter pilots, police informants, people in witness protection, and military and police vehicles — to IBM's cloud, then distributed cleartext copies by email to IBM subcontractor staff in the Czech Republic and to a Serbian firm handling network and firewall maintenance, none of whom had undergone Swedish security-clearance vetting. The story triggered a political crisis: government ministers faced no-confidence motions and two ultimately resigned or were forced out, and the agency's former director-general, already dismissed in January 2017, was separately fined for mishandling classified information.

**Entry.** `entry/inadvertent-disclosure` — the agency itself uploaded and emailed the data to parties who should not have had unrestricted access; `entry/third-party-access` — the outsourcing arrangement itself granted a foreign vendor's unvetted staff standing access to the full database, independent of any single email.

**Escalation.** None required. The data was handed over directly, in cleartext, to people already outside any security-clearance process — there was no technical barrier left to overcome once the agency itself distributed it.

**Blast radius.** No precise figure was ever published, but reporting consistently describes the exposure as covering the driving-license and vehicle data of a large share of the Swedish population, plus sensitive government, police, and military records — with no confirmed report of any specific foreign intelligence service or criminal actor having exploited the access. The documented damage is the exposure and its political fallout, not a confirmed follow-on attack.

**Human factor.** A government agency chose to outsource sensitive national-security-adjacent data processing as a cost-saving measure, then bypassed its own legally required security-clearance process for the foreign contractor staff who would handle it, and normalized emailing cleartext extracts of the database to make the arrangement work operationally — a chain of individually rational shortcuts that collectively discarded the country's own classification rules.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-08 (vendor access time-boxed and separately monitored, rather than standing, unrestricted database access granted as a matter of course), HUMAN-04 (contractors and subprocessors held to the same vetting and access controls as employees — explicitly the control that was skipped here), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data, which would have surfaced that unvetted foreign staff held a live copy of the crown-jewel registries).


**What happened.** Taringa! — an Argentina-based social network and content-sharing site widely described as "the Latin American Reddit," with tens of millions of mostly Spanish-speaking users across Latin America — had its user database compromised, dated by its own breach disclosure to August 2017. Roughly 28.7 million accounts were exposed, including usernames, email addresses, and passwords hashed with MD5, an algorithm fast enough that attackers were able to crack a large share of them back to plaintext soon after the dump began circulating.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — neither Taringa's own disclosure nor available reporting specifies the technical vector used to pull the user database; no CVE, credential, or endpoint was publicly named.


**What happened.** French security researcher Benkow found a Netherlands-hosted server, left with directory listing enabled, that powered a large-scale spam operation dubbed "Onliner." Working with researcher Troy Hunt, he catalogued roughly 711 million records on the server, ranging from plain scraped email address lists to email-and-password combinations recycled from earlier breaches (including LinkedIn's), up to files pairing an email, a password, and a working SMTP server hostname and port — meaning some of these were live, functioning credentials for real organizations' outbound mail servers, not just old leaked passwords. Hunt published his analysis on August 29, 2017 and loaded the data into Have I Been Pwned, while deliberately withholding which specific organizations' mail servers were among those compromised, to avoid amplifying the abuse before operators could be notified and act.

**Entry.** `entry/credential-stuffing` and `entry/credential-reuse` — the spam operators appear to have built their working SMTP-credential list by testing email/password combinations harvested from unrelated prior breaches against mail servers until a working login was found; anyone who reused a breached password on their mail account's SMTP login handed the operators a working relay.

**Escalation.** None required. A valid SMTP username and password is itself the full capability needed to send mail through that server — there is no further privilege to escalate to.

**Blast radius.** 711 million total records, the large majority being plain scraped or recycled email/password lists used to seed spam target lists rather than proof of a fresh compromise. The more serious subset — described by Hunt as ranging from just over 3,000 to as many as 142,000 records in individual files — consisted of full working SMTP credentials, meaning real mail infrastructure at an unknown number of organizations worldwide was actively being used to relay spam without their knowledge at the time of discovery.

**Human factor.** None identified at the level of a single decision; the record exists because of an accumulation of ordinary password reuse across millions of individual accounts over years, which gave criminal operators a large enough haystack to find working mail-server logins purely by testing recycled credentials at scale.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-06 (rate limiting, lockout, and breached-password checks on all authentication surfaces, including SMTP login — not just customer-facing web logins), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal outbound mail volume per account, which would flag a hijacked SMTP credential being used for bulk spam), CRED-07 (credentials that expire rather than remaining valid indefinitely, so a password breached years earlier eventually stops being useful against a live mail server).

**What happened.** UK secondhand electronics retailer CeX disclosed on August 30, 2017 that attackers had stolen a database of roughly two million WeBuy website customer accounts — names, addresses, emails, phone numbers. ```yaml

```yaml
  - https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/litigation-releases/lr-24541
  - https://news.apache.org/foundation/entry/media-alert-the-apache-software
```

**What happened.** Attackers exploited an unpatched Apache Struts vulnerability (CVE-2017-5638) in Equifax's online dispute-portal application starting May 13, 2017, ultimately reaching the personal data of about 147 million Americans (an initial disclosure of 143 million was revised upward over the following year), plus 15.2 million UK residents and roughly 19,000 Canadians. Equifax discovered the intrusion on July 29, 2017 and disclosed it publicly on September 7 — 76 days after access began. The Apache Software Foundation itself publicly confirmed that same month that the root cause was Equifax's failure to apply a patch that had been available since March 7, 2017.

**Entry.** `entry/unpatched-cve` — the Struts patch had been available for over two months, and an internal Equifax email on March 9 directed administrators to apply it. It was never applied to the internet-facing Automated Consumer Interview System, giving attackers remote code execution on that server.

**Escalation.** From that single web application, attackers found plaintext usernames and passwords stored on internal systems, which — combined with the absence of network segmentation between the dispute portal and Equifax's broader environment — let them reach 51 unrelated databases and query them repeatedly over 76 days while blending in as authorized users. Traffic inspection that might have caught the exfiltration earlier was blind for months because the certificate on the device used to decrypt and inspect that network segment's traffic had lapsed and gone unrenewed; only an unrelated certificate renewal on July 29 restored visibility and surfaced the activity immediately.

**Blast radius.** Names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth, addresses, and in many cases driver's license numbers for roughly 147 million people; credit card numbers for about 209,000 consumers. The 2019 FTC/CFPB/state settlement required Equifax to pay a minimum of $575 million, rising to $700 million depending on claims volume, with $300 million earmarked for victim compensation; the UK Financial Conduct Authority separately fined Equifax's UK subsidiary £11.16 million.

**Human factor.** Equifax's patch process "relied on an honour system, without strict enforcement," per the House Oversight Committee's December 2018 report, and a 2015 internal audit had already flagged gaps that were not fully remediated. Separately, three executives sold roughly $1.8 million in Equifax stock in the days after the breach was found internally but before it was disclosed; the SEC later charged former CIO Jun Ying with insider trading for exercising and selling stock ahead of the announcement, and he was convicted in 2019. The public disclosure itself compounded the damage: Equifax stood up a separate, confusing response domain, an official company Twitter account mistakenly linked to a phishing lookalike of that site, and the company initially required an arbitration waiver from consumers signing up for free credit monitoring before reversing course under public pressure.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (dependency/CVE scanning in CI that fails the build on known-exploited vulnerabilities, so an unpatched Struts instance doesn't run in production for two months), CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between tiers, so one web application's compromise doesn't reach 51 unrelated databases), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime rather than sitting in plaintext on reachable systems), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a stated disclosure clock, so 76 days of undetected access doesn't become two more months of public silence).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The Apache Software Foundation publicly confirmed that Equifax's breach was caused by failure to apply the CVE-2017-5638 patch it had shipped in March, redirecting industry attention toward patch-management discipline rather than treating Struts as an isolated Equifax failure.
- The US House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, jointly with the House Science, Space and Technology Committee, opened a formal investigation into Equifax on September 14, 2017 — the start of a review that produced a detailed public report on the failure chain in December 2018.
- SEC Chairman Jay Clayton issued a public "Statement on Cybersecurity" on September 20, 2017 alongside the EDGAR disclosure, committing the agency to enhanced internal risk assessment of its own systems — a regulator publicly acknowledging its own security debt.
- Avast/Piriform's response to the CCleaner compromise — a clean 5.34 build shipped within days of Talos's disclosure, cooperation with law enforcement to take down the attackers' command-and-control server, and a full public technical write-up — became a widely cited model for transparent supply-chain incident handling.
- A cluster of exposed AWS S3 buckets that quarter (Verizon/NICE Systems in July, and similar incidents at other large companies) sharpened industry pressure on cloud customers to actively audit default-public storage configurations rather than assume provider defaults were safe.
- Equifax's initial requirement that consumers waive their right to sue in order to use its free credit-monitoring offer drew immediate public and congressional backlash and was withdrawn within days, feeding a broader post-breach push toward no-strings-attached identity-monitoring remedies.
