# 2017 Q3 — Equifax, CCleaner, and unpatched trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/unpatched-cve` — a patch that had existed for months went unapplied on an internet-facing system (Equifax's Apache Struts flaw), the same vulnerability class being mass-exploited elsewhere that quarter.
> - **New technique this quarter:** `entry/supply-chain-build` — CCleaner's own official, signed updater became the delivery mechanism, reaching 2.27 million machines before selectively backdooring roughly 40 chosen targets.
> - **Recurring failure:** detection depended on someone outside the victim organization — a researcher, a card network, a lapsed certificate's renewal, a business partner's tip — far more often than on the victim's own monitoring.
> - **Records in this file:** 11

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.upguard.com/breaches/verizon-cloud-leak
  - https://www.theregister.com/2017/07/12/14m_verizon_customers_details_out/
```

**What happened.** UpGuard researcher Chris Vickery found an Amazon S3 bucket, owned and run by NICE Systems — an Israel-based vendor that handles Verizon call-center operations — configured to allow public, unauthenticated download. The bucket held months of Verizon customer service logs: names, addresses, account numbers, and account PINs. Vickery notified Verizon on June 13, 2017; the bucket wasn't secured until around June 22. UpGuard published its findings July 12; UpGuard estimated up to 14 million records were exposed, while Verizon said the true figure was 6 million.

**Entry.** `entry/public-storage` — the bucket required no authentication and was reachable at its AWS URL; anyone who found or guessed the address could list and download its full contents.

**Escalation.** None required. The bucket's configuration already granted read access to the entire object store, so no further privilege escalation or lateral movement was needed to reach the data.

**Blast radius.** Between 6 and 14 million Verizon customer accounts — the two organizations never reconciled the figure. Data sat exposed for at least nine days between notification and remediation, and for an unknown period before Vickery found it.

**Human factor.** A vendor handling Verizon's call-center operations set a bucket containing live customer data to public instead of private, and no routine review by either company caught it before an outside researcher did.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage private by default, legacy buckets audited), CLOUD-01 (enumerate what's publicly reachable on a schedule), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data, so its controls get audited too).

**What happened.** Between January 6 and March 11, 2017, an employee on Bupa Global's Partnership Advisory Team in Brighton, UK used his legitimate access to the SWAN customer relationship management system to extract records for 547,000 international health insurance customers, then offered the data for sale on the dark-web marketplace AlphaBay. An external partner alerted Bupa on June 16, 2017 that the data was for sale; Bupa disclosed the incident to customers and press in mid-July.

**Entry.** `entry/insider` — the employee was one of 20 people on a team whose normal duties included running unrestricted reports against the full SWAN database.

**Escalation.** None required. The employee already had every permission needed to query and export the data; the theft was misuse of a standing, legitimate account rather than a technical exploit.

**Blast radius.** 547,000 customer records (names, dates of birth, email addresses, nationalities — Bupa said no financial or medical detail was included) reached a criminal marketplace. The employee was dismissed and a UK arrest warrant was issued. The UK Information Commissioner's Office later fined Bupa £175,000 (about $228,000) for the underlying control failures.

**Human factor.** A 20-person team's day-to-day job required broad, unmonitored access to bulk-export the full customer database, and nobody noticed the extraction until a business partner spotted the data for sale five months later.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-11 (bulk export as a privileged, logged, alerting action), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read/export volume per account), HUMAN-09 (insider risk monitored by behavior, not by trust).

**What happened.** HBO confirmed on July 31, 2017 that attackers had stolen roughly 1.5 terabytes of internal data, including unreleased episodes of Ballers and Room 104, a script for an upcoming Game of Thrones episode, and internal documents and emails. An attacker using the name "Mr. Smith" demanded a multimillion-dollar bitcoin payment to stop further leaks; HBO never confirmed paying a ransom, and leaked material continued to surface for months afterward.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — HBO and investigators never publicly confirmed the initial access mechanism. The attacker claimed, in a video message to HBO's CEO, to have spent about six months penetrating the network through "multiple points of entry." That is an attacker's own claim, not an independently confirmed fact, and should be read accordingly.

**Human factor.** The identifiable failure is one of detection, not of a single mistake: whatever let an intruder operate inside HBO's network for months (by the attacker's own unverified account) before terabytes of data were exfiltrated was a monitoring gap that HBO has never detailed publicly.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centrally log authentication, authorization failures and admin actions, so months of internal access leaves a trail), OBSV-02 (alert on abnormal read/export volume), DATA-11 (treat bulk data export as a privileged, logged, alerting action).


**What happened.** Instagram confirmed on August 30, 2017 that it had patched a bug in its mobile API's account-recovery flow that returned the email address and phone number tied to any account — including high-profile ones — without verifying the requester owned that account. Before the fix, at least one attacker used the flaw to scrape contact data at scale; within days a seller calling itself "Doxagram" began offering lookups for $10 in bitcoin per celebrity record, or $5,000 for a claimed database of over 6 million accounts, including politicians, athletes, and media figures. Singer Selena Gomez's account was among those affected.

**Entry.** `entry/idor` — the password-reset API endpoint returned another account's contact details when queried by identifier, with no check that the requester was authorized to see that account's data.

**Escalation.** None required. The flaw directly returned the target data; no further access or movement was needed.

**Blast radius.** Instagram confirmed only that the bug existed and was fixed. The headline figure — over 6 million accounts — comes from the Doxagram sellers' own marketing of their database, not from Instagram, and should be treated as an attacker claim rather than a verified count.

**Human factor.** The account-recovery endpoint shipped without an authorization check tying the returned contact data to the authenticated caller — a missing check in a high-value, high-traffic flow that existed long enough for at least one attacker to scrape it at scale before it was caught.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-02 (authorization checked server-side, per object, on every request), AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data).

**What happened.** Between August 10 and 19, 2017, staff at MacEwan University in Edmonton made three wire transfers totaling CAD $11.8 million to bank accounts controlled by fraudsters impersonating Clark Builders, a construction vendor the university had worked with for over a decade. **What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-02 (high-impact actions require a second person), HUMAN-07 (sensitive operations — like changing where money is sent — need a checklist, not just a trusted employee's judgment).

```yaml

  - https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/litigation-releases/lr-24541
  - https://news.apache.org/foundation/entry/media-alert-the-apache-software
```

**What happened.** Attackers exploited an unpatched Apache Struts vulnerability (CVE-2017-5638) in Equifax's online dispute-portal application starting May 13, 2017, ultimately reaching the personal data of about 147 million Americans (an initial disclosure of 143 million was revised upward over the following year), plus 15.2 million UK residents and roughly 19,000 Canadians. Equifax discovered the intrusion on July 29, 2017 and disclosed it publicly on September 7 — 76 days after access began. The Apache Software Foundation itself publicly confirmed that same month that the root cause was Equifax's failure to apply a patch that had been available since March 7, 2017.

**Entry.** `entry/unpatched-cve` — the Struts patch had been available for over two months, and an internal Equifax email on March 9 directed administrators to apply it. It was never applied to the internet-facing Automated Consumer Interview System, giving attackers remote code execution on that server.

**Escalation.** From that single web application, attackers found plaintext usernames and passwords stored on internal systems, which — combined with the absence of network segmentation between the dispute portal and Equifax's broader environment — let them reach 51 unrelated databases and query them repeatedly over 76 days while blending in as authorized users. Traffic inspection that might have caught the exfiltration earlier was blind for months because the certificate on the device used to decrypt and inspect that network segment's traffic had lapsed and gone unrenewed; only an unrelated certificate renewal on July 29 restored visibility and surfaced the activity immediately.

**Blast radius.** Names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth, addresses, and in many cases driver's license numbers for roughly 147 million people; credit card numbers for about 209,000 consumers. The 2019 FTC/CFPB/state settlement required Equifax to pay a minimum of $575 million, rising to $700 million depending on claims volume, with $300 million earmarked for victim compensation; the UK Financial Conduct Authority separately fined Equifax's UK subsidiary £11.16 million.

**Human factor.** Equifax's patch process "relied on an honour system, without strict enforcement," per the House Oversight Committee's December 2018 report, and a 2015 internal audit had already flagged gaps that were not fully remediated. Separately, three executives sold roughly $1.8 million in Equifax stock in the days after the breach was found internally but before it was disclosed; the SEC later charged former CIO Jun Ying with insider trading for exercising and selling stock ahead of the announcement, and he was convicted in 2019. The public disclosure itself compounded the damage: Equifax stood up a separate, confusing response domain, an official company Twitter account mistakenly linked to a phishing lookalike of that site, and the company initially required an arbitration waiver from consumers signing up for free credit monitoring before reversing course under public pressure.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (dependency/CVE scanning in CI that fails the build on known-exploited vulnerabilities, so an unpatched Struts instance doesn't run in production for two months), CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between tiers, so one web application's compromise doesn't reach 51 unrelated databases), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime rather than sitting in plaintext on reachable systems), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a stated disclosure clock, so 76 days of undetected access doesn't become two more months of public silence).

```yaml
  - https://blogs.cisco.com/security/talos/ccleanup-a-vast-number-of-machines-at-risk
```

**What happened.** Cisco Talos and Avast disclosed on September 18, 2017 that the official CCleaner v5.33 installer and CCleaner Cloud v1.07.3191 — distributed from Piriform's own download servers between August 15 and September 12, 2017 — had been modified to include a backdoor before being signed with Piriform's legitimate code-signing certificate. Roughly 2.27 million machines downloaded and ran the trojanized installer. Security firm Morphisec first flagged the anomaly to Avast, which had acquired Piriform two months earlier.

**Entry.** `entry/supply-chain-build` — Talos assessed that attackers first accessed an unattended Piriform developer workstation on March 11, 2017 via the remote-support tool TeamViewer, then used that foothold to modify the build environment itself, so the malicious code was compiled and signed as part of a routine, trusted release rather than injected afterward. How the attacker first reached that workstation was never publicly established.

**Escalation.** `escalation/signed-artifact` — the payload rode Piriform's own valid signing certificate past every endpoint tool that trusted signed CCleaner releases. `escalation/supply-chain-cascade` — Piriform's legitimate, high-trust distribution channel was itself the mechanism used to reach downstream customers, including a deliberately selected second stage.

**Blast radius.** All 2.27 million installs received a first-stage payload that collected host, network, and admin-rights information. Talos found evidence attackers used that reconnaissance to hand-select roughly 40 machines at major technology and telecom companies — including Google, Microsoft, Cisco, Intel, Samsung, Sony, and VMware — for a second-stage payload. No organization confirmed further compromise or data loss resulted from that second stage, so confirmed impact remains limited to the backdoor's presence rather than any proven theft.

**Human factor.** A single developer workstation left reachable via unattended remote-access software gave attackers a path into the build pipeline itself, where "this is what we compiled" had no independent verification step before signing and shipping to millions of users.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-03 (build provenance and artifact signing that can prove what shipped matches what was reviewed, not just that Piriform's certificate signed it), CICD-08 (treating the build system itself as production and monitoring it accordingly — the tampering sat undetected for roughly a month on the public download server), DEPS-10 (a named, rehearsed response — for downstream organizations — to "a vendor we run has been compromised").

**What happened.** Whole Foods Market disclosed on September 28, 2017 — about a month after Amazon completed its acquisition of the company — that payment card data had been stolen from point-of-sale systems at taprooms and full table-service restaurants inside roughly 100 stores. The exposure window varied by location, running as early as March 10, 2017 through as late as September 28, 2017. Card numbers, expiration dates, verification codes, and cardholder names were captured; the company's primary grocery checkout registers run on a separate system and were not affected, and Amazon.com transactions were never connected to Whole Foods' systems at all.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — Whole Foods never disclosed how attackers reached the taproom and restaurant point-of-sale network. The pattern (malware on POS terminals capturing card data over months) is consistent with the RAM-scraping POS malware common in retail breaches of that era, but the company did not confirm a specific mechanism.

**Escalation.** None required, as described. Once inside the affected POS environment, no further lateral movement into other systems was reported; segmentation between that subsystem and the primary checkout system is credited with keeping the exposure limited to the specialty venues.

**Blast radius.** Whole Foods never disclosed a count of affected customers or cards. The breach was first flagged not by Whole Foods' own monitoring but by payment card issuers, who noticed a pattern of fraudulent transactions traced back to cards used at Whole Foods locations.

**Human factor.** The taproom and restaurant venues ran on a different, apparently less-monitored point-of-sale system than the main grocery registers, and nobody inside Whole Foods detected months of card-data theft on it — detection came from card networks watching for fraud patterns downstream, not from the merchant.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between tiers and environments — the same separation that protected the primary registers should have come with equivalent monitoring on the taproom POS), OBSV-03 (detection must itself be monitored; relying on card issuers to notice fraud months later is a broken sensor).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The Apache Software Foundation publicly confirmed that Equifax's breach was caused by failure to apply the CVE-2017-5638 patch it had shipped in March, redirecting industry attention toward patch-management discipline rather than treating Struts as an isolated Equifax failure.
- The US House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, jointly with the House Science, Space and Technology Committee, opened a formal investigation into Equifax on September 14, 2017 — the start of a review that produced a detailed public report on the failure chain in December 2018.
- SEC Chairman Jay Clayton issued a public "Statement on Cybersecurity" on September 20, 2017 alongside the EDGAR disclosure, committing the agency to enhanced internal risk assessment of its own systems — a regulator publicly acknowledging its own security debt.
- Avast/Piriform's response to the CCleaner compromise — a clean 5.34 build shipped within days of Talos's disclosure, cooperation with law enforcement to take down the attackers' command-and-control server, and a full public technical write-up — became a widely cited model for transparent supply-chain incident handling.
- A cluster of exposed AWS S3 buckets that quarter (Verizon/NICE Systems in July, and similar incidents at other large companies) sharpened industry pressure on cloud customers to actively audit default-public storage configurations rather than assume provider defaults were safe.
- Equifax's initial requirement that consumers waive their right to sue in order to use its free credit-monitoring offer drew immediate public and congressional backlash and was withdrawn within days, feeding a broader post-breach push toward no-strings-attached identity-monitoring remedies.
