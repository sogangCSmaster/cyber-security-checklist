# 2017 Q4 — Cover-Ups, Stale Breaches, and Crypto Heists

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/unknown` — most breaches disclosed this quarter named no technical mechanism at all; where one was described, it was payment-card malware on point-of-sale terminals (Forever 21, Hyatt) or a credential left in source control (Uber).
> - **New technique this quarter:** SMB credential-hash harvesting via malicious Word template injection and trade-press watering holes, used by the Dragonfly 2.0 campaign against energy-sector staff (CISA TA17-293A).
> - **Recurring failure:** lessons didn't carry forward at repeat victims — Hyatt's second card breach in two years, Youbit's second hack in one year, and two old user databases (Disqus, Imgur) that sat undetected for years before an outside researcher found them.
> - **Records in this file:** 11

```yaml
  - https://thehackernews.com/2017/10/disqus-comment-system-hacked.html
```

**What happened.** Security researcher Troy Hunt obtained a copy of a Disqus user database and notified the company on October 5, 2017; Disqus confirmed the following day that the data came from a July 2012 intrusion it had never previously detected or disclosed. The snapshot held email addresses, usernames, and sign-up/login dates in plain text for 17.5 million accounts, plus salted SHA-1 password hashes for roughly a third of them.

**Entry.** Disqus has never published how the 2012 intruder got in — no CVE, credential, or vulnerability class was ever named publicly. This record uses `entry/unknown` rather than guess at a mechanism no source documents.

**Escalation.** Nothing in any disclosure describes lateral movement or a second system being reached; the entire user table for that era appears to have been pulled in one action. `escalation/none-required` is used as the closest documented description, not as a confirmed technical finding.

**Blast radius.** 17.5 million accounts' worth of emails, usernames, and account metadata were exposed, with roughly a third also losing a password hash. Disqus reported no evidence of unauthorized logins and forced a precautionary reset for all affected users.

**Human factor.** The proximate failure is five years of non-detection: nobody at Disqus found this internally, and it surfaced only because a third party independently obtained and cross-referenced the data. A secondary factor is that a 2012-era account, already stale, was still sitting in a form useful to an attacker in 2017.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-06 (modern, memory-hard password hashing rather than salted SHA-1) would have made the stolen hashes far less useful; DATA-09 (data minimization and retention limits) would have reduced how much a five-year-old dataset still exposed; OBSV-03 (detection itself monitored, so a five-year blind spot is itself an incident) addresses the root gap.

```yaml
  - https://www.securityweek.com/hyatt-hotels-hit-another-card-breach/
```

**What happened.** Hyatt disclosed in October 2017 that malware had harvested payment-card data — cardholder name, card number, expiration date, and internal verification code — from cards manually entered or swiped at front desks across 41 properties in 11 countries between March 18 and July 2, 2017. It was Hyatt's second card breach in two years; a 2015 incident had hit 250 properties in 50 countries.

**Entry.** Hyatt did not publish how the malware first reached its systems. No initial vector — phishing, remote-access abuse, an unpatched service — was named in either its own statement or subsequent reporting.

**Escalation.** Malware appeared on point-of-sale terminals at 41 separately operated properties within the same roughly three-and-a-half-month window. That pattern points to a shared corporate or payment-processing network reaching many properties rather than 41 independent local compromises, consistent with insufficient separation between corporate IT and property-level payment systems — the same weakness reporting attributed to the larger 2015 incident.

**Blast radius.** Card data was exposed for an undisclosed number of guests across 41 hotels in 11 countries (5 in the U.S., 18 in China) over a 106-day window before Hyatt's internal detection on July 2, 2017; public disclosure followed roughly three months later.

**Human factor.** This is Hyatt's second card-theft incident since 2015. Whatever network changes followed the first breach were not sufficient to prevent a structurally similar one 18 months later — a process failure in how the prior incident's lessons were carried into production.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between tiers and between properties) would have contained the malware to a single site rather than 41; OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and admin actions) would likely have shortened the 106-day detection window; OBSV-09 (post-incident review produces a control change, not just a report) speaks directly to why the 2015 incident's lessons didn't hold.

```yaml
sources:

```

**What happened.** On October 20, 2017, the FBI and DHS published joint technical alert TA17-293A describing an ongoing campaign — tracked by Symantec as "Dragonfly 2.0" and attributed to a Russia-linked group also known as Energetic Bear — targeting government, energy, nuclear, water, aviation, and critical-manufacturing organizations in the U.S., Turkey, and Switzerland. The alert did not name individual victims; it described techniques and indicators of compromise observed since at least December 2015, with a marked increase in 2017.

**Entry.** Attackers sent spear-phishing emails carrying malicious Word documents built with a template-injection technique: opening the document made Microsoft Office silently request a remote file over SMB, which leaked the victim's Windows credential hash to an attacker-controlled server with no successful download required. A second entry path used watering holes — legitimate ICS/SCADA trade-publication websites compromised to redirect visitors to the same SMB-based credential-harvesting infrastructure.

**Escalation.** Harvested credential hashes were cracked or relayed and reused to reach further systems; once inside, attackers created administrative accounts, disabled host firewalls, enabled RDP, and ran credential-harvesting tools (including Hydra and CrackMapExec) to move further. The alert also described a staging pattern: attackers first compromised smaller, less-secure third-party suppliers trusted by the ultimate targets, then used that access as a pivot.

**Blast radius.** CISA and Symantec described access to "dozens" of energy-sector organizations across the reporting period without giving an exact count; no operational or safety impact was confirmed, and the observed activity was consistent with reconnaissance and credential theft rather than sabotage. `scale` is recorded as undisclosed because no victim count was published.

**Human factor.** The campaign's entire first stage relied on energy-sector staff opening a themed attachment or visiting a trusted trade site — both classic social-engineering surfaces — and on target organizations extending trust to third-party suppliers whose own security posture was never verified to the same standard.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on accounts with production or OT reach) would have made a harvested password hash far less useful; CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between IT and OT/control-system environments) would have limited how far a compromised credential could travel; VENDOR-08 (vendor and supplier access time-boxed and separately monitored) addresses the staging-through-suppliers pattern the alert specifically called out.


**What happened.** Forever 21 disclosed on November 14, 2017 that malware had been found on point-of-sale devices at some U.S. stores, and that its investigation traced unauthorized access back to April 3, 2017. The company's own post-mortem found that encryption on affected terminals "was not always on," letting the malware read unencrypted card-track data as it passed through the device.

**Entry.** Forever 21 did not disclose how the malware reached its POS devices. What is documented is the condition that let it succeed once there: point-to-point encryption, in place since 2015, was not consistently active at the affected registers.

**Escalation.** No further escalation was needed. With encryption off, the malware could read card-track data — card number, expiration date, and verification code, occasionally cardholder name — directly as it moved through the device; this is the schema's textbook case of data being "directly readable" once the entry point was reached.

**Blast radius.** Forever 21 has not disclosed how many cards or customers were affected, only the store-level and date-range detail. At the outer bound, exposure ran from April 3 to November 18, 2017 (229 days) across an unspecified number of U.S. locations; individual stores had shorter windows within that range.

**Human factor.** Someone, or some deployment process, allowed payment terminals to run with encryption toggled off for months without anyone noticing — a silent configuration drift on infrastructure that existed specifically to prevent this outcome.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-07 (infrastructure declared as code with drift detection) would have caught encryption silently turning off; OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read or export volume) could have flagged the malware's activity; HUMAN-07 (a checklist for sensitive deployments, not just a competent operator) would have made "is encryption on" a verified step rather than an assumption.

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2018/04/uber-agrees-expanded-settlement-ftc-related-privacy-security-claims
  - https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndca/pr/former-chief-security-officer-uber-convicted-federal-charges-covering-data-breach
  - https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2022/10/05/uber-obstruction-sullivan-hacking/
```

**What happened.** In October–November 2016, intruders downloaded an archive of Uber rider and driver data from an AWS S3 bucket, then emailed Uber demanding payment. Instead of disclosing a breach, Uber's security team paid the attackers $100,000 through its bug-bounty program, had them sign non-disclosure agreements attesting no data was retained, and told no one outside a small group — not affected users, not state regulators, and not the FTC, which was at that moment actively investigating Uber's separate 2014 breach. New CEO Dara Khosrowshahi disclosed the incident publicly on November 21, 2017, more than a year after Uber first learned of it.

**Entry.** Attackers found a hard-coded AWS access key inside a private GitHub repository used by Uber engineers. The FTC's complaint states the key had been "posted on a code-sharing website" by an Uber engineer; whoever found it could use it directly against Uber's AWS account with no further authentication step.

**Escalation.** None was required beyond what the leaked key already granted: it was broad enough (`escalation/overscoped-credential`) to reach directly into the S3 bucket holding the rider and driver archive, with no privilege-escalation or lateral-movement step documented.

**Blast radius.** Attackers obtained names, email addresses, and phone numbers for 57 million riders and drivers worldwide, and driver's license numbers for about 600,000 U.S. drivers. The concealment itself became the larger cost: in September 2018 Uber paid $148 million to settle with all 50 states and D.C. over the delayed notification; in April 2018 the FTC expanded its existing enforcement order against Uber to cover the cover-up, requiring third-party audits and bug-bounty record retention and exposing Uber to civil penalties of up to $41,484 per future violation, though the order itself carried no fine. In October 2022, a federal jury convicted former Chief Security Officer Joseph Sullivan of obstruction of justice and misprision of a felony for his role — the first criminal conviction of a corporate security executive over how a breach was handled — and he was sentenced in May 2023 to three years' probation and 200 hours of community service.

**Human factor.** Two separate decisions compound here. First, an engineer committed a live, broadly scoped AWS credential to a repository, and no scanning or review process caught it. Second, and more consequentially, once the breach was known, Uber's security and legal leadership chose to disguise a $100,000 extortion payment as a bug-bounty payout and keep it from regulators and the board rather than treat it as the breach it was — a deliberate concealment decision, not an oversight.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no secret in source, config, or git history) would have prevented the key from being committable at all; CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job, one resource) would have limited what the key could reach even if leaked; CRED-03 (rotate on exposure) would have closed access once Uber knew the key was out; OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan naming the decider and the clock) and HUMAN-02 (high-impact actions require a second person) address the cover-up directly — a payment structured to evade disclosure law should never be a decision two people can make alone.

**What happened.** On November 23, 2017, security researcher Troy Hunt emailed Imgur a dataset he believed contained its users' credentials. **What would have stopped it.** DATA-06 (passwords hashed with a modern, memory-hard algorithm) would have made the stolen hashes far less useful years later; OBSV-03 (detection itself monitored, so years of silence is itself flagged) addresses why this took an outsider to find; OBSV-06 (a published way for an outsider to report an issue) is what actually let this get fixed quickly once found, and is worth naming as a control that worked.

**What happened.** PayPal completed its $238 million acquisition of bill-payment processor TIO Networks in July 2017. ```

**What happened.** UpGuard researcher Chris Vickery discovered on October 6, 2017 that a 36 GB database file, hosted by data-analytics firm Alteryx and built from Experian's ConsumerView marketing database plus 2010 U.S. Census data, sat in an AWS S3 bucket configured to allow download by any "Authenticated AWS User." UpGuard notified Alteryx, which secured the bucket, and published its findings publicly on December 19, 2017.

**Entry.** The bucket's permissions granted access to any AWS account holder, not just Alteryx or its partners. Because AWS lets anyone create an account for free with a throwaway email address, this setting was functionally equivalent to public access despite technically requiring "authentication."

UpGuard found no evidence the bucket had been accessed maliciously before its report; `impact/none-confirmed` reflects that this was an exposure caught before any confirmed abuse.

**Human factor.** Someone configured — or left at a permissive default — an access-control setting ("Authenticated Users," rather than a specific set of accounts) that is easy to mistake for "private" but functions as "anyone with an AWS account." That the data represented one of Experian's core commercial products makes the gap between its stated value (reportedly sold for close to $40,000 per license) and its actual protection especially stark.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (nothing publicly reachable unless deliberately decided, checked by regular enumeration) would have caught this bucket before a researcher did; DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy buckets audited) directly addresses the "Authenticated Users" misconfiguration; VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data) is the control Experian and the Census Bureau needed to even know this copy of their data existed at Alteryx.

**What happened.** On December 19, 2017, South Korean cryptocurrency exchange Youbit announced that its hot wallet had been compromised, losing 17% of the exchange's total assets; the exact amount of cryptocurrency or its dollar value was not disclosed. South Korea's national cybersecurity agency (KISA) and later the White House attributed the attack to North Korea's Lazarus Group, specifically its BlueNoroff subunit, but the technical entry mechanism itself was not made public.

**Escalation.** What is known is narrower than the entry vector: the attacker reached the hot wallet — Youbit's internet-connected, immediately spendable holdings — while cold (offline) storage was untouched. `escalation/none-required` reflects that once inside, the hot wallet's funds were directly accessible with no further step described; it is notable that the hot/cold split itself limited the damage to that 17%.

**Blast radius.** 17% of Youbit's total exchange assets were lost; neither a cryptocurrency amount nor a dollar figure was ever disclosed by the company, so `scale` and `cost_usd` are recorded as undisclosed rather than estimated from unconfirmed press figures. The loss was enough to force the exchange into bankruptcy.

**Human factor.** This was Youbit's second hack within the same year. Whatever remediation followed the first incident did not sufficiently reduce what a second attacker could reach in the exchange's live, internet-connected wallet.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation, here between hot and cold wallet infrastructure) is the control that already partially worked and should have been extended further; OBSV-09 (post-incident review produces a control change, not just a report) speaks directly to the gap between the April hack and this one; a hot-wallet balance cap tied to near-term operational need has no matching control ID in `checklist.md` today, so this record uses `DATA-NEW` for it — enforced minimization of funds held in any internet-connected wallet.

**What happened.** Nissan Canada Finance (NCF) became aware of unauthorized access to its systems on December 11, 2017, and publicly notified customers ten days later, on December 21. The breach affected approximately 1.13 million current and former NCF and INFINITI Financial Services Canada customers.

**Entry.** NCF's public notice referred only to "unauthorized person(s)" gaining access to customer information; no technical mechanism, vulnerability, or compromised credential was named in its disclosure or in subsequent coverage.

**Escalation.** No escalation detail was published. `escalation/none-required` is used as the closest available description given that customer financing records appear to have been directly reachable once access was gained, with no further mechanism disclosed.

**Blast radius.** Exposed data included customer names, addresses, vehicle make and model, vehicle identification numbers, credit scores, loan amounts, and monthly payments for roughly 1.13 million people; no payment card data was involved. NCF offered affected customers 12 months of complimentary credit monitoring through TransUnion.

**Human factor.** The clearest documented failure is the ten-day gap between NCF learning of the breach and telling the people affected by it — time in which those customers could not yet protect themselves, and which NCF's own timeline shows was a deliberate delay rather than a detection lag.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-05 (a written incident plan naming the decider, the communicator, and the disclosure clock) directly addresses the ten-day gap; DATA-04 (databases not reachable from the public internet) and OBSV-01 (centralized logging of access to sensitive systems) are the best-available general controls given NCF never disclosed which was actually missing.

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The FBI and DHS published joint technical alert **TA17-293A** on October 20, 2017, giving energy-sector defenders indicators of compromise and mitigations for the Dragonfly 2.0 campaign's SMB-credential-harvesting technique, including blocking outbound SMB at the network edge and disabling Office's automatic remote-template loading.
- Uber's November 21 disclosure triggered a coordinated investigation by all 50 U.S. state attorneys general within the same quarter, opened specifically over the year-long concealment rather than the breach itself; it concluded in a $148 million multistate settlement in September 2018, setting a template regulators would reuse in later concealment cases.
- Cloud-security researchers kept publishing misconfigured-AWS-S3 findings through the quarter; the Alteryx/Experian disclosure specifically drew attention to the "Any Authenticated AWS User" grant as a setting administrators routinely mistook for private, since it required only a free, unverified AWS sign-up to exploit.
- Troy Hunt's Have I Been Pwned continued to function as an informal clearinghouse for old breach data, prompting both Disqus (within a day) and Imgur (within 24 hours) to verify and disclose years-old breaches this quarter — reinforcing rapid response to outside researcher contact as an expected baseline rather than optional courtesy.
- Point-of-sale card breaches disclosed this quarter (Forever 21, Hyatt) renewed industry attention on point-to-point encryption at payment terminals as a control that must be continuously verified in production, not just enabled once at deployment.
- PayPal's decision to suspend TIO Networks' entire platform on November 10, 2017 — weeks before public disclosure and while still investigating — set a visible precedent for treating a newly acquired company's infrastructure as unverified until it is independently audited against the acquirer's own security bar.
