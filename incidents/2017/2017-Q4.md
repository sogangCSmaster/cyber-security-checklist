# 2017 Q4 — Cover-Ups, Stale Breaches, and Crypto Heists

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/unknown` — most breaches disclosed this quarter named no technical mechanism at all; where one was described, it was payment-card malware on point-of-sale terminals (Forever 21, Hyatt) or a credential left in source control (Uber).
> - **New technique this quarter:** SMB credential-hash harvesting via malicious Word template injection and trade-press watering holes, used by the Dragonfly 2.0 campaign against energy-sector staff (CISA TA17-293A).
> - **Recurring failure:** lessons didn't carry forward at repeat victims — Hyatt's second card breach in two years, Youbit's second hack in one year, and two old user databases (Disqus, Imgur) that sat undetected for years before an outside researcher found them.
> - **Records in this file:** 19


**What happened.** Hyatt disclosed in October 2017 that malware had harvested payment-card data — cardholder name, card number, expiration date, and internal verification code — from cards manually entered or swiped at front desks across 41 properties in 11 countries between March 18 and July 2, 2017. It was Hyatt's second card breach in two years; a 2015 incident had hit 250 properties in 50 countries.

**Entry.** Hyatt did not publish how the malware first reached its systems. No initial vector — phishing, remote-access abuse, an unpatched service — was named in either its own statement or subsequent reporting.

**Escalation.** Malware appeared on point-of-sale terminals at 41 separately operated properties within the same roughly three-and-a-half-month window. That pattern points to a shared corporate or payment-processing network reaching many properties rather than 41 independent local compromises, consistent with insufficient separation between corporate IT and property-level payment systems — the same weakness reporting attributed to the larger 2015 incident.

**Blast radius.** Card data was exposed for an undisclosed number of guests across 41 hotels in 11 countries (5 in the U.S., 18 in China) over a 106-day window before Hyatt's internal detection on July 2, 2017; public disclosure followed roughly three months later.

**Human factor.** This is Hyatt's second card-theft incident since 2015. Whatever network changes followed the first breach were not sufficient to prevent a structurally similar one 18 months later — a process failure in how the prior incident's lessons were carried into production.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between tiers and between properties) would have contained the malware to a single site rather than 41; OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and admin actions) would likely have shortened the 106-day detection window; OBSV-09 (post-incident review produces a control change, not just a report) speaks directly to why the 2015 incident's lessons didn't hold.

**What happened.** Security researcher Troy Hunt obtained a copy of a Disqus user database and notified the company on October 5, 2017; Disqus confirmed the following day that the data came from a July 2012 intrusion it had never previously detected or disclosed. The snapshot held email addresses, usernames, and sign-up/login dates in plain text for 17.5 million accounts, plus salted SHA-1 password hashes for roughly a third of them.

**Entry.** Disqus has never published how the 2012 intruder got in — no CVE, credential, or vulnerability class was ever named publicly. This record uses `entry/unknown` rather than guess at a mechanism no source documents.

**Escalation.** Nothing in any disclosure describes lateral movement or a second system being reached; the entire user table for that era appears to have been pulled in one action. `escalation/none-required` is used as the closest documented description, not as a confirmed technical finding.

**Blast radius.** 17.5 million accounts' worth of emails, usernames, and account metadata were exposed, with roughly a third also losing a password hash. Disqus reported no evidence of unauthorized logins and forced a precautionary reset for all affected users.

**Human factor.** The proximate failure is five years of non-detection: nobody at Disqus found this internally, and it surfaced only because a third party independently obtained and cross-referenced the data. A secondary factor is that a 2012-era account, already stale, was still sitting in a form useful to an attacker in 2017.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-06 (modern, memory-hard password hashing rather than salted SHA-1) would have made the stolen hashes far less useful; DATA-09 (data minimization and retention limits) would have reduced how much a five-year-old dataset still exposed; OBSV-03 (detection itself monitored, so a five-year blind spot is itself an incident) addresses the root gap.


**What happened.** Far Eastern International Bank, a mid-sized Taiwanese bank, discovered in early October 2017 that intruders had used malware planted on its internal computers and servers to reach its SWIFT payment terminal and push roughly $60 million in fraudulent international transfers to accounts in the United States, Cambodia, and Sri Lanka. The bank and Taiwanese authorities, working with counterpart banks abroad, recovered all but about $500,000 of the total within days. Taiwan's premier ordered a government-wide review of financial-sector cybersecurity in response.

**Entry.** Neither the bank nor Taiwanese authorities disclosed how the malware first reached its network — no phishing email, compromised vendor, or specific vulnerability was named. The bank described the malware itself as "of a type never seen before." BAE Systems researchers attributed the intrusion to North Korea's Lazarus Group based on code overlap with malware from earlier SWIFT heists, though that is a vendor assessment rather than an official government attribution; the initial foothold itself remains undocumented, so this record uses `entry/unknown`.

**Escalation.** Once inside, the malware moved from ordinary bank systems to the SWIFT terminal used for interbank transfers and harvested the credentials needed to authorize payments — a path that should not exist between general-purpose computers and a payment-messaging endpoint capable of moving tens of millions of dollars.

**Blast radius.** Attackers initiated transfers totaling close to $60 million to accounts in the US, Cambodia, and Sri Lanka. Rapid detection and cooperation with foreign banks recovered all but roughly $500,000; two suspects were arrested in Sri Lanka, one found with $1.1 million of the stolen funds in a personal account, while a third suspect remained at large.

**Human factor.** A payment-messaging terminal capable of authorizing multi-million-dollar international transfers was reachable from systems compromised by ordinary malware, with no documented additional control — such as a required second approver — standing between a stolen credential and an executed transfer.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between ordinary IT systems and payment-messaging infrastructure) would have blocked the malware's path to the SWIFT terminal; AUTH-11 (every state-changing interface authenticates its caller, including non-HTTP ones like SWIFT) speaks to the credential-harvesting step; HUMAN-02 (high-impact actions require a second person) would have required a second, independent authorization before tens of millions of dollars could move.

```yaml
  - https://grahamcluley.com/f35-fighter-hack/
```

**What happened.** In July 2016, an intruder the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) later codenamed "Alf" broke into the network of a small, unnamed subcontractor to Australia's Department of Defence — an aerospace engineering firm with around 50 staff and a single dedicated IT employee — and over roughly two weeks exfiltrated about 30 gigabytes of restricted technical data covering the F-35 Joint Strike Fighter, the P-8 Poseidon, the C-130, JDAM guidance kits, and Australian naval vessel designs. ASD learned of the intrusion from a partner organisation in November 2016 and made the case public a year later, on October 11, 2017, when incident-response manager Mitchell Clarke presented it at an Australian Information Security Association conference and it featured in the ACSC's 2017 Threat Report.

**Entry.** ASD's own account, as reported, gives two documented weaknesses rather than one clean chain: an internet-facing server carrying a vulnerability that had gone unpatched for around 12 months, and — separately, elsewhere on the same small network — internet-facing services still running factory default logins (`admin`/`admin`, `guest`/`guest`). Either alone would likely have been sufficient against a network with one IT staffer; this record tags both rather than picking one.

**Escalation.** Once in, the intruder used stolen administrative credentials and installed multiple webshells (contemporary reporting associated the tooling with "China Chopper," commonly seen in Chinese-linked intrusions, though officials stopped short of formal attribution) to move laterally and maintain persistent access across the firm's network for the roughly two weeks it took to locate and exfiltrate the defence files.

**Blast radius.** About 30 GB of data classified under Australian and, via ITAR, US export-control rules was taken, covering active US and Australian defence programs. Defence Industry Minister Christopher Pyne said publicly that the attacker's identity was unknown and "could be one of a number of different actors... a state actor, [or] a non-state actor" — this record uses `actor/unknown` rather than repeat unconfirmed speculation as fact.

**Human factor.** A single security-cleared prime contractor's supply chain extended, unchecked, to a 50-person firm running defence-sensitive design data behind an unpatched server and default logins, with no indication anyone outside that firm had verified its security posture matched the sensitivity of what it held.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems) directly addresses a 12-month-old unpatched vulnerability; CRED-10 (no default or shared credential in anything shipped or deployed) closes the admin/admin, guest/guest gap; VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding sensitive data) is what Defence needed to know this subcontractor held ITAR-controlled files at all.

**What happened.** Security researcher Troy Hunt had been sitting on a 27 GB MySQL backup file, "masterdeeds.sql," since a source sent it to him in March 2017 without context. In October 2017 he began investigating its origin publicly on Twitter; journalist Tefo Mohapi traced it to real-estate group Jigsaw Holdings, whose web server had the file sitting in a directory with browsing enabled — openly downloadable by anyone who found the link. The data was taken offline on October 18, 2017, after being reachable for at least seven months and, on the file's own metadata, potentially since 2015.

**Entry.** No attacker or intrusion is documented anywhere in this record: someone with legitimate access to the file — most likely at Jigsaw Holdings or its data supplier, Dracore Data Sciences — placed it on a public-facing web server and left directory listing switched on. That combination of `entry/inadvertent-disclosure` and `entry/misconfiguration` is the closest match the vocabulary offers; there is no dedicated tag for "real estate" in `sector/`, and `sector/consumer` is used here as the nearest fit, noted as a gap.

**Blast radius.** Reported figures for the file varied as investigation continued: initial coverage described roughly 30–60 million identities, while Hunt's own final count put it at about 66.36 million total records (including around 9 million deceased people), covering some 57 million living individuals — close to the entire population of South Africa. Exposed fields included ID numbers (which encode date of birth and gender), names, ethnicity, marital status, estimated income, and physical addresses. No confirmed exploitation of the data was reported; Hunt noted only that "at the absolute least, the data was lost control of."

**Human factor.** Someone published a sensitive consumer database to a location with no access control, and no one — not Jigsaw, not Dracore, not any monitoring system — noticed for a period measured in months, quite possibly years, until an outside researcher who had been sent the file eventually asked the internet to help identify where it came from.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits) would have reduced how much a stray backup file could expose; HUMAN-07 (a checklist for sensitive operations, not just a competent operator) is what should have stood between "generate a backup" and "leave it on a public web root"; OBSV-03 (detection itself monitored, so a file sitting exposed for months is itself flagged) addresses why this took an outsider to find.

**What happened.** In mid-October 2017, Malaysian technology forum Lowyat.net reported that databases covering tens of millions of mobile subscriber records were being traded, and eventually given away, on its forums. The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) confirmed on October 23 that 46.2 million mobile subscriptions were affected — more than Malaysia's population, reflecting multiple SIMs per person — sourced from at least a dozen telcos and mobile virtual network operators including Celcom, Maxis, DiGi, and U Mobile. Bundled with the telecom data were records from job portal JobStreet.com (reportedly around 17 million records) and several medical professional bodies, including the Malaysian Medical Council and Malaysian Dental Association.

**Blast radius.** MCMC's confirmed figure of 46.2 million mobile subscriptions, plus the JobStreet and medical-body records, made this one of the largest data exposures ever reported in Malaysia, touching a large majority of the country's population across telecom, employment, and healthcare-adjacent professional data. MCMC initially ordered Lowyat's article taken down as a "preventive measure" before approving its restoration two days later.

**Human factor.** Data three or more years old continued circulating privately for years before reaching a public forum, with no telco, regulator, or intermediary catching or disclosing the exposure in that window — the incident became visible only because it was eventually offered for sale somewhere public enough for a journalist to see it.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data) matters most here, given data from a dozen-plus separate organisations surfaced together, suggesting a common point the affected bodies may not have tracked; OBSV-03 (detection itself monitored) addresses years of undetected circulation; DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits) would have reduced how much multi-year-old data was still valuable to expose.


**What happened.** On October 20, 2017, the FBI and DHS published joint technical alert TA17-293A describing an ongoing campaign — tracked by Symantec as "Dragonfly 2.0" and attributed to a Russia-linked group also known as Energetic Bear — targeting government, energy, nuclear, water, aviation, and critical-manufacturing organizations in the U.S., Turkey, and Switzerland. The alert did not name individual victims; it described techniques and indicators of compromise observed since at least December 2015, with a marked increase in 2017.

**Entry.** Attackers sent spear-phishing emails carrying malicious Word documents built with a template-injection technique: opening the document made Microsoft Office silently request a remote file over SMB, which leaked the victim's Windows credential hash to an attacker-controlled server with no successful download required. A second entry path used watering holes — legitimate ICS/SCADA trade-publication websites compromised to redirect visitors to the same SMB-based credential-harvesting infrastructure.

**Escalation.** Harvested credential hashes were cracked or relayed and reused to reach further systems; once inside, attackers created administrative accounts, disabled host firewalls, enabled RDP, and ran credential-harvesting tools (including Hydra and CrackMapExec) to move further. The alert also described a staging pattern: attackers first compromised smaller, less-secure third-party suppliers trusted by the ultimate targets, then used that access as a pivot.

**Blast radius.** CISA and Symantec described access to "dozens" of energy-sector organizations across the reporting period without giving an exact count; no operational or safety impact was confirmed, and the observed activity was consistent with reconnaissance and credential theft rather than sabotage. `scale` is recorded as undisclosed because no victim count was published.

**Human factor.** The campaign's entire first stage relied on energy-sector staff opening a themed attachment or visiting a trusted trade site — both classic social-engineering surfaces — and on target organizations extending trust to third-party suppliers whose own security posture was never verified to the same standard.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on accounts with production or OT reach) would have made a harvested password hash far less useful; CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between IT and OT/control-system environments) would have limited how far a compromised credential could travel; VENDOR-08 (vendor and supplier access time-boxed and separately monitored) addresses the staging-through-suppliers pattern the alert specifically called out.


**What happened.** On October 24, 2017, a ransomware campaign later named "Bad Rabbit" hit roughly 200 organizations, the large majority in Russia with significant impact in Ukraine and smaller numbers in Turkey, Germany, and elsewhere. Confirmed victims included Kyiv Metro (ticketing and payment systems), Odesa International Airport (flight-information displays), Russian news agency Interfax, and Ukraine's Ministry of Infrastructure; the malware encrypted files and disk partitions and demanded a Bitcoin ransom.

**Entry.** Kaspersky's analysis traced the initial infection to drive-by downloads served from compromised, otherwise-legitimate Russian and Ukrainian news and media websites: visitors were served a page prompting them to run `install_flash_player.exe`, a fake Adobe Flash update that, once manually executed with administrator rights, dropped the ransomware. The tag vocabulary has no dedicated entry for this fake-update, drive-by pattern; `entry/rce` is used as the closest match for the resulting code execution, alongside the social engineering that got a victim to run it.

**Escalation.** Once running on one machine, Bad Rabbit needed no further attacker action to spread: it scanned local networks for open SMB shares, attempted the leaked NSA "EternalRomance" exploit (the same SMB vulnerability family behind WannaCry and NotPetya months earlier), tried a hardcoded list of common credentials, and used a bundled Mimikatz component to harvest further credentials from each newly infected host — worming through corporate networks on its own.

**Blast radius.** Kaspersky counted around 200 targeted organizations, concentrated in Russia and Ukraine, with real operational disruption at a metro system, an international airport, and a national news agency. Researchers noted some encrypted files' shadow copies were left intact, suggesting partial recovery was technically possible without paying, and several code-level similarities to June 2017's NotPetya outbreak led researchers to suspect a shared origin, though attribution was never confirmed.

**Human factor.** The campaign depended on two unaddressed weaknesses at once: staff at compromised organizations were willing to run an unsolicited "Flash update" prompted by a website, and the SMB vulnerability it used to spread internally had already been patched by Microsoft months earlier — in the update that stopped WannaCry — but evidently not applied everywhere it needed to be.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing and laterally-reachable systems) would have closed the EternalRomance path months before this outbreak; CLOUD-05 (network segmentation) would have limited self-propagation once one host was infected; VENDOR-05 (third-party scripts and content on sensitive pages minimized or isolated) speaks to the compromised legitimate news sites that served the initial payload to their own visitors.

**What happened.** Offshore law firm Appleby, headquartered in Bermuda with offices across Jersey, Guernsey, the Cayman Islands, and elsewhere, had its systems accessed and roughly 13.4 million confidential documents — about 1.4 terabytes spanning nearly 50 years of loan agreements, trust deeds, financial statements, and internal email — copied out, by Appleby's own account, in 2016. German newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung received the documents and shared them with the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists (ICIJ), which published its "Paradise Papers" investigation on November 5, 2017, naming clients ranging from a US cabinet secretary to entities tied to Queen Elizabeth II's private estate.

**Entry.** Appleby said it was hit by "an illegal computer hack," describing "an intruder who deployed the tactics of a professional hacker" and "covered his/her tracks," and stated its own forensic review ruled out an inside leak — but the firm never published a technical account of how the intruder first got in. `entry/unknown` reflects that gap.

Given the sheer volume taken — 13.4 million files across nearly five decades of records — this looks like broad access to Appleby's document management systems rather than a narrow, targeted pull, but no source describes the specific mechanism that got the intruder from initial access to that scale of exposure.

Appleby maintained publicly that the disclosed arrangements were lawful and that it had "done nothing unlawful," disputing the characterization of its clients' conduct rather than the fact of the breach.

**Human factor.** Appleby's own account places the intrusion in 2016, yet the firm did not confirm or disclose it until October 2017, after ICIJ and its media partners had already started approaching Appleby with questions — meaning either detection or disclosure (the firm has not said which) lagged the actual event by roughly a year.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-11 (bulk export is a privileged, logged, alerting action) is the most direct gap: 1.4 terabytes leaving a law firm's systems should be close to impossible to miss; OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read or export volume) is the detection control that specifically would have caught it; OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan naming the decider and the clock) addresses the roughly year-long gap between the intrusion and Appleby's public acknowledgment of it.

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The FBI and DHS published joint technical alert **TA17-293A** on October 20, 2017, giving energy-sector defenders indicators of compromise and mitigations for the Dragonfly 2.0 campaign's SMB-credential-harvesting technique, including blocking outbound SMB at the network edge and disabling Office's automatic remote-template loading.
- Uber's November 21 disclosure triggered a coordinated investigation by all 50 U.S. state attorneys general within the same quarter, opened specifically over the year-long concealment rather than the breach itself; it concluded in a $148 million multistate settlement in September 2018, setting a template regulators would reuse in later concealment cases.
- Cloud-security researchers kept publishing misconfigured-AWS-S3 findings through the quarter; the Alteryx/Experian disclosure specifically drew attention to the "Any Authenticated AWS User" grant as a setting administrators routinely mistook for private, since it required only a free, unverified AWS sign-up to exploit.
- Troy Hunt's Have I Been Pwned continued to function as an informal clearinghouse for old breach data, prompting both Disqus (within a day) and Imgur (within 24 hours) to verify and disclose years-old breaches this quarter — reinforcing rapid response to outside researcher contact as an expected baseline rather than optional courtesy.
- Point-of-sale card breaches disclosed this quarter (Forever 21, Hyatt) renewed industry attention on point-to-point encryption at payment terminals as a control that must be continuously verified in production, not just enabled once at deployment.
- PayPal's decision to suspend TIO Networks' entire platform on November 10, 2017 — weeks before public disclosure and while still investigating — set a visible precedent for treating a newly acquired company's infrastructure as unverified until it is independently audited against the acquirer's own security bar.
