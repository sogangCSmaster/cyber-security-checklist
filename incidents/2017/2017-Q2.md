# 2017 Q2 — Self-Propagating Worms and Open Buckets

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/unpatched-cve — unpatched SMBv1 (MS17-010/EternalBlue) drove both WannaCry and NotPetya's spread, the quarter's two largest events by victim count.
> - **New technique this quarter:** Leaked NSA exploit code weaponized into self-propagating worms — first as ransomware (WannaCry), then disguised as ransomware while actually a data-destroying wiper delivered through a trusted software update (NotPetya).
> - **Recurring failure:** Ordinary hygiene gaps — unpatched systems, single-factor remote access, and cloud storage or backup services left open by default — turned into some of the quarter's largest losses.
> - **Records in this file:** 10

**What happened.** Between March 24 and April 18, 2017, malware running on point-of-sale terminals at most of Chipotle's roughly 2,250 U.S. restaurants (plus seven Pizzeria Locale locations and some Canadian restaurants) read payment card data as customers paid. Chipotle disclosed the incident on April 25, 2017, and published investigation findings in the following weeks.

**Entry.** Chipotle never publicly disclosed how the malware first reached its point-of-sale environment; the company's statements describe the malware's behavior but not the initial access vector.

**Escalation.** The malware reached the large majority of restaurant locations nationwide over roughly four weeks. Chipotle did not publish how it propagated across so many sites, but the pattern is consistent with point-of-sale terminals and store networks that were not segmented from one another, letting compromise of one environment reach many others without added attacker effort.

**Blast radius.** Card numbers, expiration dates, internal verification codes, and in some cases cardholder names were captured from magnetic-stripe track data at nearly all affected restaurants for about a month. Chipotle has not disclosed a card or transaction count; law enforcement, payment networks, and forensic firms were engaged during the investigation.

**Human factor.** No individual mistake was identified in public reporting; the failure was structural — a nationwide restaurant network where compromise of one point of sale could reach payment systems at thousands of locations with no described internal segmentation.

**What would have stopped it.** Network segmentation isolating POS terminals from each other and from the rest of the store network (CLOUD-05); centralized logging of POS and admin activity to catch the malware's installation (OBSV-01); and alerting on abnormal card-read or export volume per terminal (OBSV-02).

```

**What happened.** Sabre Corporation disclosed on May 2, 2017 that an unauthorized party had accessed payment card and reservation data in its SynXis Central Reservations system, used by more than 32,000 independently owned and branded hotel properties worldwide, including Four Seasons, Trump Hotels, Hard Rock, Loews, Kimpton, and Rosewood. The affected hotels' own systems were not compromised — the exposure ran entirely through Sabre's shared, centrally hosted platform.

**Entry.** An attacker obtained valid login credentials to an account within the SynXis Central Reservations system. That account could be reached with only a username and static password, since Sabre had not required multi-factor authentication on the system; how the credentials were originally obtained was never publicly disclosed.

**Escalation.** No further technical escalation was needed: the compromised account's normal, legitimate purpose already granted visibility into reservation and payment data across many separate hotel properties on the shared platform, so one credential unlocked data belonging to dozens of unrelated hotel brands.

**Blast radius.** The unauthorized access ran from August 10, 2016 to March 9, 2017 — about seven months — before Sabre shut it down. Press and breach-tracking estimates put the exposure at roughly 1.3 million payment cards, though Sabre itself never published a precise figure. A 2020 multistate attorneys-general settlement found Sabre had also taken "months and in some cases years" to give hotels the information they needed to notify their own guests; Sabre paid $2.4 million to 28 states to resolve that investigation.

**Human factor.** A single shared credential, protected only by a static password, was able to reach payment data belonging to thousands of independent hotel businesses that had no visibility into, or control over, Sabre's own authentication practices.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on the SynXis account (AUTH-01); rate limiting and lockout on the login surface (AUTH-06); and a written incident and disclosure plan with a stated time-to-notify, given regulators' central complaint was the notification delay (OBSV-05).

```

**What happened.** On May 3, 2017, an email that appeared to come from someone the recipient knew invited them to open a shared "Google Docs" file. Clicking through led to a genuine Google OAuth consent screen asking the victim to grant a third-party app named "Google Docs" permission to read their email and manage their contacts; accepting it did not steal a password, but handed the attacker's app a live token that let it email the same lure to the victim's entire contact list.

**Entry.** The foothold was a phishing email that led to an OAuth consent grant, not a stolen credential — the victim authorized a malicious application directly through Google's own legitimate login and permissions flow.

**Escalation.** No further attacker action was required after the first grants: the malicious app used its own granted contacts access to automatically re-send the same lure, making the campaign self-propagating and abusing Google's legitimate OAuth-token and contacts features to spread.

**Blast radius.** Google said it disabled the offending app and pushed protections within about an hour of the campaign starting, and estimated fewer than 0.1% of Gmail users were affected; separate contemporaneous reporting put that figure at roughly one million accounts, though Google did not confirm an absolute count. No confirmed theft or misuse of message content was reported.

**Human factor.** Millions of users were primed to trust an authentic-looking Google permission screen arriving from a "familiar" sender, and Google's OAuth app-registration process at the time let an attacker name a malicious app "Google Docs" and request broad Gmail and contacts scopes with no more friction than any legitimate integration.

**What would have stopped it.** Review before authorizing new connected applications, rather than one click on a consent screen (VENDOR-03); scoping and expiring OAuth grants and reviewing unused ones (VENDOR-02); and user training that covers OAuth-consent phishing specifically, not just password phishing (HUMAN-05).

**What happened.** Researchers at Kromtech Security Research Center, scanning Shodan for exposed rsync backup services, found a backup server holding Bronx-Lebanon Hospital Center patient records open to anyone on the internet, with no username or password required. The backup was managed for the hospital by a third-party vendor, iHealth Innovations; exposed files included names, addresses, diagnoses, and highly sensitive details such as HIV status, domestic-violence and sexual-assault reports, and substance-abuse histories.

**Entry.** The rsync backup service was reachable directly from the internet and accepted connections with no authentication at all — a configuration researchers found simply by scanning Shodan for exposed rsync endpoints, not by exploiting any software flaw.

Kromtech reported the exposure responsibly and iHealth secured the server after being notified; no evidence of malicious access before discovery was reported.

**Human factor.** The hospital had delegated its backup infrastructure to a vendor and had no visibility into, or apparent verification of, how that vendor had configured a service holding some of its most sensitive patient data.

**What would have stopped it.** Keeping backup and database services off the public internet entirely (DATA-04); routinely enumerating what is actually internet-reachable rather than assuming it is not (CLOUD-01); and maintaining an inventory of vendors holding sensitive data with a basic security review of their configuration (VENDOR-01).

```

**What happened.** On May 12, 2017, the WannaCry worm began encrypting files on Windows computers worldwide and demanding a $300 bitcoin ransom, spreading on its own between machines with no user action required after the first infection on a network. It is estimated to have hit around 200,000 computers in more than 150 countries. The UK's National Health Service was the most severely disrupted single organization: the National Audit Office found at least 34% of English NHS trusts affected — 37 trusts directly infected and locked out of devices, another 44 disrupted as a precaution — forcing roughly 19,000 appointments and procedures to be cancelled. Telefónica, FedEx, Renault-Nissan factories, and Deutsche Bahn were among many other organizations hit; the US, UK, and allied governments would later attribute the attack to North Korea's Lazarus Group, in December 2017, after this quarter.

**Entry.** WannaCry spread through EternalBlue, an exploit for a flaw in Microsoft's SMBv1 file-sharing protocol (part of the MS17-010 bulletin, commonly associated with CVE-2017-0144). Microsoft had shipped a patch on March 14, 2017, two months before the attack; the exploit itself, believed developed by the US National Security Agency, had been published on April 14, 2017 by the group calling itself Shadow Brokers. Every NHS organization infected had unpatched or unsupported Windows systems, and the National Audit Office found that of the 88 (of 236) NHS trusts that had undergone a cybersecurity inspection before the attack, not one had passed.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial exploit: WannaCry scanned for other reachable machines with the same unpatched SMBv1 flaw and infected them automatically, spreading across flat internal networks and the open internet with no further attacker involvement.

**Blast radius.** The worm's spread was cut short within hours when security researcher Marcus Hutchins registered a domain name found in the malware, which acted as an unintentional kill switch. NHS England's own review put its cost to the health service at £92 million (about £20 million in lost output during the attack and £72 million in subsequent IT recovery). Global damage estimates as high as $4 billion have been widely cited but rest on modeled and extrapolated methodology rather than a confirmed tally; the attackers collected only around 52 bitcoin (roughly $140,000) in ransom, and WannaCry's payment-to-decryption mechanism was broken, so most who paid did not recover files.

**Human factor.** Two months elapsed between the patch's release and the outbreak, during which known-vulnerable, internet- and internally-reachable systems were left unpatched, including unsupported operating systems that could no longer receive a patch at all. The National Audit Office also found that basic perimeter controls, such as not exposing SMB to the internet, would independently have prevented infection regardless of patch status.

**What would have stopped it.** Regularly enumerating and closing unnecessary internet-facing exposure, including SMB (CLOUD-01); inventorying and retiring unsupported legacy systems that cannot be patched (CLOUD-09); and applying vendor-released security patches within a defined SLA, particularly on internet- or laterally-reachable services (CLOUD-NEW — a patch-management SLA control not yet in the checklist).

```

**What happened.** OneLogin, a single-sign-on and identity management provider used by roughly 2,000 companies in 44 countries, disclosed that an intruder used a compromised set of AWS API keys to access its AWS infrastructure starting around 2 a.m. Pacific on May 31, 2017. The attacker used the keys to create several new instances inside OneLogin's environment and query internal database tables.

**Entry.** The attacker authenticated to OneLogin's AWS API using a valid AWS access key, reaching it "from an intermediate host with another, smaller service provider" in the US; OneLogin never publicly explained how the key itself was originally obtained.

**Escalation.** The AWS key was scoped broadly enough to let the attacker provision new infrastructure and query production database tables directly — a single credential granting far more reach than any routine task should have needed, so no further escalation technique was required to reach sensitive tables.

**Blast radius.** The attacker accessed tables containing information about users, applications, and various categories of keys; OneLogin said it could not rule out that the attacker had also obtained the ability to decrypt encrypted fields in that data. OneLogin staff noticed unusual database activity around 9 a.m. Pacific — about seven hours after access began — and shut down the affected instance and revoked the keys within minutes, then told customers to generate new API keys and OAuth tokens, recycle any secrets stored in OneLogin, and rotate credentials. OneLogin did not publish a customer or record count.

**Human factor.** An AWS credential with reach into production database tables and the ability to spin up new infrastructure was usable from outside OneLogin's own network for roughly seven hours before anyone at an identity-security company noticed.

**What would have stopped it.** Scoping credentials to one job and one resource rather than broad account access (CRED-06); expiring credentials instead of long-lived keys (CRED-07); and alerting on abnormal database read volume or unexpected new infrastructure creation (OBSV-02).

**What happened.** On June 27, 2017, a wave of computers across Ukraine and then worldwide began displaying a ransom note and became unusable; the malware, dubbed NotPetya, was later determined to be a wiper that destroyed data with no real way to pay for recovery, disguised as ransomware. It hit Ukrainian government agencies, banks, the airport and metro system, and the Chernobyl radiation-monitoring network, then spread through the global operations of multinationals including shipping giant A.P. Moller-Maersk, pharmaceutical company Merck, FedEx's TNT Express unit, snack maker Mondelez, and materials firm Saint-Gobain.

**Entry.** Attackers had compromised the build and update server of M.E.Doc, a Ukrainian tax and accounting software used by most companies doing business in Ukraine, planting a backdoor as far back as April 14, 2017. On June 27, that backdoor was used to push a malicious update through M.E.Doc's legitimate, trusted update channel, so any machine running the software installed the wiper as if it were a routine vendor update.

**Escalation.** M.E.Doc's compromise cascaded outward to every organization that trusted its update channel; the wiper then spread inside each victim's network on its own, combining the EternalBlue and EternalRomance SMB exploits (the same exploit family used by WannaCry weeks earlier) with credential-harvesting techniques similar to Mimikatz to extract cached administrator credentials and move laterally, reaching fully patched machines on flat networks that shared credentials or trust with an already-infected host.

**Blast radius.** Maersk had to rebuild roughly 4,000 servers, 45,000 computers, and 2,500 applications, and halted port and logistics operations at terminals worldwide for days; the company later said the incident would cost it $200-300 million. Other named victims each described impacts in the hundreds of millions of dollars. Widely cited estimates put NotPetya's aggregate global damage at around $10 billion, which would make it among the costliest cyberattacks on record, but — like most such aggregate figures — this rests on compiled company disclosures and outside assessments rather than a single confirmed tally, and is treated here as a disputed, low-confidence estimate. The US, UK, and allied governments attributed the attack in February 2018 to Russia's military intelligence agency (the GRU); the US Department of Justice indicted named GRU officers in October 2020 — both after this quarter.

**Human factor.** Organizations across Ukraine and multinationals with Ukrainian operations extended the same automatic trust to M.E.Doc's update mechanism that they would to any signed vendor patch, while internally, flat networks and cached administrative credentials meant a single infected machine could reach systems well beyond where the update was ever installed.

**What would have stopped it.** Network segmentation that keeps a compromised host from reaching credentials or systems beyond its own segment (CLOUD-05); monitoring vendor advisories and having contractual visibility into a critical software vendor's own security practices (VENDOR-06); and applying vendor security patches — MS17-010 had been available since March 2017 — within a defined SLA (CLOUD-NEW, the same patch-management gap WannaCry exposed weeks earlier).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Microsoft broke its own policy of not patching unsupported products, issuing emergency MS17-010 patches for Windows XP, Server 2003, and Windows 8 on May 13, 2017 — one day after WannaCry began spreading — to blunt the outbreak.
- Independent security researcher Marcus Hutchins halted WannaCry's spread within hours of the outbreak by registering a domain name hardcoded into the malware, which acted as an unintentional kill switch.
- The WannaCry and NotPetya outbreaks drove a more forceful industry and CERT push, through May and June 2017, to disable legacy SMBv1 both at the network perimeter and internally, rather than merely recommending it.
- Following the May 3 Gmail worm, Google added anti-abuse detection for suspicious OAuth app behavior and moved to tighten review of third-party apps requesting broad Gmail and contacts scopes.
- Three separate incidents disclosed this quarter (Sabre, OneLogin, Bronx-Lebanon) sharpened scrutiny of single-factor remote access to shared platforms, broadly scoped cloud API keys, and vendor-managed backup services as recurring root causes rather than one-off failures.
- The Deep Root Analytics/RNC exposure became one of 2017's most-cited examples of default-public cloud storage risk, adding to industry pressure that eventually led Amazon Web Services to ship account-wide S3 "Block Public Access" controls in November 2018.
