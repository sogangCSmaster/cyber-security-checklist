# 2018 Q4 — Inherited Systems and Borrowed Trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-vendor — trust extended to a vendor, contractor, acquired IT estate, or open-source maintainer (AccuDoc's billing systems, Nordstrom's contractor, Marriott's inherited Starwood network, event-stream's new maintainer) opened more records this quarter than any single exploit technique
> - **New technique this quarter:** a malicious npm dependency that fingerprinted its target and stayed dormant everywhere except one victim's specific build environment, evading detection for over two months (event-stream/Copay)
> - **Recurring failure:** server-side authorization assumed rather than verified — being logged in was repeatedly treated as being authorized to see any record a function could return (Employees Retirement System of Texas, USPS)
> - **Records in this file:** 16

```

**What happened.** In October 2018, security researcher Vinny Troia of NightLion Security found an Amazon-hosted server belonging to sales-engagement startup Apollo that required no authentication and held a database of more than 200 million contact records assembled from LinkedIn, AngelList, Facebook, Salesforce, Twitter, and other sources. KrebsOnSecurity covered the find alongside two similar exposures Troia had located earlier the same year, using it to illustrate the murky line between a researcher and an opportunist working the same unsecured servers.

**Entry.** `entry/public-database` — the server ran with no password and no access control in front of it; anyone who found its address could query the full contact database directly, no credential or exploit required.

**Escalation.** None required — the exposed server was itself the asset; there was no further system to move into.

**Blast radius.** More than 200 million contact records — names, employers, job titles, and, for a large share, email addresses and other identifying details scraped or purchased from several platforms — were reachable for as long as the server stayed misconfigured. No evidence surfaced that anyone besides Troia accessed the data before Apollo secured it.

**Human factor.** A company assembling a very large, sensitive third-party contact database put it on cloud infrastructure with no authentication in front of it — the same pattern Troia had already found twice that year at other companies, in a period when exposed Amazon-hosted databases were routine enough that researchers were the ones finding them first.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating "nothing is publicly reachable unless decided" as a standing, regularly enumerated rule (CLOUD-01); keeping databases and search indexes off the public internet entirely (DATA-04); and ensuring no endpoint — including a database's own query interface — returns bulk user data without authentication (AUTH-03).

**What happened.** CNO Financial Group, parent of insurance carrier Bankers Life, reported a breach affecting 566,217 individuals — one of the largest healthcare-sector breaches disclosed in October 2018, per HHS breach-reporting data compiled by HIPAA Journal. Stolen employee credentials were used to log into company websites, exposing policyholder and insurance-applicant information.

**Entry.** `entry/stolen-credentials` — available reporting states only that "employee credentials were stolen and used to gain access to company websites"; whether the theft was through phishing, malware, or a reused password from an unrelated breach is not specified in the source available for this record.

**What happened.** The U.S. Department of Defense disclosed on October 12, 2018 that a single commercial vendor managing part of its travel services had been breached, exposing personal information and payment card data belonging to as many as 30,000 U.S. military and civilian personnel. DoD said it identified the breach on October 4, 2018, but that the underlying intrusion could have begun months earlier.

**Entry.** `entry/supply-chain-vendor` — the compromise happened inside a third-party travel-management company's systems, not the Pentagon's own network. **What would have stopped it.** An inventory of every vendor holding sensitive personnel data, with a defined access scope (VENDOR-01); time-boxed, separately monitored vendor access rather than open-ended trust (VENDOR-08); and treating bulk export or query of personnel records as a privileged, logged, alerting action wherever DoD could enforce it contractually (DATA-11).

entry: [entry/idor]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/pii, impact/financial-data]
factor: [factor/process-failure, factor/detection-failure]
scale: 1248263 people
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: 228
confidence: medium
controls: [AUTH-02, DATA-01, DATA-02]
sources:
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/1-25-million-records-exposed-in-employees-retirement-system-of-texas-data-breach/
```

**What happened.** A coding error introduced into the Employees Retirement System of Texas's member self-service portal on January 1, 2018 let a modified search on the "Annual Out-of-Pocket Premium" function return other members' information instead of only the logged-in user's own. An ERS member noticed the flaw on August 17, 2018, after a search unexpectedly returned personal data for 50 other members; ERS disabled the function immediately, and the breach became public around October 23, 2018.

**Entry.** `entry/idor` — the search function accepted a user-modified query and returned matching records without verifying the requester was authorized to see anyone but themselves: a direct object reference with no per-record authorization check, live in production for roughly eight months.

**Escalation.** None required. Once an authenticated member reached the flawed function, the query itself returned other members' names, Social Security numbers, and premium data — no further compromise was needed.

**Blast radius.** Up to 1,248,263 current and former members and beneficiaries were potentially affected (names, Social Security numbers, ERS member ID numbers, and premium-payment information). ERS's own account puts confirmed viewing at a small, accidental disclosure to 50 members before the function was disabled; the gap between "potentially reachable" and "actually viewed" was never fully reconciled in public reporting, which is why scale confidence here is medium rather than high.

**Human factor.** A code change shipped without a test verifying that one member's search could not return another member's data — the review and QA process for this function had no negative-authorization check, so the bug rode along in production for most of 2018 until an ordinary member doing routine business stumbled onto it.

**What would have stopped it.** Row-level authorization, default-deny, on every client-reachable table or function (DATA-01); a negative test proving one user cannot read another user's records (DATA-02); and authorization checked server-side, per object, on every request rather than relying on login alone (AUTH-02).

  - https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/cathay-pacific-slammed-for-security-failures-following-hack-which-exposed-9-4-million-people-worldwide
  - https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/24/cathay-pacific-passenger-data-stolen-breach
```

**What happened.** Cathay Pacific disclosed on October 24, 2018 that attackers had accessed passenger data belonging to up to 9.4 million people worldwide. The UK Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) later found unauthorized access dated back to around October 2014 and continued, in separate episodes, until Cathay Pacific identified it between March and May 2018; the airline notified the ICO on October 25, 2018.

**Entry.** `entry/unpatched-cve` — the ICO's investigation found Cathay Pacific had failed to patch a public-facing server against a vulnerability that had been publicly known for more than a decade, and was running outdated, unsupported operating systems on servers that processed personal data.

**Escalation.** Two separate intrusion episodes were identified. The ICO singled out Cathay's VPN, reachable by roughly 41,000 users with a username and password alone; the ICO stated directly that requiring MFA there would have stopped attackers from using any stolen credential to reach the data, which is why this is tagged as both lateral movement and a legacy-system bypass rather than a single clean exploit chain.

**Blast radius.** Personal data of 9.4 million people globally — including 111,578 UK residents — was exposed: names, nationalities, dates of birth, passport numbers (860,000), Hong Kong identity card numbers (about 245,000), phone numbers, addresses, and travel history. Database backups containing this data were also unencrypted. The ICO fined Cathay Pacific £500,000 (about US$640,000) in March 2020 — the maximum available under the pre-GDPR UK Data Protection Act 1998 regime that applied to the start of this intrusion.

**Human factor.** Patching a decade-old, publicly known vulnerability and rolling out MFA on VPN access were both deprioritized for years, and out-of-support operating systems were left running production workloads. The ICO's own characterization was a "catalogue of errors," not one sophisticated technique.

**What would have stopped it.** MFA on every account with VPN or production reach (AUTH-01); encryption of sensitive fields and backups at rest (DATA-07); and keeping internet-facing servers and their operating systems inside vendor support and patched against known, public CVEs within a defined window (CLOUD-10).

**What happened.** Radisson Hotel Group notified customers between October 30 and 31, 2018 that its Radisson Rewards loyalty program had been breached. The company said the intrusion occurred on September 11, 2018 and was discovered on October 1, 2018, and that it affected less than 10 percent of Radisson Rewards member accounts worldwide; Radisson declined to give an exact number.

  - https://www.theregister.com/2018/11/06/hsbc_security_broken/
```

**What happened.** HSBC Bank USA notified customers on November 2, 2018 that attackers had used credential stuffing — usernames and passwords harvested from breaches at unrelated companies — to log into some customers' online banking accounts between roughly October 4 and October 14, 2018.

**Entry.** `entry/credential-stuffing` — attackers replayed credentials already exposed in prior, unrelated breaches against HSBC's own login form. Because some customers had reused the same password at HSBC, valid-looking logins succeeded with no HSBC-side compromise required.

**Escalation.** None required — a successful login was the entire attack. Names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, account numbers, balances, and transaction histories were all visible from the authenticated session itself.

**Blast radius.** Reporting places confirmed unauthorized access at around 14,000 accounts (figures across outlets vary somewhat, hence medium confidence here); HSBC suspended online access for affected accounts, reset the associated credentials, and offered a year of identity-theft protection.

**Human factor.** The control that stops credential stuffing regardless of password reuse — MFA that a replayed password alone cannot satisfy — was not required for HSBC's US online banking logins at the time.

**What would have stopped it.** MFA on every account able to view financial data or move money (AUTH-01); rate limiting and breached-password screening on the login form (AUTH-06); and MFA that resists more than a password replay, such as number matching or a hardware key (AUTH-09).

sources:
  - https://blog.npmjs.org/post/180565383195/details-about-the-event-stream-incident
  - https://snyk.io/blog/a-post-mortem-of-the-malicious-event-stream-backdoor/
```

**What happened.** In September 2018, event-stream's original maintainer, Dominic Tarr — who had lost interest in the widely used npm utility package years earlier — handed publishing rights to a volunteer, "right9ctrl," who had offered to help maintain it. On September 9, 2018 the new maintainer published event-stream 3.3.6 with a new dependency, flatmap-stream, containing an AES-encrypted payload (decrypted using the package's own description field as the key) built to activate only inside the Copay bitcoin wallet's specific build environment and, once active, exfiltrate the private keys of any wallet holding more than 100 BTC or 1,000 BCH. npm's security team was alerted and removed both packages on November 26, 2018.

**Entry.** `entry/supply-chain-dependency` — the path into Copay's build was a legitimate `npm install` of event-stream, which by then transitively pulled in the malicious flatmap-stream package; no review at Copay caught the added, obfuscated dependency before it shipped in official Copay desktop releases 5.0.2 through 5.1.0.

**Escalation.** `escalation/supply-chain-cascade` — one compromised open-source package rode into every downstream project that installed it. The payload's narrow environment-fingerprinting kept it dormant, and largely undetected, everywhere except its one intended target, which is precisely what let it survive in the registry for over two months.

**Blast radius.** Builds from source between October 25 and November 26, 2018 could have produced a backdoored Copay app, and Copay confirmed the malicious code shipped in official releases 5.0.2–5.1.0. No confirmed dollar figure for stolen funds was ever made public; the targeting was narrow enough (100+ BTC or 1,000+ BCH holders) that it is not publicly established how many, if any, victims met the threshold before removal — hence `impact/credentials` rather than a claimed theft figure.

**Human factor.** npm's own post-mortem states plainly that "the attacker used social engineering" to obtain maintainer access. Tarr, an unpaid volunteer maintaining a project he had stopped using, extended trust to a stranger who offered to help, with no vetting process behind that handoff — the entire chain runs through one burned-out maintainer's understandable, unverified yes.

**What would have stopped it.** A cooldown before newly published dependency versions are adopted downstream (DEPS-03); surfacing and reviewing new transitive dependencies in pull requests rather than trusting the lockfile blindly (DEPS-08); and treating maintainer health and continuity as a real selection and monitoring criterion for critical dependencies (DEPS-09).

**What happened.** AccuDoc Solutions, the billing vendor for North Carolina-based Atrium Health, discovered that an unauthorized third party had accessed its databases between September 22 and September 29, 2018, and notified Atrium Health on October 1, 2018. Atrium's own systems and its own code were not the point of entry; patient billing records held by its vendor were.

**Entry.** `entry/supply-chain-vendor` — the attacker reached Atrium's patients' data by compromising AccuDoc's systems, not Atrium's. Neither AccuDoc nor Atrium has publicly disclosed how the intruder first got into AccuDoc's own network, which is the one significant unknown in an otherwise well-documented incident.

```

**What happened.** Marriott disclosed on November 30, 2018 that an unauthorized party had access to the Starwood guest reservation database — a system Marriott inherited through its 2016 acquisition of Starwood — for years before detection. Marriott's investigation traced the intrusion back to sometime in 2014, three years before Starwood was folded into Marriott; an internal security tool first flagged a suspicious attempt to query the guest reservation database on September 8, 2018, which triggered the investigation that uncovered the breach.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — Marriott has not publicly confirmed the initial infection vector. Investigators found a remote access trojan (RAT) and the credential-harvesting tool Mimikatz already resident on Starwood servers; later press reporting (citing US investigators) attributed the intrusion to actors linked to China's Ministry of State Security, but that attribution was never confirmed in a Marriott statement or a regulatory filing, so `actor/unknown` is used here rather than `actor/nation-state`.

**Escalation.** Mimikatz extracted credentials from system memory; attackers used a compromised administrator account — reused, undetected, for roughly four years — to run queries against the guest reservation database and move through the Starwood environment (`escalation/credential-reuse`, `escalation/lateral-movement`, `escalation/long-lived-credential`).

**Blast radius.** Marriott initially said up to 500 million guest records were affected, later revising the figure down to approximately 383 million unique guests — the range is given here because both figures were officially stated at different points. Exposed data included names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, passport numbers, Starwood Preferred Guest account details, dates of birth, and — for a smaller subset — unencrypted passport numbers and payment card numbers (many since expired). The UK ICO fined Marriott £18.4 million in 2020 (roughly US$23.8 million) for GDPR failures; Australia's privacy regulator separately secured an enforceable undertaking.

**Human factor.** A years-old intrusion in an acquired company's systems went undetected through the entire due-diligence and integration process of a major acquisition — nobody validated that Starwood's infrastructure was clean before connecting it to Marriott's, and the compromised administrator credential kept working the whole time because nothing forced its rotation or flagged its anomalous use.

**What would have stopped it.** Centralized logging and alerting on authentication and database-export activity from day one, not four years in (OBSV-01); credentials that expire rather than remaining valid indefinitely (CRED-07); and auditing an acquired company's systems for existing compromise before connecting them to the parent's network (VENDOR-07).

**What happened.** Morele.net, a major Polish online electronics retailer, disclosed in December 2018 that criminals had obtained personal data on more than 2 million customers from its online-store databases. Morele.net reported two related incidents to Poland's data protection authority (UODO): the first, in November 2018, covering customer names, delivery addresses, phone numbers, and account login information; a second, in December 2018, with a broader scope that included data from customer loan and installment-payment applications.

**Entry.** `entry/phishing` — per Morele.net's own account to UODO, the attacker used a phishing attack to obtain access, which was then used to harvest customer data. The company said it learned of the intrusion only after customers began reporting suspicious activity, not through its own monitoring.

**Escalation.** Not established beyond the initial access in the reporting available for this record. UODO's investigation focused less on how far the intruder moved internally and more on the fact that Morele.net's authentication and access controls were "ineffective" for the actual risk level and could not detect unusual network activity once the intruder was inside — which is why `escalation/none-required` is used here as the closest available tag rather than a confirmed finding.

**Blast radius.** More than 2 million customers had names, delivery addresses, phone numbers, and account credentials exposed in the first wave; the second wave added loan and installment-application data for an unspecified additional subset. Stolen phone numbers and order details were then used in a follow-on SMS phishing campaign against Morele.net's own customers, who received messages demanding a small "outstanding payment" through a fake payment page referencing their real, recent orders. In September 2019, UODO fined Morele.net PLN 2.83 million (about €660,000, roughly $726,000 at the time) — then the largest GDPR fine issued in Poland — citing insufficient technical and organizational measures; Morele.net said it would appeal.

**Human factor.** UODO's decision found that Morele.net's access and authentication controls were not matched to the risk its customer database represented, and that the company had no effective way to notice the anomalous access before its own customers started reporting the follow-on phishing messages.

**What would have stopped it.** Rate limiting and anomaly screening on account and admin authentication surfaces (AUTH-06); alerting on abnormal read or export volume from the customer database (OBSV-02); and security-awareness training reflecting current phishing technique for staff with access to bulk customer data (HUMAN-05).

**What happened.** A U.S. county government, identified in HHS breach reporting only as "Adams County," disclosed a health-plan data breach affecting 258,120 individuals — one of the largest healthcare-sector breaches reported in December 2018. The stated cause was a former employee who accessed a network server without authorization after leaving the county's employment; the specific state and department were not given in the source available for this record.

**Entry.** `entry/offboarding-failure` — the former employee's access to the network server had not been revoked when their employment ended, and they used that still-valid access rather than any new exploit or stolen credential.

**Escalation.** None required — the former employee's existing account permissions were already sufficient to reach the health-plan data on the server; no further compromise was needed or described.

**Blast radius.** 258,120 individuals covered by the county's health plan had protected health information exposed to the former employee's continued access. Beyond the individual count and the stated cause, the specific data fields, exposure window, and county location were not detailed in the source available for this record, which is why confidence here is medium.

**What would have stopped it.** A tracked joiner-mover-leaver process with a maximum revocation time enforced on exit (HUMAN-03); access that is inventoried, owned, and expires automatically when unused (AUTH-08); and centralized logging and alerting on authentication and server access so a departed employee's login would itself be an anomaly worth flagging (OBSV-01).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **npm's public post-mortem reframed maintainer handoffs as a trust decision.** npm published a detailed account of how event-stream's takeover happened and pulled both packages the day it was notified; the incident became a widely cited argument for treating an unsolicited offer to adopt an abandoned package as something requiring scrutiny, not just relief that a volunteer showed up.
- **A year-old bug report finally got fixed once a journalist called.** USPS patched the Informed Delivery/Informed Visibility API flaw shortly after KrebsOnSecurity's report; a researcher had privately reported the same weakness about a year earlier and never received a response, underscoring the gap a working, monitored vulnerability-disclosure channel (OBSV-06) is meant to close.
- **GDPR enforcement got its first real numbers.** The ICO's investigation into Cathay Pacific, opened this quarter, and its eventual £500,000 fine were among the earliest tests of enforcement power under the regime that had only taken effect in May 2018 — an early signal that unpatched public-facing servers and MFA-free VPNs would carry a direct regulatory cost.
- **HHS's own tallies showed email-account takeovers becoming routine, not exceptional.** October–December 2018 HHS OCR breach data included several single- or few-mailbox Office 365 compromises (HealthEquity, Elizabethtown Community Hospital, Kent County Community Mental Health Authority, among others) that quarter, ahead of the industry settling on mailbox-level MFA and anomalous-login alerting as a baseline healthcare control.
- **DOJ and Treasury went after SamSam's operators and its cash-out network.** On November 28, 2018, the Justice Department indicted two Iranian nationals over the SamSam ransomware campaign, while Treasury's OFAC separately sanctioned two Iran-based individuals accused of laundering SamSam's ransom payments — one of the first uses of sanctions power against specific cryptocurrency addresses.
- **Several of the quarter's biggest breaches were disclosed with no named technical cause.** Marriott, Quora, and AccuDoc all notified regulators and customers without publicly stating the initial vulnerability or access method — a reminder that meeting legal disclosure obligations and publishing a useful post-mortem are not the same thing, and a limit on how much even a well-sourced record in this corpus can specify.
