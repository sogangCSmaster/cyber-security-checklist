# 2018 Q2 — Supply-Chain Trust Meets GDPR's Debut

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-vendor — a compromised or malicious third-party script/vendor was the path in for two of the ten records ([24]7.ai's embedded chat platform and the Inbenta-hosted Magecart skimmer on Ticketmaster)
> - **New technique this quarter:** A JavaScript payment-page skimmer riding inside a trusted, legitimately-loaded third-party script (Magecart via Inbenta) — harvesting card data as a customer types it, rather than stealing it from a database afterward
> - **Recurring failure:** Long, silent dwell before anyone noticed — Panera (8 months), MyHeritage (~8 months), Exactis (~2 months), the [24]7.ai chain (~6 months to public notice) — and in most cases it was an outsider, not the victim's own monitoring, who surfaced it
> - **Records in this file:** 10

```yaml
sources:

  - https://www.csoonline.com/article/565050/panera-bread-blew-off-breach-report-for-8-months-leaked-millions-of-customer-records.html
```

**What happened.** Panerabread.com ran a customer-facing API that returned name, email, physical address, birthday, loyalty account number, and the last four digits of a stored payment card in plain text for any account whose ID was requested. Security researcher Dylan Houlihan privately reported the flaw to Panera's director of information security on 2 August 2017; the director's first response called the report likely fraudulent. The endpoint was still live when Krebs on Security contacted Panera's CIO on 2 April 2018, and the site was taken offline within two hours.

**Entry.** The API accepted a numeric, sequential account identifier with no authentication and no per-object authorization check, so incrementing the number in the URL returned the next customer's record.

**Escalation.** None required — the endpoint was the data itself; no further pivoting was needed once the URL pattern was known.

**Blast radius.** The exact count is disputed. Panera's own count, after the story broke, was fewer than 10,000 records; Krebs's analysis of the sequential IDs indexed by the site put the true number above 37 million. Both figures are given because neither is independently confirmed, and Panera's fix (retroactively requiring login) also made the original scope harder to verify after the fact.

**Human factor.** Panera's information-security director dismissed a specific, correctly diagnosed report from an outside researcher as fraudulent and then let it sit unresolved for eight months despite repeated prompting from the researcher.

**What would have stopped it.** Per-object authorization on the endpoint so one account could not enumerate another's (AUTH-03); non-sequential, unguessable identifiers so the pattern itself couldn't be walked (INPUT-09); and a real vulnerability-disclosure channel with an SLA, so a correct report from an outsider doesn't die with one dismissive reply (OBSV-06).


**What happened.** [24]7.ai, a third-party vendor whose chat-widget software was embedded on the payment pages of Best Buy, Delta Air Lines, Sears, and Kmart, was compromised between 26 September and 12 October 2017. Malware present in [24]7.ai's software let attackers capture payment card data — name, address, card number, CVV, expiration date — as customers typed it into the chat-connected checkout flow. [24]7.ai notified its clients in March 2018; Delta and Sears disclosed to their own customers on 4 April, Best Buy the next day.

**Entry.** Not publicly detailed. [24]7.ai has not disclosed how attackers first got into its own network; what is confirmed is that malware embedded in its software was the vehicle for capturing card data on client sites.

**Escalation.** A single compromise of the vendor's platform reached every retailer that had embedded [24]7.ai's widget on a payment page — the vendor's trusted position was the multiplier, not any further technical pivoting.

**Blast radius.** No combined total was ever published. Sears said fewer than 100,000 of its customers' cards were involved; Delta described a subset it later characterized as potentially several hundred thousand; Best Buy said a small fraction of its customers. All three stressed most of their traffic did not use the chat feature.

**Human factor.** Three large retailers each embedded a third party's live script directly on a page that handled full card data, with no apparent control limiting what that script could read or send off-page — and it took roughly six months from containment for any of them to tell their customers.

**What would have stopped it.** An inventory of which vendors' code runs on payment pages and what data it can reach (VENDOR-01); isolating or minimizing third-party scripts on checkout flows, e.g. running the widget in a sandboxed iframe with no access to card fields (VENDOR-05); and a contractual notification clock so a vendor's breach reaches the client's customers in days, not months (VENDOR-06).

```yaml
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/twitter-admits-recording-plaintext-passwords-in-internal-logs-just-like-github/
```

**What happened.** Twitter disclosed that a bug in its password-hashing pipeline wrote passwords to an internal log before the hashing step completed, leaving them readable in plaintext in a system Twitter's own engineers and staff could access. Twitter found the bug itself, removed the logged passwords, and asked all users to change their password as a precaution.

**Entry.** Not an external intrusion — a logic error in Twitter's own authentication code routed the pre-hash password value into a debug/internal log instead of only the resulting hash.

**Escalation.** None required — the log was already internally readable; the finding was that it should never have contained the value at all.

**Blast radius.** Twitter said it had no evidence the plaintext values left its systems or were viewed maliciously, and did not disclose how many accounts' passwords were actually captured in the log versus how long the bug had been live; the precautionary password-reset advisory went to the full user base (reported at the time as roughly 330-336 million accounts).

**Human factor.** Code handling a password on its way to being hashed was also allowed to write that value to a general logging path, and no log-scrubbing or secrets-in-logs check caught it before it reached production — a process gap, not a malicious act.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating a log as a place a secret can leak from, same as source or config (CRED-01), and structured logging with field-level scrubbing so an authentication value can never be written in full (OBSV-04).


**What happened.** Cisco's Talos group disclosed a modular malware family, VPNFilter, that had quietly infected at least 500,000 small-office/home routers and network-attached storage devices — Linksys, MikroTik, Netgear, TP-Link, and QNAP models among them — across at least 54 countries, spreading since as early as 2016. Talos assessed with high confidence that the campaign was linked to APT28 ("Fancy Bear"), and the FBI obtained a court order to seize a domain used as one of the malware's command-and-control fallback mechanisms.

**Entry.** VPNFilter compromised devices using known, published vulnerabilities and factory-default credentials that owners had never changed — Talos noted that most targeted devices, especially older models, had straightforward public exploits or default logins available.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial device compromise — each router or NAS was independently exploitable, and once accessed, the malware had the persistence and traffic visibility it needed on that device without further pivoting.

**Blast radius.** At least 500,000 devices in 54 countries at the time of disclosure, with capabilities including credential and traffic theft, monitoring of Modbus SCADA traffic passing through infected gateways, and a destructive "kill" module able to brick a device; no mass bricking event was confirmed as of this disclosure.

**Human factor.** Consumer and small-office router/NAS owners never changed factory-default credentials and never applied firmware updates for years-old, publicly known vulnerabilities, and device vendors shipped nothing that forced either.

**What would have stopped it.** Not exposing device admin interfaces to the open internet (CLOUD-08); and — a gap the existing checklist does not yet name — network-device vendors forcing a unique credential on first setup and shipping automatic security-patch updates by default (AUTH-NEW: consumer/SOHO network devices must not ship with a reusable default credential and must auto-apply security patches).


**What happened.** An external security researcher found a file named "myheritage" on a private server outside the company and reported it to MyHeritage's CISO on 4 June 2018. It contained the email addresses and per-user hashed passwords of 92,283,889 accounts created up to 26 October 2017, which MyHeritage identified as originating from its own systems. Family trees, DNA data, and payment information lived on separate, segregated systems and were not in the file.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. MyHeritage has not said how the data left its systems or who took it; the incident became known only because a researcher happened to find the resulting file on an unrelated external server.

**Escalation.** None evident from the artifact itself — the exposed file was a flat export of the account table (email plus hash), not evidence of a multi-stage compromise — but because the original access method is unknown, whether escalation was needed to obtain it is likewise unknown.

**Blast radius.** 92,283,889 user accounts' email addresses and hashed passwords, current as of an October 2017 cutoff; MyHeritage said it found no evidence the data had been used.

**Human factor.** Roughly eight months passed between the apparent breach date and the company learning about it, and it learned only because an outsider stumbled on the file elsewhere, not through any detection of its own. MyHeritage also acknowledged it did not yet offer two-factor authentication and fast-tracked it in response.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on accounts so a stolen password hash alone isn't enough (AUTH-01), and centralized logging with alerting on abnormal data access or export volume that might have caught the original exfiltration closer to when it happened (OBSV-01, OBSV-02).


**What happened.** South Korean exchange Coinrail detected unauthorized withdrawals in the early hours of 10 June 2018 and immediately put the platform into maintenance mode. Roughly 30% of the tokens it held — reported at around $40 million, including large amounts of Pundi X (NPXS), Aston X, and Dent — were taken. **What would have stopped it.** Alerting on abnormal outbound transaction volume (OBSV-02); and — a gap the checklist does not yet name for custodial crypto platforms — capping hot-wallet balances and sweeping to cold storage on a fixed schedule so no single compromise can reach the full balance (DATA-NEW: hot-wallet exposure is capped, swept to cold storage on schedule, and reconciled continuously).


**What happened.** Ticketmaster found malicious code running inside a customer-support chat product supplied and hosted by Inbenta Technologies and embedded on Ticketmaster's own sites. The script — a Magecart-style skimmer — read name, address, email, phone number, payment details, and Ticketmaster login credentials as customers entered them, and sent the data to a third party. Ticketmaster disabled the script on discovery in late June 2018. International customers who purchased or attempted to purchase tickets between September 2017 and late June 2018 were potentially affected; UK customers specifically, between February and June 2018.

**Entry.** A JavaScript file Inbenta had customized for Ticketmaster was modified to add skimming code; Ticketmaster's own infrastructure and code were not directly compromised — the path in was the trusted third-party script loaded on the checkout page.

**Escalation.** None needed beyond the initial script modification — running inside the customer's browser on the payment page, the skimmer already had access to every field the customer typed, and Ticketmaster's position as the site loading that script is what let the compromise reach its customers (a supply-chain cascade from Inbenta to Ticketmaster to Ticketmaster's own customer base).

**Blast radius.** An estimated 40,000 UK customers had payment details taken; the international exposure window was broader (September 2017-June 2018) but no single confirmed global total was published. The UK Information Commissioner's Office later fined Ticketmaster UK Limited £1.25 million (about $1.7 million) for failing to protect customer payment data.

**Human factor.** Ticketmaster allowed a third party's live, modifiable script to run unrestricted on pages that captured full payment card data, with no apparent control limiting what that script could read or where it could send data, and the skimmer ran for months before detection.

**What would have stopped it.** Minimizing, isolating, or sandboxing third-party scripts on payment pages so a compromised widget cannot read card fields (VENDOR-05); pinning or self-hosting any script loaded from a URL the company doesn't control, with integrity checks (DEPS-06); and alerting on abnormal outbound data flows from the checkout page (OBSV-02).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **GDPR took effect 25 May 2018.** Within hours, Max Schrems's newly formed advocacy group NOYB filed complaints against Google (targeting Android) and Facebook/Instagram/WhatsApp over "forced consent" under Article 7(4) — the first real-world test of the regulation's complaint mechanism, arriving before any breach-notification case had even been tested.
- **MyHeritage's disclosure landed nine days after GDPR's start** and was notified to European regulators under the new regime; the company also used the incident to fast-track two-factor authentication it had not previously offered — an example of a GDPR-era deadline accelerating security work that had otherwise stalled.
- **Cisco Talos's VPNFilter research led to an FBI-obtained court order** seizing a command-and-control domain, plus a public call for owners of consumer and small-office routers and NAS devices to reboot them, reviving attention on unpatched, default-credentialed home network equipment as a nation-state staging ground.
- **Panera Bread's eight-month non-response to a private vulnerability report**, publicized alongside similar stories this quarter, renewed pressure on companies without a published disclosure channel to stand one up rather than leave researchers to escalate through the press.
- **The run of third-party/vendor breaches this quarter** ([24]7.ai affecting Best Buy, Delta, and Sears/Kmart, and the Inbenta-hosted Magecart skimmer on Ticketmaster) sharpened industry focus on what a script loaded from a vendor can read on a payment page, feeding into later PCI guidance on securing e-commerce checkout pages against JavaScript skimmers.
- **Twitter's self-disclosed plaintext-password logging bug** reinforced existing guidance — echoed after similar prior incidents at other platforms — that authentication secrets must never reach an application log, prompting renewed attention to log-scrubbing and structured-logging practices industry-wide.
