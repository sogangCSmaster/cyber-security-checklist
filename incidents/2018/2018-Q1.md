# 2018 Q1 — Vendor Trust, Platform Oversharing, Silicon Flaws

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/exposed-service and inherited third-party credentials — internet-facing remote access opened the quarter's healthcare ransomware wave (Hancock Health's vendor-RDP compromise, and the wider SamSam campaign class behind Allscripts).
> - **New technique this quarter:** Olympic Destroyer, a wiper built with forensic artifacts researchers later found had been deliberately planted to misdirect attribution toward the wrong nation-state.
> - **Recurring failure:** systems and access nobody had re-reviewed since they were first granted — a shut-down subsidiary's forgotten S3 bucket (FedEx/Bongo), a deprecated booking platform (Orbitz), and standing vendor credentials (Hancock Health).

**What happened.** Charles River Medical Associates, a small Massachusetts primary-care practice, was one of five providers that reported a lost or stolen portable device to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services in January 2018 — the largest of the five, affecting 9,387 patients. HIPAA Journal's compilation of that month's Office for Civil Rights breach filings groups it among 21 healthcare breaches reported that month, the majority attributed to human error rather than hacking.

**Entry.** A portable electronic device holding patient records was lost or stolen; the public reporting available does not specify which, or where the device was last in staff possession.

**Escalation.** None required. Whoever ended up with the device could read whatever was stored on it directly, with no further technical step needed.

**Blast radius.** 9,387 patients' records were on the device. HIPAA Journal's report noted that four of the five loss-or-theft incidents it tracked that month, including this one, involved portable devices, and that "those incidents could have been avoided if encryption had been used" — implying this device was not encrypted.

**Human factor.** A practice-owned device carrying thousands of patients' records left the building without the encryption that would have made its loss a non-event — an ordinary, common failure mode next to the quarter's nation-state and ransomware cases, and one HHS's own January tally shows was the dominant cause of that month's healthcare breaches.

**What would have stopped it.** Sensitive fields encrypted at rest would have made the device's loss immaterial regardless of what was on it (DATA-07); a checklist for sensitive operations — here, taking patient data off-site on a portable device — rather than relying on individual judgment addresses the process gap (HUMAN-07); and data minimization, so no single portable device holds more patient records than a specific task needs, would have shrunk what this one loss actually exposed (DATA-09).

  - https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2018/01/03/processor-vulnerabilities-meltdown-and-spectre
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meltdown_(security_vulnerability)
```

**What happened.** Google Project Zero and academic researchers independently found that speculative execution, a performance feature built into nearly every CPU shipped since the mid-1990s, let a running process read memory it was never authorized to see, including kernel memory and other processes' data. Coordinated disclosure, originally planned for 9 January, was moved up to 3 January 2018 after press speculation forced an early release. Two related flaw families were named: Meltdown (Intel-specific, CVE-2017-5754) and Spectre (nearly universal, CVE-2017-5753 and CVE-2017-5715).

**Entry.** There is no single victim network here — the entry tag marks disclosure timing rather than an intrusion. The closest available tag, zero-day, is used loosely: operating-system and firmware mitigations were still incomplete and rolling out across the industry when the flaw became public.

**Escalation.** None required, and that is the finding: a successful exploit reads arbitrary privileged memory directly from unprivileged code. There is no separate step to reach the sensitive data — reading it is the entire attack.

**Blast radius.** No real-world exploitation was ever publicly confirmed. The blast radius that materialized was operational: emergency kernel patches (Linux KPTI, Windows KVA Shadow, macOS updates), browser mitigations such as Chrome's Site Isolation, and CPU microcode updates industry-wide, with measurable performance costs on I/O-heavy workloads and particular concern for multi-tenant cloud platforms where unrelated customers' code runs on shared hardware.

**Human factor.** None identified as a single mistake — this was two decades of an industry-wide architectural assumption, that speculative execution's timing side effects were not a security boundary, going untested until researchers checked it directly.

**What would have stopped it.** This is a hardware design class, not a chain a single control breaks. Segmentation between tenants on shared infrastructure limits the value of a cross-tenant read (CLOUD-05); and a tested, staged rollout process for vendor-issued microcode and kernel mitigations (CLOUD-10) — since Intel's first microcode patches caused enough reboot instability that Intel had to pull and reissue them within weeks.

  - https://www.outlookindia.com/national/uidai-registers-fir-against-tribune-reporter-over-aadhaar-data-breach-report-news-306508
```

**What happened.** On 3 January 2018, The Tribune reported that its reporter had paid an anonymous WhatsApp seller Rs 500 (about $8) and, within roughly 20 minutes, received a working login for a portal built for Aadhaar enrolment operators. That login returned the full demographic record — name, address, PIN code, photo, phone number, and email — for any of India's more than 1.1 billion enrolled Aadhaar numbers on request, and a further Rs 300 bought software to print a physical Aadhaar card for any number queried. UIDAI, the agency running India's national biometric ID system, disputed that this amounted to a database breach and filed a police complaint against the reporter and the newspaper; the newspaper stood by its reporting.

**Entry.** The Tribune traced the access to Village-Level Enterprise (VLE) operators — locally contracted agents previously authorized to enrol citizens for Aadhaar cards. In November 2017, UIDAI restricted new enrolment to banks and post offices, cutting these VLEs out of paid work; Tribune's sources described the underlying access-reselling scheme as having run for some months by the time of publication, without a precise start date. Facing lost income once formally cut out of the program, some of an estimated one lakh (100,000) such operators are suspected of having kept using, or resold, credentials that should have been deactivated when their role ended.

**Escalation.** None required. Whoever held a working "Enrolment Agency Administrator" login could query any Aadhaar number directly through the legitimate portal; no additional technical step was needed to reach full demographic records.

**Blast radius.** The Tribune verified the mechanism worked by successfully querying details for numbers its reporter did not already know, but could not and did not determine how many of the 1.1 billion enrolled records were actually queried by the sellers' other customers before access was cut off. UIDAI disputed that any breach occurred at all and maintained that no centralized biometric data — fingerprints or iris scans — was reachable through this portal. This record uses 1.1 billion as the theoretical population the access method could reach, not a confirmed extraction count, and sets confidence low given the single-investigation sourcing and the regulator's outright denial.

**Human factor.** A large population of contracted operators lost their intended access on paper when the enrolment program changed, but not in practice — their credentials kept working, and a market for reselling that access formed once their legitimate income dried up.

**What would have stopped it.** Access inventoried, owned, and expiring automatically when a role changes is the direct fix for credentials that outlived their purpose (AUTH-08); tracking joiners, movers, and leavers with a maximum revocation time would have caught the November 2017 policy change as a trigger to deactivate VLE logins (HUMAN-03); and credentials with a hard expiry, rather than an indefinite lifetime, limit how long a forgotten account stays useful to resell (CRED-07).

```

**What happened.** At around 9:30 p.m. on 11 January 2018, SamSam ransomware encrypted files across multiple information systems at Hancock Regional Hospital in Greenfield, Indiana. The hospital declared an internal disaster, reverted to paper charting, and paid the ransom rather than wait out a slower backup restoration.

**Entry.** Attackers logged into a hospital server through Remote Desktop Protocol, which was enabled and reachable from the open internet, using the administrative login credentials of an account the hospital had created for a hardware vendor's remote support access.

**Escalation.** That vendor account carried administrative rights, which the attackers used with remote-execution tooling to push SamSam onto other machines across the hospital's network — an overscoped credential and a flat network doing the rest of the work once the RDP door was open.

**Blast radius.** Forensic review found no evidence that patient data was exfiltrated; SamSam operators of this era were focused on encryption for ransom, not theft. The hospital paid four bitcoin (about $55,000 at the time) and had critical systems back to normal by 15 January, four days after the attack began.

**Human factor.** A vendor's remote-access account was provisioned with more privilege than its purpose needed, left standing indefinitely, and reachable through a service exposed directly to the internet — three separate, individually common decisions that combined into a single path from the public internet to hospital-wide encryption.

**What would have stopped it.** Vendor access that is time-boxed and separately monitored would have caught the standing, overscoped credential (VENDOR-08); access inventoried and expiring when unused closes the same gap (AUTH-08); and keeping admin interfaces off the public internet would have removed the RDP exposure that made the stolen credential usable from outside at all (CLOUD-08).

**What happened.** SamSam ransomware hit two Allscripts data centers, in Raleigh and Charlotte, North Carolina, on 18 January 2018. Allscripts is a cloud vendor for electronic health records and e-prescribing; the outage cut off record and prescribing access for an estimated 1,500 client practices, about 3% of its customer base, for up to a week.

The operational impact fell largely on downstream customers: practices that had done nothing wrong on their own systems lost access to patient records and e-prescribing for days, in at least one case forcing a practice to turn away patients because staff could not reach charts for over 8,500 people.

**Human factor.** Allscripts' security operations center caught the intrusion fast — abnormal activity at 2 a.m., confirmed ransomware by 6 a.m. — but a single SaaS vendor's ransomware incident became, in effect, an incident for every practice that depended on it, with no visibility or control of their own into the vendor's segmentation or recovery time.

**What would have stopped it.** Segmentation between environments, so one data center's compromise does not reach the other, addresses Allscripts' own chain (CLOUD-05); restore-tested backups would have shortened the week-long recovery (DATA-08); and an inventory of every third party holding your data or running your workflow is what would have let the affected practices understand and plan for this concentration of risk beforehand (VENDOR-01).

**What happened.** In the early hours of 26 January 2018, roughly 523 million NEM tokens were moved out of Coincheck, a Japanese cryptocurrency exchange, to an attacker-controlled account. Reported values at the time ranged from about $530 million to $547 million depending on the exchange rate used; this record uses the more commonly cited $530 million figure and flags the range given the dispute. About 260,000 customers held the affected NEM balances.

Japan's Financial Services Agency issued business improvement orders and inspected the exchange; Coincheck later reimbursed affected customers from its own funds. Attribution was never conclusively established; this record does not repeat unconfirmed claims about which country the attackers operated from.

**Human factor.** A phishing email reached and was opened by an employee, and the organization had made a deliberate, staffing-driven decision to keep a large operational balance in a hot wallet without the second-key control that would have required more than one compromised login to move funds.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on accounts with production reach raises the cost of turning a phished endpoint into wallet access (AUTH-01); a second person required for high-impact actions, the multisig Coincheck lacked, would have stopped a single compromised machine from moving the full balance (HUMAN-02); and credentials scoped to one job, one resource limit how much a single compromised endpoint can reach in the first place (CRED-06).

```

**What happened.** Strava, a fitness-tracking app, published a global "heatmap" in November 2017 built from roughly a billion users' activities and three trillion GPS data points. On 27–28 January 2018, Australian university student Nathan Ruser noticed that in conflict zones and remote areas with otherwise little foot traffic — parts of Syria, Afghanistan, and Somalia among them — clusters and running-route patterns on the public map lined up precisely with the shape and location of military bases, supply routes, and patrol paths, because military personnel using Strava were often the only source of tracked activity in those areas.

**Entry.** Individual soldiers and other personnel had legitimately used Strava to track their own runs and rides, and — by default or by choice — left that activity shared publicly rather than opting into Strava's private mode or drawing a privacy zone around sensitive locations. No account was compromised and no single policy was violated by any one user; each person's own data was exposed by their own, individually reasonable-seeming choice.

**Escalation.** Strava's heatmap feature aggregated millions of individually unremarkable activity shares into one browsable global visualization. That aggregation is what turned scattered personal fitness data into a strategically useful map: no single soldier's shared jog was sensitive by itself, but the pattern formed by many of them together clearly outlined a base's perimeter and a patrol's routine.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed follow-on attack was reported, but the exposure itself was the finding: locations and activity patterns for personnel at U.S. and allied bases in Iraq, Syria, Afghanistan, and elsewhere were identifiable, including what appeared to be a main supply route for coalition forces in Syria. The U.S. and other militaries opened reviews of personnel device and app policies; Strava did not overhaul the heatmap's privacy handling until mid-March 2018, restricting street-level detail to registered users.

**Human factor.** Neither the individual users nor Strava did anything most people would call reckless in isolation — sharing a workout publicly is the product's normal use. What was missing was any policy, from the militaries involved or from Strava, addressing what happens when thousands of individually low-risk disclosures are aggregated into one place.

**What would have stopped it.** A policy restricting or disabling public activity-sharing and heatmap participation for personnel and devices at operational sites is not yet a named control in this checklist and is recorded here as a gap (HUMAN-NEW); data minimization — not retaining and republishing years of granular, individually identifiable route data in an aggregate public product — would have reduced how precisely any single location could be reconstructed (DATA-09); and a clear channel for outside researchers to flag this kind of aggregate exposure before it goes public could have let Strava or military privacy teams act before Ruser's finding went viral (OBSV-06).

**What would have stopped it.** Treating anything the browser can read as public, with secrets kept server-side, closes the exact gap here (CRED-02); and short-lived, revocable, rotating sessions would have limited how much a leaked token was worth even if the exposure had gone undetected longer (AUTH-04).

**Human factor.** Swisscom extended a sales partner standing, high-volume query access to its full customer database without multi-factor authentication or a query-rate limit, so a single stolen password was enough to reach data on 10% of the country's population.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on the partner's login would have made the stolen password alone insufficient (AUTH-01); treating bulk record access as a privileged, logged, alerting action — which is exactly what Swisscom added after the fact by blocking high-volume queries — would have caught or blocked this in progress (DATA-11); and time-boxed, separately monitored vendor access limits how much a compromised partner credential can reach even before misuse is detected (VENDOR-08).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **The SEC issued formal cybersecurity-disclosure guidance on 21 February 2018** (Release No. 33-10459), clarifying that public companies must disclose material cyber risks and incidents in a timely way and addressing insider-trading exposure around undisclosed incidents. Source: https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2018-22
- **Meltdown and Spectre triggered emergency mitigations industry-wide** — Linux KPTI, Windows KVA Shadow, and Chrome Site Isolation among them — alongside CPU microcode updates; Intel's first microcode patches caused reboot instability and had to be pulled back within weeks, a reminder to test firmware-level patches before fleet-wide rollout. Source: https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2018/01/03/processor-vulnerabilities-meltdown-and-spectre
- **The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center issued a public service announcement on 21 February 2018** (I-022118-PSA) on the ongoing rise in W-2 and business-email-compromise phishing, recommending out-of-band verification for any executive request to send payroll or tax data. Source: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2018/PSA180221
- **Drupal released a critical remote-code-execution patch, SA-CORE-2018-002** ("Drupalgeddon2," CVE-2018-7600, CVSS 24/25) on 28 March 2018, ahead of the mass-exploitation wave that followed in the second quarter; sites that patched immediately within this quarter avoided it. Source: https://www.drupal.org/sa-core-2018-002
- **Facebook announced platform changes on 21 March 2018** in direct response to the Cambridge Analytica disclosures: auditing apps with pre-2014 access to large amounts of data, automatically revoking an app's access after 90 days of disuse, restricting new apps to name, profile photo, and email by default, and expanding its bug bounty to cover data-misuse reports. Source: https://about.fb.com/news/2018/03/cracking-down-on-platform-abuse/
