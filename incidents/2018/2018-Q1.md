# 2018 Q1 — Vendor Trust, Platform Oversharing, Silicon Flaws

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/exposed-service and inherited third-party credentials — internet-facing remote access opened the quarter's healthcare ransomware wave (Hancock Health's vendor-RDP compromise, and the wider SamSam campaign class behind Allscripts).
> - **New technique this quarter:** Olympic Destroyer, a wiper built with forensic artifacts researchers later found had been deliberately planted to misdirect attribution toward the wrong nation-state.
> - **Recurring failure:** systems and access nobody had re-reviewed since they were first granted — a shut-down subsidiary's forgotten S3 bucket (FedEx/Bongo), a deprecated booking platform (Orbitz), and standing vendor credentials (Hancock Health).
> - **Records in this file:** 11

dwell_days: unknown

sources:
  - https://meltdownattack.com/
  - https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2018/01/03/processor-vulnerabilities-meltdown-and-spectre
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meltdown_(security_vulnerability)
```

**What happened.** Google Project Zero and academic researchers independently found that speculative execution, a performance feature built into nearly every CPU shipped since the mid-1990s, let a running process read memory it was never authorized to see, including kernel memory and other processes' data. Coordinated disclosure, originally planned for 9 January, was moved up to 3 January 2018 after press speculation forced an early release. Two related flaw families were named: Meltdown (Intel-specific, CVE-2017-5754) and Spectre (nearly universal, CVE-2017-5753 and CVE-2017-5715).

**Entry.** There is no single victim network here — the entry tag marks disclosure timing rather than an intrusion. The closest available tag, zero-day, is used loosely: operating-system and firmware mitigations were still incomplete and rolling out across the industry when the flaw became public.

**Escalation.** None required, and that is the finding: a successful exploit reads arbitrary privileged memory directly from unprivileged code. There is no separate step to reach the sensitive data — reading it is the entire attack.

**Blast radius.** No real-world exploitation was ever publicly confirmed. The blast radius that materialized was operational: emergency kernel patches (Linux KPTI, Windows KVA Shadow, macOS updates), browser mitigations such as Chrome's Site Isolation, and CPU microcode updates industry-wide, with measurable performance costs on I/O-heavy workloads and particular concern for multi-tenant cloud platforms where unrelated customers' code runs on shared hardware.

**Human factor.** None identified as a single mistake — this was two decades of an industry-wide architectural assumption, that speculative execution's timing side effects were not a security boundary, going untested until researchers checked it directly.

**What would have stopped it.** This is a hardware design class, not a chain a single control breaks. Segmentation between tenants on shared infrastructure limits the value of a cross-tenant read (CLOUD-05); and a tested, staged rollout process for vendor-issued microcode and kernel mitigations (CLOUD-10) — since Intel's first microcode patches caused enough reboot instability that Intel had to pull and reissue them within weeks.

```

**What happened.** At around 9:30 p.m. on 11 January 2018, SamSam ransomware encrypted files across multiple information systems at Hancock Regional Hospital in Greenfield, Indiana. The hospital declared an internal disaster, reverted to paper charting, and paid the ransom rather than wait out a slower backup restoration.

**Entry.** Attackers logged into a hospital server through Remote Desktop Protocol, which was enabled and reachable from the open internet, using the administrative login credentials of an account the hospital had created for a hardware vendor's remote support access.

**Escalation.** That vendor account carried administrative rights, which the attackers used with remote-execution tooling to push SamSam onto other machines across the hospital's network — an overscoped credential and a flat network doing the rest of the work once the RDP door was open.

**Blast radius.** Forensic review found no evidence that patient data was exfiltrated; SamSam operators of this era were focused on encryption for ransom, not theft. The hospital paid four bitcoin (about $55,000 at the time) and had critical systems back to normal by 15 January, four days after the attack began.

**Human factor.** A vendor's remote-access account was provisioned with more privilege than its purpose needed, left standing indefinitely, and reachable through a service exposed directly to the internet — three separate, individually common decisions that combined into a single path from the public internet to hospital-wide encryption.

**What would have stopped it.** Vendor access that is time-boxed and separately monitored would have caught the standing, overscoped credential (VENDOR-08); access inventoried and expiring when unused closes the same gap (AUTH-08); and keeping admin interfaces off the public internet would have removed the RDP exposure that made the stolen credential usable from outside at all (CLOUD-08).

**What happened.** SamSam ransomware hit two Allscripts data centers, in Raleigh and Charlotte, North Carolina, on 18 January 2018. Allscripts is a cloud vendor for electronic health records and e-prescribing; the outage cut off record and prescribing access for an estimated 1,500 client practices, about 3% of its customer base, for up to a week.

**Entry.** Not publicly disclosed. SamSam campaigns in this period were most commonly associated with brute-forced RDP and exploitation of unpatched JBoss application servers, but neither has been confirmed as the specific vector here.

**Escalation.** Whatever the initial foothold, the ransomware reached two geographically separate data centers, indicating insufficient isolation between them once the attacker was inside either.

**Blast radius.** No evidence of data exfiltration was reported. The operational impact fell largely on downstream customers: practices that had done nothing wrong on their own systems lost access to patient records and e-prescribing for days, in at least one case forcing a practice to turn away patients because staff could not reach charts for over 8,500 people.

**Human factor.** Allscripts' security operations center caught the intrusion fast — abnormal activity at 2 a.m., confirmed ransomware by 6 a.m. — but a single SaaS vendor's ransomware incident became, in effect, an incident for every practice that depended on it, with no visibility or control of their own into the vendor's segmentation or recovery time.

**What would have stopped it.** Segmentation between environments, so one data center's compromise does not reach the other, addresses Allscripts' own chain (CLOUD-05); restore-tested backups would have shortened the week-long recovery (DATA-08); and an inventory of every third party holding your data or running your workflow is what would have let the affected practices understand and plan for this concentration of risk beforehand (VENDOR-01).

  - https://www.apriorit.com/dev-blog/561-coincheck-hack
```

**What happened.** In the early hours of 26 January 2018, roughly 523 million NEM tokens were moved out of Coincheck, a Japanese cryptocurrency exchange, to an attacker-controlled account. Reported values at the time ranged from about $530 million to $547 million depending on the exchange rate used; this record uses the more commonly cited $530 million figure and flags the range given the dispute. About 260,000 customers held the affected NEM balances.

**Entry.** A Coincheck employee's computer was compromised through a phishing email carrying commodity remote-access malware, reported as the Mokes and Netwire families, giving the attacker a foothold inside the company.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial foothold: Coincheck held the NEM in a single internet-connected "hot wallet" with no multi-signature requirement, so whoever controlled the compromised endpoint's access could move the full balance in one transaction. Coincheck's own president later cited understaffing as a reason cold storage and multisig had not been implemented for this asset.

**Blast radius.** All NEM held in the hot wallet, worth roughly half a billion dollars at the time, was gone within minutes. Japan's Financial Services Agency issued business improvement orders and inspected the exchange; Coincheck later reimbursed affected customers from its own funds. Attribution was never conclusively established; this record does not repeat unconfirmed claims about which country the attackers operated from.

**Human factor.** A phishing email reached and was opened by an employee, and the organization had made a deliberate, staffing-driven decision to keep a large operational balance in a hot wallet without the second-key control that would have required more than one compromised login to move funds.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on accounts with production reach raises the cost of turning a phished endpoint into wallet access (AUTH-01); a second person required for high-impact actions, the multisig Coincheck lacked, would have stopped a single compromised machine from moving the full balance (HUMAN-02); and credentials scoped to one job, one resource limit how much a single compromised endpoint can reach in the first place (CRED-06).

  - https://gizmodo.com/grammarly-bug-let-snoops-read-everything-you-wrote-onli-1822740378
```

**What happened.** Google Project Zero researcher Tavis Ormandy found that Grammarly's Chrome and Firefox extensions, installed by an estimated 22 million Chrome users at the time, exposed each user's authentication token to every website the browser visited. Four lines of JavaScript on any page could grab the token and log in as that user, reading their documents, history, and account data.

**Entry.** The extension shipped a session-equivalent credential somewhere any visited website's script could read it, rather than keeping that authentication server-side and scoped to Grammarly's own origin.

**Escalation.** None required: possession of the leaked token was equivalent to being logged in as the victim. No further step was needed to reach full account access.

**Blast radius.** Ormandy reported the bug on 2 February 2018, and Grammarly shipped a fix within hours. Grammarly stated it had no evidence any user's data was actually accessed through the flaw before the fix; the 22 million figure represents potential exposure, not confirmed victims.

**Human factor.** No one caught, in security review before shipping, that the extension's cross-origin authentication handling would hand its token to any page that asked for it — a design and review gap rather than a single person's mistake.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating anything the browser can read as public, with secrets kept server-side, closes the exact gap here (CRED-02); and short-lived, revocable, rotating sessions would have limited how much a leaked token was worth even if the exposure had gone undetected longer (AUTH-04).

  - https://www.virusbulletin.com/virusbulletin/2018/10/vb2018-paper-who-wasnt-responsible-olympic-destroyer/
```

**What happened.** During the 9 February 2018 opening ceremony of the Pyeongchang Winter Olympics, malware later named Olympic Destroyer disrupted Wi-Fi, internet-facing display systems, and pyeongchang2018.com, briefly stopping some attendees from printing tickets. Organizers restored services within about 12 hours by falling back to prepared contingency systems.

**Entry.** Cisco Talos, which published the first detailed technical analysis on 12 February 2018, stated plainly that the initial infection vector was not known, since the malware could have been delivered in any number of ways once it reached a network.

**Escalation.** Once running, the malware harvested credentials from browsers and from memory using Mimikatz-like techniques, then reused those freshly stolen credentials with PsExec and WMI to copy and execute itself on other machines — spreading through the network on stolen logins rather than an exploit.

**Blast radius.** The payload was built to destroy, not steal: it deleted shadow copies and event logs, disabled recovery options, and shut down the machines it reached, aiming to disrupt the Games' technology rather than exfiltrate data. No confirmed data theft was reported. Attribution was deliberately obscured — researchers later found forensic artifacts planted to implicate the wrong actor — and formal government attribution, to Russia's GRU, did not come until October 2018, outside this quarter.

**Human factor.** Whatever the initial entry point, once inside, one compromised account's credentials were enough to reach and disable systems across Wi-Fi, broadcast support, and ticketing — evidence that these functions shared enough of a flat network for lateral movement to matter more than the entry point itself.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA raises the cost of the credential theft that powered lateral movement (AUTH-01); segmentation between systems and tiers would have kept a compromise of one function, such as Wi-Fi, from reaching ticketing and broadcast support (CLOUD-05); and centralized logging of authentication and admin-tool use is what would have caught PsExec/WMI-driven lateral movement before the wiper detonated broadly (OBSV-01).

```

**What happened.** Researchers at Kromtech Security Center found an Amazon S3 bucket, readable without authentication, containing more than 119,000 scanned identity documents — passports, driver's licenses, national ID cards, military IDs, utility bills, resumes, and more — belonging to customers of Bongo International, a cross-border shipping service FedEx acquired in 2014 and later shut down. Kromtech found the bucket on 5 February 2018; FedEx secured it by 14 February.

**Human factor.** A subsidiary that FedEx had already shut down kept a storage bucket of years-old, highly sensitive scans publicly reachable, apparently unnoticed by anyone since the acquisition — a forgotten system carrying live risk long after the business decision that created it was over.

**What would have stopped it.** Object storage private by default, with legacy buckets audited, is the direct fix (DATA-03); acquired and inherited systems audited before being left running addresses the acquisition angle (VENDOR-07); and data minimization with enforced retention limits would have removed the documents years before anyone could find them, regardless of the bucket's permissions (DATA-09).

**What happened.** Reporting by The Guardian/Observer and The New York Times, based on whistleblower Christopher Wylie, revealed on 17 March 2018 that Cambridge Analytica had obtained Facebook data on tens of millions of users through a personality-quiz app and used it for political ad targeting, including on the 2016 Trump campaign. The initial reporting cited 50 million profiles; Facebook's own later analysis, published 4 April 2018 and outside this quarter, raised the figure to as many as 87 million. Both figures are given here since they were produced weeks apart by different parties using different methods.

**Entry.** About 270,000 users knowingly authorized Aleksandr Kogan's "thisisyourdigitallife" quiz app through Facebook Login in 2014. No password or account was stolen; access came through the same consent flow any Facebook platform app used.

**Escalation.** Facebook's Platform API at the time let an authorized app pull data not just on the consenting user but on that user's friends, without those friends' direct consent — a product feature that turned 270,000 authorizations into data on tens of millions of people. Kogan then passed the harvested data to Cambridge Analytica, in violation of Facebook's platform terms.

**Blast radius.** Data included profile information, page likes, and other details used to build psychographic and political-targeting profiles on up to 87 million people. Facebook first learned Cambridge Analytica still held the data in December 2015 and required a certification of deletion rather than verifying it or notifying affected users; the certification later proved false. Congressional testimony followed, and — in July 2019, well outside this quarter — a $5 billion FTC settlement.

**Human factor.** Facebook's platform design assumed that a user's consent could authorize an app to collect their friends' data too, and Facebook's own 2015 response to learning of the violation was to request a written promise of deletion rather than confirm it or tell affected users — a disclosure and verification failure on top of a permissions design one.

**What would have stopped it.** OAuth tokens scoped, expiring, and reviewed, with unused grants revoked, addresses the friends-data overreach directly (VENDOR-02); bulk export treated as a privileged, logged, alerting action would have flagged an app pulling data at this scale (DATA-11); and a written incident plan with a named decider and a disclosure clock is what Facebook's 2015 response lacked, since it treated a known platform-terms violation as a private certification exercise rather than an incident (OBSV-05).

**What happened.** Under Armour announced on 29 March 2018 that an unauthorized party had accessed data for roughly 150 million MyFitnessPal accounts in February 2018. It learned of the intrusion on 25 March, four days before disclosing it publicly.

**Entry.** Not publicly disclosed, unusual for a breach of this scale. Under Armour did not describe the technical vector used to reach the account database.

**Escalation.** None evident from the public record — the compromise reached usernames, email addresses, and hashed passwords directly, consistent with a single account database being the target — but because the entry method is unknown, whether further escalation was needed is likewise unknown.

**Blast radius.** Exposed data covered usernames, email addresses, and hashed passwords for about 150 million accounts. Under Armour said payment card data, collected and processed separately, was not affected, and that it does not collect government identifiers such as Social Security numbers through the app. Under Armour's own disclosure noted that most passwords were hashed with bcrypt, a modern algorithm, but a smaller subset used the weaker SHA-1 function.

**Human factor.** None identified regarding the intrusion itself, given the undisclosed entry vector; the identified gap is in preparation — a subset of accounts was still protected by a weak hashing algorithm at a company managing 150 million user records.

**What would have stopped it.** Passwords hashed with a modern, memory-hard algorithm, applied uniformly, closes the SHA-1 gap directly (DATA-06); and centralized logging with alerting on abnormal access or export volume would have shortened the roughly month-long gap between the February intrusion and its 25 March discovery (OBSV-01, OBSV-02).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **The SEC issued formal cybersecurity-disclosure guidance on 21 February 2018** (Release No. 33-10459), clarifying that public companies must disclose material cyber risks and incidents in a timely way and addressing insider-trading exposure around undisclosed incidents. Source: https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2018-22
- **Meltdown and Spectre triggered emergency mitigations industry-wide** — Linux KPTI, Windows KVA Shadow, and Chrome Site Isolation among them — alongside CPU microcode updates; Intel's first microcode patches caused reboot instability and had to be pulled back within weeks, a reminder to test firmware-level patches before fleet-wide rollout. Source: https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2018/01/03/processor-vulnerabilities-meltdown-and-spectre
- **The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center issued a public service announcement on 21 February 2018** (I-022118-PSA) on the ongoing rise in W-2 and business-email-compromise phishing, recommending out-of-band verification for any executive request to send payroll or tax data. Source: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2018/PSA180221
- **Drupal released a critical remote-code-execution patch, SA-CORE-2018-002** ("Drupalgeddon2," CVE-2018-7600, CVSS 24/25) on 28 March 2018, ahead of the mass-exploitation wave that followed in the second quarter; sites that patched immediately within this quarter avoided it. Source: https://www.drupal.org/sa-core-2018-002
- **Facebook announced platform changes on 21 March 2018** in direct response to the Cambridge Analytica disclosures: auditing apps with pre-2014 access to large amounts of data, automatically revoking an app's access after 90 days of disuse, restricting new apps to name, profile photo, and email by default, and expanding its bug bounty to cover data-misuse reports. Source: https://about.fb.com/news/2018/03/cracking-down-on-platform-abuse/
