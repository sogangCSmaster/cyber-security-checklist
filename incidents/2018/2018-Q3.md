# 2018 Q3 — Web Skimmers, Stolen Tokens, Third-Party Trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/third-party-access — the two costliest breaches this quarter turned on contractor or vendor credentials: a compromised Swissport account at British Airways and a former contractor's shared login at Chegg, with an exposed subcontractor server at Level One Robotics showing the same pattern from the vendor's side
> - **New technique this quarter:** Magecart's payment-page skimmer moved off third-party widgets (Inbenta at Ticketmaster, Q2) and onto attacker-modified first-party checkout code at British Airways and Newegg
> - **Recurring failure:** Absent, optional, or interceptable multi-factor authentication on privileged and remote-access accounts — British Airways, SingHealth, Reddit, Chegg, and UnityPoint Health all trace back to it

```yaml
  - https://www.techtarget.com/healthtechsecurity/news/366595655/UnityPoint-Health-Reaches-28M-Settlement-Over-2018-Data-Breach
```

**What happened.** A wave of phishing emails spoofed to look like they came from a UnityPoint Health executive convinced at least one employee to hand over email-account credentials. Attackers used that access to read internal mailboxes between March 14 and April 3, 2018; UnityPoint detected the intrusion on May 31, 2018, and began notifying roughly 1.4 million patients in July, making this the largest U.S. healthcare breach disclosed that year.

**Entry.** Targeted spear-phishing email impersonating a senior executive, sent to employees with access to internal mail systems.

**Escalation.** None required — the compromised mailboxes themselves held the patient data (names, addresses, medical and treatment information, lab results, insurance details, and for some patients Social Security and payment card numbers), so reading the mail was the breach.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 1.4 million patients' protected health information and, for a subset, financial data. A federal class-action suit followed, which UnityPoint later settled for a reported $2.8 million.

**Human factor.** The apparent underlying goal was business email compromise — diverting a payroll or vendor payment — and an employee acted on a spoofed executive request without independently verifying it; UnityPoint added mandatory phishing training and multi-factor authentication only after the fact.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on email accounts, so a phished password alone is not enough (AUTH-01); payment and data-access requests verified through a second channel before acting (HUMAN-10); training on current BEC and executive-impersonation technique (HUMAN-05); centrally logged mailbox access to shorten the 78-day gap between compromise and detection (OBSV-01).

```yaml
factor: [factor/social-engineering, factor/no-segmentation, factor/detection-failure]
scale: undisclosed
cost_usd: 2402984
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: high
controls: [HUMAN-05, AUTH-07, CLOUD-05]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2018/07/hackers-breached-virginia-bank-twice-in-eight-months-stole-2-4m/
```

**What happened.** A small community bank, The National Bank of Blacksburg in Virginia, was breached twice in eight months by what forensic investigators believed was the same group, using tools and infrastructure of Russian origin. The details became public in July 2018 through the bank's lawsuit against its insurer, Everest National Insurance Co., over a coverage dispute about the resulting losses — the first the wider public heard of either intrusion, even though the intrusions themselves happened in May 2016 and January 2017.

**Entry.** Both intrusions began with a spear-phishing email to a bank employee. The May 2016 email led to malware on an employee's computer and, from there, a second computer with access to the STAR Network, First Data's debit card transaction switch. The January 2017 email again delivered malware, this time reaching both the STAR Network and Navigator, software the bank used to manage customer account credits and debits.

**Escalation.** In each case, a single phished workstation had a path straight through to systems that controlled real money movement, with no separation between an ordinary employee's email client and the bank's card-transaction and account-management tooling. In 2016 the attackers used that access to disable PIN verification and withdrawal/debit limits; in 2017 they used Navigator to fraudulently credit money into customer accounts before draining it back out, while actively watching the accounts and deleting evidence of what they had done.

**Blast radius.** The May 2016 intrusion let attackers cash out roughly $569,000 through ATMs across North America over the Memorial Day weekend. The January 2017 intrusion, over a single weekend, fraudulently credited more than $2 million to customer accounts before withdrawing it back out, netting the bank a loss of $1,833,984 — a combined $2.4 million across both incidents. No customer-data scale was disclosed.

**Human factor.** The bank added "velocity rules" to slow suspicious transactions after the first breach, but eight months later an employee was phished again, and the second attack simply used a different piece of the bank's own back-office software to move money instead of the ATM switch — evidence the fix addressed a symptom rather than the underlying lack of separation between email-reachable workstations and payment infrastructure.

**What would have stopped it.** Training current enough to catch a second, differently-worded phishing attempt after the first one succeeded (HUMAN-05); admin and account-management tooling like Navigator sitting behind its own separate authentication and audit trail (AUTH-07); network segmentation so a phished front-office workstation has no path to the STAR Network or core banking software (CLOUD-05).

### I2018Q3-03 · ComplyRight efile4biz.com credential-harvesting breach

```yaml
impact: [impact/pii, impact/credentials]

tech: [tech/saas]

dwell_days: 32
confidence: medium
controls: [INPUT-03, AUTH-01, OBSV-05]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2018/07/human-resources-firm-complyright-breached/
```

**What happened.** ComplyRight, a vendor whose cloud platform prepares W-2 and 1099 tax forms on behalf of roughly 76,000 small and mid-sized businesses, had unauthorized access to its efile4biz.com site between 20 April and 22 May 2018. ComplyRight initially told affected individuals that fewer than 10% of people with tax forms on the platform were affected; a subsequent filing with the Wisconsin Attorney General's office put the confirmed number at 662,000, and notification letters did not go out until late July 2018 — roughly two months after discovery.

**Entry.** ComplyRight never officially disclosed how intruders first gained the ability to modify the efile4biz.com site. The most likely explanation, based on the pattern of what was stolen, is that malicious code was planted on the login page to record the passwords customers typed in as they signed in to prepare tax forms.

**Escalation.** The captured passwords were themselves the way in: with a real customer's working login, the attackers could sign in as that business and pull whatever W-2/1099 data — including Social Security numbers, names, and addresses — sat in the account, with no further exploitation needed.

**Blast radius.** 662,000 people's tax-form data, including Social Security numbers, contact information, and, for the businesses whose logins were captured, their own account credentials. ComplyRight offered 12 months of free credit monitoring, which does little against tax-refund and employment fraud committed with a stolen SSN.

**Human factor.** ComplyRight took roughly a month to detect the intrusion and then two more months to begin notifying the people affected, initially undercounting the scope by an order of magnitude and largely ignoring press inquiries in the interim — a disclosure process that left affected individuals unaware for the period when the stolen SSNs were freshest and most useful to a fraudster.

**What would have stopped it.** A content-security policy and output encoding on the login page, so injected credential-stealing code has nowhere to send what it captures (INPUT-03); MFA on customer accounts, so a captured password alone cannot reach the tax-form data (AUTH-01); a written incident plan with a firm disclosure clock, rather than a scope estimate and notification timeline that both grew after the fact (OBSV-05).


**What happened.** On 27 June 2018, Barcelona-based online survey and forms provider Typeform discovered that an attacker had downloaded a partial, unencrypted backup of customer response data while the company was using that backup for internal testing; Typeform said it shut off the exposure within about 30 minutes of noticing it and disclosed publicly on 3 July 2018. Because Typeform's own customers are themselves companies and public bodies running surveys on tens of thousands of their own end users, the breach fanned out to a long list of downstream organizations — including Monzo Bank, Fortnum & Mason, Travelodge, and Australia's Tasmanian Electoral Commission — each of which had to separately notify the people who had filled out one of their forms.

**Entry.** Typeform has not explained exactly how the attacker reached the backup, only that it was "in the process of completing a test of certain functionalities using the partial unencrypted backup" when "the hacker was able to access this specific information at this time" — indicating the backup was reachable from outside during that testing window rather than sitting behind Typeform's normal production access controls.

**Escalation.** None required — the backup file itself contained the finished survey responses (names, emails, phone numbers, postal codes, dates of birth, and for some forms bank details or Social Security numbers), so downloading it was the entire breach.

**Blast radius.** Only responses submitted before 3 May 2018 were affected; Typeform never published a single combined total, but individual customer notifications tallied by press coverage put the aggregate above 100,000 records, spread across an unknown number of Typeform's enterprise and government clients worldwide. Account passwords and Stripe-processed payment data were not affected.

**Human factor.** A live, unencrypted extract of customer data was pulled into a testing process and left reachable from outside Typeform's normal access path, with no one apparently asking whether test environments needed the same protection as production.

**What would have stopped it.** Sensitive fields encrypted at rest, including backups used for testing (DATA-07); a hard boundary keeping real customer data out of test and development contexts (CRED-08); retention limits so backups do not sit around holding data long past its need (DATA-09).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2018/07/20/data-breach-level-one-automakers
```

**What happened.** UpGuard's cyber-risk research team found that Level One Robotics and Controls, a Michigan-based systems integrator that builds factory-floor automation for major automakers, had left an internal file-transfer server open to the internet with no authentication. The server held 157 GB of data belonging to the automaker clients it served — including Tesla, Volkswagen Group, Fiat Chrysler, Ford, General Motors, Toyota, and ThyssenKrupp — such as assembly-line schematics, factory floor plans, robot configuration files, VPN and ID-badge access request forms, and non-disclosure agreements, some over a decade old. UpGuard contacted Level One on July 5, 2018, and the exposure was closed by July 10; UpGuard published its findings on July 20, 2018.

**Entry.** The exposure used rsync, a file-synchronization protocol. Level One had not restricted which IP addresses could connect and had not required any credential, so any device on the internet could connect and browse the full directory tree.

**Escalation.** None required — the rsync share was directly readable, and UpGuard additionally found it was writable, meaning anyone who found it could have altered files in place, for example substituting bank routing details in payment instructions or planting malware inside engineering documents.

**Blast radius.** 157 GB spanning roughly ten years of records from at least eight automotive manufacturers and their tier-one suppliers, plus Level One's own business records (invoices, contracts, bank account numbers) and employee data. No confirmed malicious access is known; UpGuard's researchers are the only identified party to have found and retrieved the data before it was closed.

**Human factor.** Someone configured a production file-transfer service for convenience — letting engineers and contractors sync large CAD and configuration files — without ever adding an IP allowlist or authentication step, and neither Level One nor its automaker clients were auditing what the subcontractor was exposing to the internet.

**What would have stopped it.** Nothing publicly reachable unless deliberately so, checked by regular enumeration (CLOUD-01); an inventory of every third party holding your data (VENDOR-01); vendor access time-boxed and separately monitored (VENDOR-08).

```yaml
  - https://www.techtarget.com/healthtechsecurity/news/366595902/Massive-SingHealth-Data-Breach-Caused-by-Lack-of-Basic-Security
```

**What happened.** Between 27 June and 4 July 2018, an intruder queried and copied the personal particulars of 1.5 million SingHealth patients — including then-Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong — from the Sunrise Clinical Manager database, along with outpatient dispensed-medicine records for 160,000 of them. Singapore's government disclosed the breach on 20 July 2018 and convened a Committee of Inquiry (COI), whose public report called the intrusion "deliberate, targeted and well-planned."

**Entry.** The COI could not conclusively establish how the attacker first reached a front-end workstation inside a SingHealth cluster hospital. A 2017 penetration test had already flagged weak administrator passwords and inadequate network segmentation, and remediation of those findings was, in the COI's words, "mismanaged and inadequate."

**Escalation.** From the compromised workstation, the attacker exploited a coding weakness in how Citrix remote-access servers connected to the Sunrise Clinical Manager database, then moved laterally using administrator credentials. Two-factor authentication existed but was not enforced as the only path to admin access, so the attacker could reach the database through routes that bypassed it entirely.

**Blast radius.** 1.5 million patients' names, NRIC numbers, addresses, dates of birth, race and gender; 160,000 patients' outpatient prescription records. Staff noticed anomalous database queries during the attack window but did not recognize them as a cyberattack, in part because no incident-reporting framework told them the activity warranted escalation.

**Human factor.** A chain of middle-management decisions — unpatched pentest findings, an admin-access policy with a non-2FA back door, and staff untrained to recognize what they were looking at — rather than any single failure, is what the COI blamed for making the breach possible.

**What would have stopped it.** MFA enforced as the only path to every admin account, with no bypass route (AUTH-01); segmentation between the Citrix tier and the clinical database tier (CLOUD-05); centrally logged and alerted admin/database access (OBSV-01); training current enough that anomalous queries get escalated, not shrugged off (HUMAN-05).

```yaml
  - https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2018/08/02/reddit-breach/
```

**What happened.** Between June 14 and 18, 2018, an attacker took over a handful of Reddit employees' accounts at its cloud-hosting and source-control providers, despite those accounts being protected by SMS-based two-factor authentication. Reddit detected the intrusion on June 19 and disclosed it publicly on August 1, 2018.

**Entry.** Reddit's own account says the attacker intercepted the SMS text messages carrying employees' one-time login codes — most likely via an SS7 network attack or a SIM swap — which defeated the second factor even though it was enabled; Reddit did not disclose how the underlying account passwords were obtained. This is tagged `entry/session-theft` as the nearest defined vector, since a valid second-factor token was intercepted and used to skip the check it was meant to enforce, rather than a literal browser session cookie being stolen.

**Escalation.** None required — the compromised provider accounts already had read access sufficient to pull data directly.

**Blast radius.** A backup database from 2007 covering usernames, hashed passwords, and email addresses for accounts active between Reddit's 2005 launch and May 2007, plus logs of digest emails sent between June 3 and June 17, 2018, which linked current usernames to email addresses. Reddit said the exact number of affected accounts was not precisely known.

**Human factor.** Reddit had rolled out MFA in good faith but chose the weakest common form of it — SMS — for accounts protecting its cloud and source-control infrastructure, and had not yet moved those accounts to token-based or hardware-key authentication.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing- and interception-resistant MFA — not SMS — on every account with production reach (AUTH-01); a second factor that cannot be satisfied by intercepting a code, such as a hardware security key (AUTH-09).


**What happened.** Over 11-13 August 2018, attackers stole roughly ₹94 crore (about $13.5 million) from Cosmos Cooperative Bank, a 112-year-old bank based in Pune, India and one of the country's largest urban cooperative banks. On 11 August, malware on the bank's card-payment processing network created a rogue "proxy switch" that approved ATM withdrawal requests without checking them against real account balances or limits, letting coordinated mules empty cash from ATMs in more than 25 countries within about two hours. Two days later, on 13 August, a second wave used compromised SWIFT access to wire roughly $2 million to an account at Hang Seng Bank in Hong Kong. Cosmos Bank disclosed the breach publicly on 14 August; a Pune court later convicted 11 people, believed to be local mules rather than the original intruders.

**Entry.** Not publicly detailed. Cosmos Bank and independent analysts described malware reaching the bank's card-payment processing infrastructure, but neither the initial foothold nor how that malware first arrived — phishing, a compromised vendor connection, or something else — was ever made public.

**Escalation.** Once inside, the attackers reached the switch that authorizes ATM and card transactions and interposed their own proxy in front of it — a step security researcher Brian Krebs noted was only possible because the payment-processing network was not fully encrypted, letting an intruder with network access alter what response an ATM request received. That proxy answered "approved" to any request, and, per investigators, the bank's own transaction and withdrawal limits had also been disabled, removing the caps that would otherwise have bounded the damage.

**Blast radius.** $11.5 million pulled from ATMs across more than 25 countries plus roughly $2 million wired via SWIFT to Hong Kong — a combined $13.5 million, all from the bank's own operating accounts; Cosmos Bank said customer deposit accounts were not touched. No count of exposed card records was published.

**Human factor.** The bank's own payment-switch traffic was not encrypted end-to-end, its fraud-control limits had been stripped or bypassed, and even though staff caught unusually repeated ATM activity within about two hours on 11 August, that detection didn't stop a second, different attack against the SWIFT system two days later — the response to the first wave didn't extend to closing the door the second wave used.

**What would have stopped it.** Segmentation keeping the payment-switch tier isolated from whatever the initial malware compromised (CLOUD-05); alerting on abnormal transaction volume and value per account, tuned to catch the kind of burst withdrawal pattern the FBI had warned about just a day before this attack (OBSV-02); server-side authorization that validates each transaction against the real ledger rather than trusting a switch's own approval (AUTH-02).

```yaml
  - https://www.irishtimes.com/news/ireland/irish-news/personal-details-of-37-000-eir-customers-stolen-in-data-breach-1.3604034
```

**What happened.** An employee laptop belonging to Irish telecom operator eir was stolen offsite on 12 August 2018. The laptop should have been fully encrypted under eir's standard policy, but a faulty security update had silently stripped standard encryption from around 1,500 of the company's laptops in the days before the theft, leaving this one — and its contents — readable to whoever took it. eir notified Ireland's Data Protection Commission and Gardaí immediately, and the DPC issued a public statement on 22 August 2018.

**Entry.** Physical theft of a company laptop from outside eir's premises; no network intrusion was involved.

**Escalation.** None required — the encryption that should have made the stolen device's contents unreadable had already been removed by the faulty update, so anyone with the laptop in hand had the data in plain form.

**Blast radius.** 36,642 eir customers' names, email addresses, phone numbers, and account numbers, plus contact details for 177 eir employees. No financial data was stored on the device, and as of the DPC's statement there was no evidence the data had been used by a third party. Of the roughly 1,484 other laptops caught by the same faulty update, 1,438 had been re-encrypted within days and 21 remained unencrypted a week after discovery.

**Human factor.** A software update silently disabled a core security control on a fleet of laptops, and nothing in eir's process caught that roughly 1,500 machines had quietly lost their encryption until one of them was stolen — the gap between requiring encryption and verifying encryption is actually still on is what turned a routine device theft into a data breach.

**What would have stopped it.** Endpoint management that verifies laptops stay encrypted rather than assuming a policy setting still holds (CICD-07); sensitive customer fields encrypted at a layer independent of device-level disk encryption, so a single misapplied update can't remove protection outright (DATA-07); treating silent security-control drift — 1,500 laptops losing encryption with no alert — as an incident in its own right, not just the theft that exposed it (OBSV-03).

**What happened.** Facebook's engineering team found on September 25, 2018 that attackers had been exploiting a combination of coding flaws to steal "access tokens" — the credentials that keep a person logged in without re-entering a password — letting them take over other people's accounts. ---

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **Facebook's mass-logout playbook.** Facebook disabled the vulnerable "View As" feature immediately, force-invalidated tokens for every plausibly affected account rather than only the confirmed 50 million (about 90 million forced re-logins in total), and published a Help Center notice so users could check whether they'd been logged out because of the incident.
- **British Airways' fast notification tested GDPR's new clock.** BA reported to the ICO within about 24 hours of containment, one of the first large-scale tests of GDPR's mandatory 72-hour breach-notification rule (in force only since May 2018); the ICO later cited that promptness, and BA's full cooperation, as mitigating factors even while imposing what was then one of its largest-ever fines.
- **Singapore moved quickly on SingHealth.** The government convened an independent Committee of Inquiry within weeks of disclosure and ordered an immediate review of public healthcare IT systems, ahead of the COI's 2019 recommendations for mandatory two-factor authentication and network separation across the public healthcare sector.
- **Reddit's postmortem became the reference case against SMS 2FA.** Reddit's public account of SMS interception defeating employee 2FA was widely cited industry-wide as evidence that SMS is not a phishing- or interception-resistant second factor, and the company said it would move critical accounts to token-based authentication.
- **Magecart's pattern got a name across two more victims.** Following Ticketmaster/Inbenta in Q2, independent researchers publicly attributed the British Airways and Newegg skimmers to the same Magecart cluster, sharpening industry recognition of checkout-page JavaScript injection as a distinct, recurring threat separate from third-party-widget compromises, and strengthening the case for content-security-policy restrictions on payment pages.
- **Superdrug's episode reinforced credential-hygiene advice.** Coming amid a run of similar UK retailer credential-stuffing extortion attempts in mid-2018, it renewed consumer guidance on unique passwords per site and pushed retailers toward screening logins against known-breached-password lists rather than trusting a correct password alone.
- **Chegg's response prioritized blast-radius containment.** Resetting all 40 million user passwords immediately on discovery, rather than only notifying, was a comparatively aggressive containment step in a quarter otherwise marked by slow detection (SingHealth, Newegg) and stale, unrevoked access (Chegg's own root cause).
