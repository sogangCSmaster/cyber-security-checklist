
**What happened.** The FBI told Congress in early April 2026 that an intrusion into an unclassified bureau system holding pen-register and trap-and-trace surveillance data had been formally declared a "major incident" under FISMA — a threshold only a handful of US agencies cross in any year. Reporting identified the exposed data as phone numbers belonging to surveillance targets. The bureau began investigating abnormal activity on the system on February 17, 2026; the investigation itself was first reported publicly in early March, and the major-incident declaration and congressional notification made it a Q2 disclosure.

**Entry.** The FBI said access "was obtained through a third party." Reporting to lawmakers described the attacker leveraging a commercial internet service provider's vendor infrastructure to reach the bureau's system. Neither the vendor nor the specific mechanism inside that vendor has been named publicly.

**Escalation.** No escalation detail was published. The `escalation/lateral-movement` tag here reflects only the one movement the FBI itself described — from a telecommunications vendor's infrastructure into a bureau system — and should not be read as a claim about what happened inside the FBI network.

**Blast radius.** The bureau has identified phone numbers of surveillance targets as exposed. No count of affected targets, no date range for the collection, and no statement about whether content or only metadata was reachable have been made public. Because the exposed numbers identify who US law enforcement is watching, the intelligence value is high even though the data volume may be small.

**Human factor.** A law-enforcement system whose entire purpose is to hold the identities of people under court-authorized surveillance was reachable through a commercial carrier's vendor, and the connection was trusted enough that traversing it did not raise an alarm for months.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-08 (vendor access is time-boxed and monitored separately from employee access, so a carrier's infrastructure cannot quietly become a path into a wiretap system), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding a token into your systems — the FBI could not name the vendor publicly, which suggests the dependency chain was longer than one hop), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of access to the most sensitive datasets an agency holds; abnormal activity was ultimately what surfaced this, in February, after an unknown dwell period).


**What happened.** Duc, a money-transfer app run by Toronto-based Duales that lets users send funds domestically and internationally including to Cuba, left an Amazon-hosted storage server publicly accessible with no password. Security researcher Anurag Sen of CyPeace found it and contacted TechCrunch, which notified the company's chief executive on April 2, 2026. The files were made inaccessible the same week.

**Entry.** No attacker and no exploit. The bucket was configured to allow public reads; anyone with a web browser who knew the server's address could list and download its contents. The files were stored unencrypted.

**Escalation.** None required — the identity documents were the first thing reachable, not something reached after a foothold.

**Blast radius.** Over 360,000 files were in the exposed bucket, spread across multiple folders each holding tens of thousands of user-uploaded documents: driver's licences and passports submitted for know-your-customer verification. The oldest files dated to September 2020 and new ones were still being uploaded daily, so the exposure window plausibly spans five and a half years. No confirmed malicious access was reported. Duales did not publish a count of affected users.

**Human factor.** A small fintech collected government identity documents as a regulatory obligation, then kept every one of them — going back to 2020 — in one unencrypted, world-readable bucket. The collection was required; the indefinite retention in plaintext was a choice nobody revisited.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy buckets audited — the default on Amazon S3 is already private, so this took an affirmative change), CLOUD-01 (enumerate regularly what is publicly reachable; a quarterly sweep would have caught a five-year-old public bucket), DATA-07 (identity documents deleted after the verification they were collected for completes, which would have made the 2020–2024 files simply absent).


**What happened.** CERT-EU, the computer emergency response team serving the EU institutions, confirmed on April 3, 2026 that attackers had obtained a secret API key tied to the European Commission's AWS account and used it to take roughly 92 gigabytes of compressed data, which was subsequently leaked online. CERT-EU dated the origin of the breach to March 19, 2026. The path in was a supply-chain compromise of Trivy, the widely used open-source container vulnerability scanner published by Aqua Security, which had itself been compromised in March 2026; the Commission downloaded a tampered build.

**Entry.** A legitimate update channel delivered a malicious payload. A compromised release of Trivy — a security tool, run with access to build and cloud environments precisely because that is its job — executed inside the Commission's environment and exfiltrated an AWS API key it found there.

**Escalation.** The stolen key granted enough reach over the Commission's AWS account to enumerate and pull tens of gigabytes across multiple data stores, including mailbox contents. A scanner's credential should not have been able to read production mail archives; the key's scope, not the scanner's compromise, is what turned one bad binary into a 92 GB loss.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 92 GB compressed, including names, email addresses, and around 52,000 files of sent message content, with data touching at least 29 other EU entities beyond the Commission itself. CERT-EU said it was still analysing the published data and had begun contacting affected organisations. Two groups feature: TeamPCP conducted the intrusion, and a ShinyHunters member confirmed acquiring and leaking data TeamPCP had taken — so the leak volume is attacker-published and CERT-EU had not fully verified it at disclosure.

**Human factor.** A security tool was trusted the way security tools usually are: installed quickly, run with broad permissions, and not version-pinned or held back for a cooling-off period. The organisation whose job is coordinating incident response for the EU institutions was compromised through its own scanning stack.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-03 (a cooldown before adopting a newly published version, which would have put the tampered Trivy release outside the adoption window), CRED-03 (rotate on exposure — any credential reachable from a host that ran a compromised binary is burned), CLOUD-03 (least-privilege IAM: the credential available to a container scanner had no business being able to read 52,000 sent messages).

**What happened.** ChipSoft supplies HiX, the electronic patient record used by the large majority of Dutch hospitals and by many general practices, so when the company was hit by ransomware late on 7 April 2026 the disruption propagated into care delivery rather than staying inside a software vendor. Dutch public broadcaster NOS reported the attack the same evening. Customers disconnected from ChipSoft's hosted services on security advice, general practices in North Limburg and rehabilitation clinics lost access, and the Rotterdam Eye Hospital was among the named affected institutions. The Embargo group claimed the intrusion and posted a countdown threatening to publish roughly 100 GB of data. On 28 April ChipSoft said the stolen data had been destroyed and that its own experts had confirmed the destruction was technically sound, while declining to say whether it had paid or how it could rule out retained copies.

**Entry.** Never published. ChipSoft has not said how the attackers got in, and neither the Dutch DPA filings nor the NOS reporting name a vector. This record uses `entry/unknown` deliberately rather than guessing at a vendor appliance or a stolen credential.

**Escalation.** The escalation was commercial rather than technical: one supplier sits between an attacker and several hundred care providers, so compromising the supplier reached the patient records of organisations that were never touched directly. More than sixty separate breach notifications were filed with the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens over this single incident, most of them by the affected care providers rather than by ChipSoft, which is the clearest measure of how far one vendor's bad week travelled.

**Blast radius.** Around 100 GB claimed by Embargo, containing treatment information about patients of general practices, rehabilitation clinics and at least one specialist hospital. Neither ChipSoft nor the care providers published a person count. Hospital operations were largely protected because most hospital HiX installations run on-premises and were disconnected quickly; primary care, which consumes the hosted service, took the damage. ChipSoft's own communications shifted over nine days from "no indication patient data was taken" to "data leakage cannot be excluded" to a confirmation that patient data had in fact been taken — a sequence that cost the company credibility with its own customers and is recorded here as `factor/disclosure-failure`.

**Human factor.** Two decisions dominate. First, the Dutch health system concentrated its record-keeping in one supplier over two decades, which is efficient until the supplier has a bad night. Second, ChipSoft's early statements described a best case as though it were a finding. The care providers who repeated those statements to their own patients then had to retract them, which is why the notification volume to the regulator arrived in a second wave rather than a first.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data — the care providers that answered the regulator fastest were those that already knew what ChipSoft held for them), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows and active monitoring of vendor advisories, so a customer learns from the vendor rather than from the national broadcaster), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan naming the communicator and the disclosure clock, which is precisely what would have prevented three contradictory public positions in nine days), DATA-08 (restore-tested backups not writable by production credentials, the control that decides whether disconnecting is survivable), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between the hosted multi-tenant service and everything else).

### I2026Q2-05 · MCP STDIO parameter injection across the official SDKs

```yaml

confidence: medium
controls: [AGENT-04, AGENT-05, AGENT-02, INPUT-02, CLOUD-08]
sources:
  - https://www.ox.security/blog/the-mother-of-all-ai-supply-chains-critical-systemic-vulnerability-at-the-core-of-the-mcp/
  - https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-mcp-security-crisis-20260504-csa-styled/
```

**What happened.** On 15 April 2026 OX Security published research arguing that the way the official Model Context Protocol SDKs launch a local server over the STDIO transport turns any application that lets a user influence that launch configuration into a command-execution surface. The pattern is the same in the Python, TypeScript, Java and Rust SDKs, because it is what the transport is specified to do: take a command and its arguments and hand them to the host to run. OX estimated up to 200,000 vulnerable instances across packages with more than 150 million downloads, found around 7,000 publicly reachable MCP servers, and obtained ten high or critical CVEs against downstream products including LiteLLM, Windsurf, LibreChat and Langchain-Chatchat. The Cloud Security Alliance's 4 May research note collected the same findings alongside the older tool-poisoning, rug-pull and tool-shadowing problems and the fact that the MCP authorization specification treats authentication as optional.

**Entry.** Not a memory-corruption bug and not a coding mistake in any one product. The STDIO transport is defined to spawn a local process from a supplied command and argument list. Products that build that list from something a user, a document or a web form can influence — a hosted "add your own MCP server" field, a config file an agent is allowed to write, a URL parameter — are handing untrusted input to a process launcher. The flaw is in the composition, which is why it reproduced across four independently written SDKs.

**Escalation.** None required. The spawned process inherits whatever the host application already has: the developer's shell, the CI runner's token, the container's mounted credentials. Several of the CVEs describe the step from an unauthenticated web request straight to local execution, with no intermediate privilege boundary because the MCP host is usually running as the developer.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed malicious exploitation was published. OX reported reproducing the issue against six live production platforms with permission, and made more than thirty coordinated disclosures to downstream projects; most of the named products shipped fixes, with Windsurf's still listed as unpatched at the time of the CSA note. The exposure is best read as latent rather than realised: a very large installed base in which agent hosts accept configuration from places their authors did not think of as a security boundary.

**Human factor.** Anthropic's position, as OX reports it, was that the behaviour is what the specification describes and that sanitising the input is the integrating developer's job. That is a defensible reading of a protocol document and a poor prediction of what thousands of integrators would actually do. The recurring decision underneath the incident is treating an agent's configuration surface as configuration — something trusted operators set once — at the moment products started letting end users edit it.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-04 (MCP servers and IDE extensions pinned, reviewed and version-diffed, so the set of things an agent may launch is a reviewed list rather than whatever arrives at runtime), AGENT-05 (no MCP server or agent endpoint exposed without authentication — the 7,000 reachable servers are that control missing), AGENT-02 (agent credentials scoped to the task, which is what caps the value of a process that does get spawned), INPUT-02 (server-side schema validation on every input, applied to the launch configuration as strictly as to any other user-supplied structure), CLOUD-08 (agent and admin interfaces off the public internet).

**What happened.** ANTS is the French agency that issues secure identity and vehicle documents. On 16 April 2026 an actor using the handle "breach3d" advertised a dataset said to come from ANTS; the Paris prosecutor's office later put the volume at twelve to eighteen million lines. ANTS confirmed abnormal activity on its network from 13 April and authenticated the sample records. A fifteen-year-old French national was taken into custody on 25 April and a formal judicial investigation was opened on 29 April, with charges carrying up to seven years and a €300,000 fine. The French security outlet ZATAZ, examining the sample, argued the headline figure was inflated: the sample was about a hundred lines, the field names looked reconstructed rather than exported, and the absence of birthplaces and postal addresses did not match what an ANTS citizen database should contain. ZATAZ's reading is that a genuine compromise occurred but that what was taken is more likely professional-account data than the full citizen register.

None of the three has been described by ANTS or by the prosecutor, so the record carries `entry/unknown`.

**Blast radius.** Genuinely disputed, which is why this record is `confidence: low`. The prosecutors' twelve-to-eighteen-million figure derives from the seller's own claim; ZATAZ's structural analysis suggests a much smaller and differently shaped dataset. ANTS issued a public alert telling citizens to watch for fraud attempts, which is the right action under uncertainty and also an admission that the agency could not yet bound the loss. No confirmed downstream fraud has been published.

**Human factor.** The part of this story that transfers is not the teenager. It is the claim — uncontested in the reporting — that two vulnerability reports arrived at a national identity-document agency and went unanswered. Whether or not that excuses anything legally, an organisation that has no working route for an outsider to report a flaw converts finders into sellers, and it loses the only cheap chance it had to fix the problem before the data moved.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-06 (a published, monitored way for an outsider to report a vulnerability, with an acknowledgement SLA — the single control most directly implicated), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read or export volume per account, which is how a multi-million-row extraction becomes visible on the day rather than at a forum listing), AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data), DATA-11 (bulk export as a privileged, logged, alerting action), DATA-09 (minimisation, so that what a single query can reach is smaller than the whole register).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/23/vercel-says-some-of-its-customers-data-was-stolen-prior-to-its-recent-hack/
```

**What happened.** Vercel, the deployment platform used by hundreds of thousands of organisations, disclosed on April 19, 2026 that an attacker had reached its internal environment and decrypted customer environment variables. Vercel's own bulletin states the incident "originated with a compromise of Context.ai, a third-party AI tool used by a Vercel employee." A follow-up on April 23 established that some customer data had been taken before the April intrusion, via infostealer malware hunting for Vercel account tokens on developer machines.

**Entry.** Not a Vercel vulnerability. Context.ai — a small AI productivity tool one Vercel employee used — was compromised, and the attacker used credentials obtained through it to take over that employee's individual Vercel Google Workspace account. Because the path ran on an already-issued OAuth session rather than a password, the employee's multi-factor authentication was never challenged.

**Escalation.** From the employee's Workspace account the attacker pivoted into the employee's Vercel account and then into a Vercel environment, where they enumerated and decrypted the environment variables that decrypt to plaintext — the ones Vercel classifies as non-sensitive. Those variables held customers' API keys, access tokens, and database credentials, so a category Vercel had labelled non-sensitive was in practice a store of live secrets for other companies.

**Blast radius.** Vercel identified "a limited subset of customers" initially and found "a small number of additional accounts" as the investigation widened; no count has been published. Affected customers were told to rotate credentials immediately. Vercel engaged Google Mandiant and law enforcement, and confirmed with GitHub, Microsoft, npm and Socket that its npm packages were not tampered with.

**Human factor.** An employee signed a small AI tool into their corporate Google account without that tool passing any vendor review, and the OAuth grant that resulted was long-lived enough to be worth stealing. The second decision that mattered was internal: classifying an environment-variable store as "non-sensitive" because Vercel itself did not need the values, when the values were other people's production credentials.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-03 (authorising a new connected application requires review — the entire chain starts with an unreviewed OAuth grant to a small AI tool), VENDOR-02 (scoped, expiring OAuth tokens with unused grants revoked, which removes the stolen session's value), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime from a manager rather than held in a platform-decryptable variable store), AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions rotated on privilege change, so a stolen token does not silently satisfy MFA weeks later).

```yaml
  - https://community.bitwarden.com/t/bitwarden-statement-on-checkmarx-supply-chain-incident/96127
```

**What happened.** Over April 21–23, 2026, attackers distributed credential-stealing payloads through official artefacts of Checkmarx, an application-security vendor: tampered `checkmarx/kics` Docker images under the `v2.1.20-debian`, `v2.1.20`, `debian`, `alpine` and `latest` tags plus a newly created `v2.1.21`; two VS Code/Open VSX extensions (`cx-dev-assist` 1.17.0 and 1.19.0, `ast-results` 2.63.0 and 2.66.0); and the `ast-github-action` GitHub Action at tag 2.3.35. Because Bitwarden's release pipeline consumed `checkmarx/ast-github-action`, the compromise cascaded: on April 22 a trojanised `@bitwarden/cli` 2026.4.0 was published to npm. Researchers attributed the campaign to TeamPCP, the group's second Checkmarx-related compromise in two months.

**Entry.** How the attackers first got into Checkmarx's own publishing infrastructure has not been disclosed. Sophos notes only that the earlier March compromise apparently used previously stolen CI/CD secrets, and states there is no intelligence tying the April attacks to it. Tagging this `entry/unknown` rather than inferring from the March incident is the honest reading.

**Escalation.** The cascade is the whole story. A compromised Checkmarx GitHub Action ran inside Bitwarden's CI pipeline, where it injected a new workflow that captured the secrets available to that workflow run, then used the harvested npm publishing credentials to push a malicious CLI release. The Action inherited whatever a release workflow can see, which included publish-capable npm tokens.

**Blast radius.** The malicious Bitwarden CLI was live for roughly 93 minutes on April 22 (17:57–19:30 ET) and drew 334 downloads before it was pulled; the package normally sees more than 70,000 weekly downloads, so timing rather than any control limited the damage. The Checkmarx KICS Docker repository has over five million cumulative pulls, and no count of tampered-tag pulls has been published. The payload harvested GitHub and npm tokens, SSH keys, `.env` files, shell history, cloud provider credentials and AI assistant configurations, encrypted them, and exfiltrated to `audit.checkmarx[.]cx` with GitHub commits as a fallback channel — a domain chosen to look like the vendor's own.

**Human factor.** Two security vendors, one of them a password manager, shipped attacker-controlled code through their official channels. The specific decision that carried it was ordinary and near-universal: referencing a third-party GitHub Action by a mutable tag rather than a pinned commit, in a workflow that could see publishing credentials.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-04 (actions and pipeline steps pinned to a commit SHA with minimal token permissions — tag 2.3.35 would not have been silently substituted), CICD-01 (CI secrets scoped per job and unavailable to steps that do not need to publish, which removes the npm token the injected workflow stole), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before adopting a newly published version, which covers container tags as much as packages), DEPS-02 (install scripts disabled by default on developer machines, limiting what a trojanised CLI achieves on install).

Unguessable object names were doing the access control, and they were not unguessable.

**Escalation.** None required — passports and selfies were the stored objects.

**Blast radius.** At least 100,000 documents belonging to thousands of applicants, including passport scans and selfie photographs. **Human factor.** People handing over a passport and a photograph of their face frequently cannot tell an official government portal from a third-party intermediary that has bought the right search ads, and the intermediary is under no comparable scrutiny. **What happened.** Tenet Security published research on 17 June 2026 describing an attack class it named agentjacking, after disclosing it to Sentry on 3 June. A Sentry DSN is a write-only credential that is designed to be embedded in front-end JavaScript so a browser can post error telemetry without authenticating; it is public on purpose. Tenet showed that anyone holding a DSN can therefore write events into that organisation's Sentry project, and that when a developer later asks an AI coding agent to look into the errors through Sentry's MCP integration, the agent reads the attacker's event as trustworthy diagnostic output and follows the remediation text inside it. Tenet found 2,388 organisations with injectable DSNs by passive reconnaissance, including a Fortune 100 company and independent developers across more than thirty countries, and reported an 85% success rate across controlled tests spanning Claude Code, Cursor and OpenAI Codex on macOS, Windows, WSL, containers, CI and cloud. Sentry responded the same day it was told, declined a root-cause fix as "technically not defensible" at the platform level, and shipped a filter for the specific exploit string.

**Entry.** Indirect prompt injection with an unusually clean delivery path. The attacker never touches the victim's repository, network or credentials; they write a plausible-looking error event, formatted to read like troubleshooting guidance, into a telemetry pipeline the victim already trusts. The agent's own tool call retrieves it. The credential required to do this is one the victim deliberately published.

**Escalation.** None required, and that is the finding. The agent already runs with the developer's privileges — their shell, their repository, their cloud sessions, their package manager. Instructions that arrive as tool output land inside that authority with no boundary between "data the agent read" and "actions the agent takes". In CI the same path reaches build credentials.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed malicious exploitation. The exposure is the 2,388 organisations whose DSNs were discoverable and whose developers use the Sentry MCP integration, and the measured 85% rate at which agents acted on injected content. Tenet released open-source hardening configurations for Cursor and Claude Code alongside the write-up, and argued that because the DSN's publicness is a deliberate product decision and the injection is plain text, the only remaining place to catch it is the agent's runtime rather than the platform or the prompt.

**Human factor.** Sentry's DSN design was correct for a decade in which the only consumer of an error event was a dashboard a human read sceptically. The assumption that broke is not Sentry's — it is every integrator's, that a feed safe to display is also safe to obey. The same reasoning is now embedded in dozens of MCP servers wrapping ticketing systems, log stores, monitoring and code review, each of which accepts writes from a wider population than its readers realise.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-01 (everything an agent reads is untrusted data, never instruction — the control the entire attack class exists to violate), AGENT-06 (human approval before an agent executes a command or writes to anything that matters, which converts a silent compromise into a prompt the developer can decline), AGENT-03 (private data, untrusted content and an external channel never combine unsupervised — an agent reading public-writable telemetry inside a repository with network access is that combination exactly), AGENT-02 (agent credentials scoped to the task rather than inherited from the developer's session), AGENT-07 (agent actions logged with their originating prompt and reversible, so an injected step can be found afterwards).

**What happened.** Klue, a Vancouver-based competitive- and market-intelligence platform, was breached on June 11–12, 2026 through what the company described as "a legacy credential associated with an integration service" that had been "originally provided to a third-party in 2022, for a limited pilot." Klue detected the intrusion on June 12, first disclosed on June 18 and confirmed the hack publicly on June 22. The extortion group Icarus claimed responsibility and threatened to publish the data, then appeared to begin deleting it; by June 25 a second group was making its own threats. This is the corpus's cleanest offboarding-failure record: the credential sat unused and unrevoked for four years and was then the entire breach.

**Entry.** A credential issued in 2022 to a third party for a limited pilot. Klue has declined to say what kind of credential it was, what the pilot was for, how long it ran, or who the recipient was — and, most importantly, has not explained why it was never revoked when the pilot ended. There is no evidence it was revoked at any point between 2022 and June 2026.

**Escalation.** Klue's product integrates with its customers' systems, so the credential led not to Klue's own data but to OAuth tokens and authentication keys for customer clouds and databases. Jamf's account is the clearest published view of the mechanism: CrowdStrike found the actor reached Jamf's Salesforce instance through Klue's integration, with activity isolated to June 11 and limited to Salesforce data reachable via the Klue integration credentials, and no lateral movement. Jamf disabled the Klue integration in Salesforce immediately. One stale vendor credential therefore became access to two hundred other companies' SaaS tenants — `escalation/supply-chain-cascade` in its most literal form.

**Blast radius.** Eleven companies confirmed exposure: Gong, Jamf, HackerOne, Huntress, Insurity, LastPass, OneTrust, Recorded Future, Snyk, Sprout Social and Tanium — a customer list weighted heavily toward security vendors, several of which hold other organisations' security data in turn. A second group claimed 195 affected Klue customers in total; that number comes from unverified threat actors and is recorded as a claim. LastPass customer support case data was specifically targeted. Neither Klue nor its customers have published per-company record counts. Klue said it saw indications that Icarus was in fact deleting the stolen data; Icarus's site went offline by June 25.

**Human factor.** Somebody ran a pilot in 2022, issued a credential to make it work, and finished the pilot. Nobody owned the credential afterwards, so nobody revoked it, and because it was never used again it never appeared in any report of anomalous activity — its dormancy was precisely what made it invisible. The blast radius was then determined by a second decision nobody revisited: that an integration credential should carry tokens into customer environments rather than be scoped per customer and per purpose.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook with a named owner and a stated maximum time-to-revoke, applied to pilots and proofs-of-concept as much as to production), CRED-07 (credentials expire — a 2022 credential with a lifetime would have died in 2022 with no human action at all), AUTH-08 (access is inventoried, owned, and expires when unused; four years of zero use is the loudest possible signal and nothing was listening), VENDOR-02 (granted OAuth tokens scoped, expiring and reviewed, with unused ones revoked — this is the control on the customer side, and it is why Jamf's exposure was contained to one Salesforce object set), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding a token into your systems, which is what let eleven companies answer the question quickly).

**What happened.** The Australian Privacy Commissioner published determinations dated 11 June 2026, announced on 24 June, finding that the telehealth provider Medmate Australia and the fertility clinic group Monash IVF interfered with the privacy of their users by deploying third-party tracking pixels that collected sensitive health information from their websites and passed it to social media platforms for targeted advertising. The Commissioner's position is that a provider using a pixel to collect sensitive information — health, political opinion, race or ethnicity — must obtain consent to do so, and that the ubiquity of online tracking does not exempt it from the Privacy Act. Neither determination publishes a count of affected individuals, so this record records the scale as undisclosed.

**Entry.** A marketing tag added to a website. Pixels are installed to measure campaigns, and they report the page the visitor was on; on a telehealth or fertility site the page title is the health condition. Nothing was hacked and nothing leaked in the usual sense — the disclosure was a designed feature of the tool, operating exactly as its vendor intended, applied to a page whose URL was a diagnosis.

**Escalation.** None required. The pixel's report goes directly from the visitor's browser to the advertising platform, so there is no server-side control point where a clinic could have inspected or filtered what left. That is precisely why the control has to sit at the decision to install, not at runtime.

**Blast radius.** Not quantified by the regulator. The character of the loss is what matters: the recipients were commercial advertising platforms whose business is building profiles, and the attribute disclosed was an inference about a person's fertility treatment or telehealth consultation, made without the person knowing a third party was in the room. Both determinations require consent before sensitive information is collected this way, which effectively ends untagged deployment of these pixels on Australian health sites.

**Human factor.** The pixels were almost certainly added by a marketing function that had no reason to think of a page view as a health record, under a growth objective, with no privacy review in the path between "add the tag" and "publish". Nobody in that chain made a reckless decision; the chain simply had no step in it where someone whose job is data protection saw the change. The same gap exists on most organisations' websites today.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-05 (third-party scripts on sensitive pages minimised, pinned or isolated — on a page whose URL is a diagnosis, the correct number of third-party scripts is zero), VENDOR-03 (authorising a new connected application or tag requires review, which is the step that was missing), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party receiving your data, including the ones that receive it from the visitor's browser rather than from your servers), DATA-09 (minimisation, including in what is sent to analytics), HUMAN-07 (a checklist for changes to pages that handle health information, with tag deployment as a named item).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Amazon S3 and Azure storage are private by default, and Amazon adds explicit warnings before public access is granted. All four identity-document exposures this quarter (Duc, Tabiq, the UK visa portal, Pay Tel) required somebody to override a safe default or to rely on unguessable object names — none was a platform failure, which means the control that closes them is inventory and enumeration, not a vendor fix.
- Vercel published a public knowledge-base bulletin naming the third-party AI tool that started the chain, engaged Google Mandiant and law enforcement, told affected customers to rotate credentials, and changed how environment variables are handled. Naming the upstream vendor rather than saying "a third party" is what let other Context.ai customers act.
- Jamf published its own customer-facing incident page for a breach that occurred entirely inside a vendor's environment, including the CrowdStrike findings and the containment timeline — a reusable template for downstream disclosure when you are the victim of someone else's breach.
- Registry and ecosystem takedowns were the fastest control in the quarter: all 84 malicious TanStack versions were deprecated within 1 hour 43 minutes of publication and removed within 4 hours 35 minutes, and the trojanised Bitwarden CLI 2026.4.0 was pulled after roughly 93 minutes with 334 downloads. Speed of removal, not prevention, is what capped these incidents — which is the argument for DEPS-03's adoption cooldown on the consuming side.
- Instructure disabled the Free-For-Teacher account tier outright rather than patching around it, after declaring the incident resolved on May 6 and being re-breached and defaced on May 7. The sequence is the guidance: do not publish "resolved" without evidence the actor's path is closed.
- Meta said the Instagram support-chatbot flaw was fixed on June 2, began force-resetting passwords on affected accounts, and started notifying users who had been targeted. The broader lesson the incident forced into the open is that an autonomous support agent with authority to change account-recovery identifiers needs a human approval step (AGENT-06), and that removing the escalate-to-human path removes the last check on impostor requests.
- Poland's ABW disclosed the water-sector intrusions in its public threat report, and Poland subsequently charged two Russian nationals over a campaign of 17 attacks on critical infrastructure including seven water and wastewater plants. CISA and the FBI issued a formal alert on internet-exposed PLCs in the water and wastewater sector on July 30, 2026 — just after this quarter, but a direct response to the same pattern: control equipment on the public internet, answering on a known port, with a default password.
- CERT-EU contacted the at least 29 other EU entities whose data appeared in the leaked archive rather than limiting notification to the European Commission, treating a single compromised cloud account as an incident for every organisation whose data it happened to hold.
