
**What happened.** At the start of January 2026 the Illinois Department of Human Services disclosed that health-related personal information about benefit recipients had been uploaded to a publicly accessible state website and left there. Reporting describes the material as having been reachable for more than three years before anyone noticed. A separate, unrelated Minnesota Department of Human Services incident was disclosed in the same window, and some aggregate reporting combines the two into a figure near one million people — which is why this record gives an imprecise figure and marks it disputed rather than adopting either number.

**Entry.** No attacker and no exploit. A file containing recipients' personal and health-related information was placed on a web location that required no authentication, by a person who intended it to be internal. Anyone who found the URL could read it.

**Escalation.** None required. The exposure point was the data itself; nothing further had to be reached or unlocked.

**Blast radius.** Hundreds of thousands of individuals served by the department, with health-related information among the fields exposed. Illinois did not publish a confirmed count at disclosure and no abuse was tied to the exposure at that point, so both the scale and the impact remain partially open.

**Human factor.** Someone published to the wrong place, and then nothing in the process looked again. A three-year exposure is not a single mistake — it is a single mistake plus the absence of any recurring check that enumerates what a government web property is actually serving to the public.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (storage and web-reachable locations private by default, with legacy paths audited), CLOUD-01 (nothing is publicly reachable unless it was decided to be, and the public surface is enumerated on a schedule rather than once), DATA-09 (retention limits, so a three-year-old extract is not still sitting there to be found).

```yaml
cost_usd: undisclosed

confidence: medium
controls: [CLOUD-05, DATA-08, DATA-12, AUTH-08, OBSV-02]
sources:
  - https://www.newspim.com/news/view/20260114001162
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/south-korean-giant-kyowon-confirms-data-theft-in-ransomware-attack
```

**What happened.** Kyowon Group is one of South Korea's large education and consumer conglomerates — the parent of the Kumon and Red Pen tutoring brands, plus travel, insurance and hospitality subsidiaries. Ransomware detonated inside the group on the morning of January 10, 2026, encrypting roughly 600 of its approximately 800 servers and taking most of its consumer services offline. Kyowon reported the incident to the Korea Internet & Security Agency the same day, and on January 14 told regulators that the personal information of about 5.54 million customers — 9.6 million registered accounts before de-duplication across eight affiliates — was in scope. No ransomware brand claimed the attack publicly.

**Entry.** Kyowon has not disclosed how the attackers got in, and no regulator has published a finding, so this record carries `entry/unknown`. The company confirmed that data left the network and that it was contacted with threats after the encryption, but described neither the initial foothold nor the exfiltration path.

**Escalation.** What is visible is the spread rather than the start. Eight separate operating companies — tutoring, travel, life insurance and others — were hit in the same event, and the group's shared internal authentication and management platform, known internally as KSS, was among the systems that went down. A single identity plane sitting across eight subsidiaries is the thing that converts one compromised affiliate into a group-wide outage.

**Blast radius.** Around 600 of 800 servers unusable, consumer web services and tutoring platforms down for days, and 5.54 million people whose records were in the affected systems. Kyowon said at disclosure that it was still determining which customer fields were actually included in the exfiltrated set, so the scale figure describes exposure scope and not confirmed loss. Backups were held on separately operated infrastructure and showed no sign of infection, which is why restoration was measured in days rather than months. South Korea's National Police Agency opened a cyber investigation and the Personal Information Protection Commission began examining the group for statutory violations.

**Human factor.** A holding company grows by acquiring and launching brands, and it consolidates the login and administration layer because that is cheaper and neater than eight of everything. Nobody writes down that this also consolidates the blast radius. The one decision that visibly paid off here was the opposite of consolidation: backups run on separate infrastructure with separate credentials were the reason there was anything to restore from.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between subsidiaries, so a shared identity and management plane does not hand one intrusion eight companies), AUTH-08 (access inventoried and owned per affiliate rather than group-wide by default), DATA-08 (separately-held, restore-tested backups — the control that demonstrably worked here and should be the model, not the exception), DATA-12 (cap what a single compromise can reach, so 5.5 million customer records across eight brands are not reachable from one foothold), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal read and export volume before the encryption stage announces the intrusion for you).


**What happened.** Endesa, Spain's largest electricity utility and a supplier to more than 20 million customers in Spain and Portugal, began notifying customers on January 12, 2026 that it had "detected evidence of unauthorised and illegitimate access to certain personal data of our customers related to their energy contracts". The data offered for sale afterwards was described by the seller as around a terabyte of SQL databases covering roughly 20 million records, marketed to a single exclusive buyer. Endesa did not publish a confirmed count, so the scale here is the attacker's claim rather than the company's finding.

**Entry.** Endesa attributed the access to a commercial platform holding contract data but did not describe how it was reached. No vulnerability, credential or vendor was named in the notification, which is why this record carries `entry/unknown`.

**Escalation.** None described, and probably none needed. The platform the attacker reached was the customer contract system itself; the records were the destination, not a stepping stone.

**Blast radius.** Customer identification details, contact information, Spanish national identity numbers (DNI), contract details and payment information including IBAN bank account numbers. Endesa stated that passwords were not among the exposed fields and that it had no evidence of fraudulent use at the time of notification. It blocked affected accounts, reviewed log records, notified the Agencia Española de Protección de Datos, and warned customers to expect identity-theft attempts and phishing that would quote real contract details back at them — which is the practical harm of a utility breach, because a caller who knows your DNI, your address and your electricity contract number is extremely convincing.

**Human factor.** A DNI and an IBAN are collected once, to set up a contract and a direct debit, and then live in the commercial platform for the life of the account and beyond. Nothing in normal utility operations requires that a customer service platform be able to return national identity numbers and bank details in bulk, but nothing in normal utility operations stops it either. The retention decision made years earlier is what determined how bad January 2026 was.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimisation and enforced retention — national identity numbers deleted after the identity check they were collected for, not carried for the life of the contract), DATA-07 (sensitive fields encrypted at rest so a platform compromise yields ciphertext for the fields that matter most), DATA-11 (bulk export as a privileged, logged, alerting action), DATA-12 (cap what one platform compromise can reach, in value and volume), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read volume, since a terabyte leaving a commercial platform is not a shape that normal customer service produces).

**What happened.** On Tuesday January 13, 2026 the AZ Monica hospital group in Antwerp took every server on both its Antwerp and Deurne campuses offline after detecting an intrusion. At least 70 operations scheduled for that day were cancelled, including non-deferrable ones; seven critically ill patients were transferred to other hospitals; the emergency department ran at reduced capacity and the mobile emergency and paramedic intervention teams were withdrawn from service. The hospital reopened the following day at roughly 50% and then 30% of normal care capacity while servers stayed down. External reporting described it as a ransomware attack; the hospital did not confirm an actor.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. AZ Monica described the detection and its response, not the path in. Security researchers examining the wider Flemish hospital estate in the days afterwards found credentials for the hospitals' shared IT service provider circulating from infostealer malware (recorded separately in this file), but no public finding ties that to AZ Monica's own intrusion, so the vector here remains unknown.

**Escalation.** Also not disclosed. The single observable fact is that the hospital judged it necessary to shut down every server on both campuses at once rather than isolate a segment — a decision that is either evidence of a flat estate or evidence of an incident team without enough visibility to scope the intrusion. Both are the same problem seen from different sides.

**Blast radius.** Two hospital campuses, at least 70 cancelled procedures on day one, seven critical-care transfers, an emergency department at reduced capacity, and weeks of degraded operation while the electronic patient record was rebuilt. A month later the hospital was still unable to calculate January salaries correctly because the payroll systems had not been restored. No patient-data exposure was confirmed by the hospital at disclosure.

**Human factor.** With no internal cause published, the failures named here are read from the outcome and should be treated that way. A hospital that must stop all seven of its emergency-response functions to contain an IT intrusion has no rehearsed degraded mode: no paper pathway that keeps theatre lists running, no isolated island for critical care, no pre-agreed threshold for when to transfer rather than cancel. Smaller hospitals rarely have a dedicated security team and buy the capability from a service provider, which moves the risk rather than reducing it.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-08 (restore-tested backups that production credentials cannot write to, which is the difference between a two-day outage and a month of manual payroll), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between clinical, administrative and campus tiers so that containment does not require shutting everything), OBSV-05 and HUMAN-07 together (a written incident plan and a rehearsed clinical downtime procedure, so the decision to cancel 70 operations is a step in a checklist rather than a judgement made at 8am with no information).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/01/14/ziekenhuizen-cyberaanval-impact/
```

**What happened.** While the AZ Monica intrusion was still being contained, the Belgian security researcher Geert Baudewijns checked whether the same weakness reached further. The IT service provider AZ Monica used also served three other Flemish hospitals. Using passwords harvested by commodity infostealer malware — the same class of credential dumps that ransomware crews buy — he was able to reach the provider's platforms at those three hospitals and read more than 71,000 patient records. VRT published the finding on January 14, 2026; the vulnerabilities were subsequently closed.

**Entry.** Stolen credentials, not an exploit. Infostealer malware running on someone's machine had captured working logins for the service provider's hospital platforms and shipped them to a collection server, from where they became purchasable. Nothing had to be broken: the password worked, and no second factor stood behind it.

**Escalation.** One supplier, one credential pattern, four hospitals. The service provider's access model did not partition its customers from one another in any way a stolen login would notice, so a credential harvested from one context opened patient platforms at institutions that had no relationship with each other and no ability to audit the arrangement.

**Blast radius.** More than 71,000 patient records at three hospitals, containing the contents of an identity card — names, home addresses and Belgian national registry numbers — alongside the fact of being that hospital's patient, which is itself health information. The researcher found no evidence that criminals had taken the same path, which is what makes this a useful record: identical mechanism, zero confirmed damage, and a fix applied before anyone had to notify patients.

**Human factor.** Small and mid-sized hospitals do not run IT departments; they buy one. The security properties of the purchase are almost never inspected, because the buyer is a hospital director assessing uptime and price, and the thing being bought is administrative access to the patient record of every institution on the supplier's book. Multi-factor authentication on the supplier's platform would have made the stolen passwords worthless; nobody with the authority to require it was in the room.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every account with reach into patient systems, which turns an infostealer dump into noise), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding patient data or a token into clinical systems — most hospitals on this supplier's list could not have named the exposure), VENDOR-08 (supplier access time-boxed and separately monitored, so a provider login used from an unusual place at an unusual hour is visible to the hospital, not just to the provider), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one customer and one purpose, never a pattern that works across a supplier's whole client base), HUMAN-04 (contractors and subprocessors held to the same controls as employees).


**What happened.** Prosura is an Australian insurer specialising in vehicle rental cover — a mid-sized business, not a household name. It detected unauthorised access to its internal IT systems on January 3, 2026 and disclosed publicly on January 14, by which time the attackers had already begun emailing Prosura's own customers directly. When Prosura did not meet their demands, the stolen data was posted for sale on a public forum. About 300,000 customers were affected; the sellers advertised 98 million lines of data, a figure Prosura did not confirm.

**Entry.** Prosura said only that its internal IT systems were compromised. No vector, credential or vulnerability was named publicly, so the initial foothold is recorded as unknown.

**Escalation.** None described. The systems reached held the policy and claims records directly, and the attackers' behaviour — straight to customer contact and then to a forum listing — is the behaviour of people who already had what they came for.

Credit card and payment data were not reached. The licence images are the durable harm: a scanned identity document cannot be reissued as easily as a password, and in Australia a licence number with a name and address is the standard input to identity verification. The extortion phase included direct fraudulent emails to customers, which turned a data breach into a live phishing campaign against the victims while the company was still assessing scope.

**Human factor.** Claims handling requires a customer to photograph their licence and send it in. Nothing then deletes it. The document sits in the claims record because storage is cheap, because an auditor might one day ask, and because no one owns the question of when it should go. A rental-insurance claim closed in 2021 does not need a licence photograph in 2026, but it still had one.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-07 (identity documents encrypted at rest and deleted once the verification they support has completed), DATA-09 (enforced retention limits on closed claims, which is the single control that would have shrunk this breach the most), DATA-11 (bulk export treated as privileged, logged and alerting), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal read volume rather than learning the size of the extraction from a forum listing), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a named communicator — when attackers mail your customers on day two, the organisation that has not decided who speaks loses the narrative and the customers' trust at once).


**What happened.** On January 15, 2026, weeks before the Australian school year began, Victoria's Department of Education told parents that an unauthorised third party had accessed the personal information of current and former students across all 1,700 of the state's government schools. The department said the intruder reached the central student account directory after first compromising one school's network. Student names, school-issued email addresses, year levels and encrypted account passwords were taken; the department stated that dates of birth, home addresses and phone numbers were held elsewhere and were not reached.

**Entry.** The department described the path as "a compromised school network" without naming how that first school was compromised, so the initial foothold is recorded as unknown. What it did describe is the step that mattered: from inside one school's network, the central directory serving every other school was reachable.

**Escalation.** A single statewide student identity system, reachable from any one of 1,700 school networks, means the weakest school in the state sets the security level for all of them. Schools differ enormously in staffing, patching and network hygiene; the directory they all authenticate against did not differentiate between them. That is flat-network escalation at the scale of a state.

**Blast radius.** Every government school in Victoria and hundreds of thousands of current and former students. The department reset every student password, which locked students out of their school accounts entirely, and prioritised reissuing credentials to final-year students sitting VCE examinations so they could get back to work before term started. No evidence emerged that the stolen data was published or sold. The recovery cost is the part that rarely appears in a breach notice: a statewide credential reset days before a school year is an operational event for 1,700 sites and every family attached to them.

**Human factor.** Education systems centralise identity because it is the only way to run 1,700 schools, and then connect it to networks administered by people whose main job is teaching. The security model assumed the schools were inside the trust boundary; the incident established that "inside" was 1,700 separate front doors, each one sufficient.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation, so one school's network is a client of the central directory and not a peer inside it), CLOUD-06 (the least-hardened site on the network carries production-grade authentication or has no production reach), AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions and credential rotation on privilege change, so a stolen directory foothold ages out), DATA-12 (cap what one compromised site can enumerate — no single school network needs to read every student record in the state), OBSV-02 (alerting on a bulk directory read, which is what this looked like from the logs' point of view).


**What happened.** MnCHOICES is the assessment system Minnesota uses to determine eligibility for long-term care and disability services; it is operated for the state by the vendor FEI Systems. On November 18, 2025 a user affiliated with a licensed health care provider was found to have accessed records in MnCHOICES far beyond what their role required. The vendor reported it to the department the next day, and the Minnesota Department of Human Services disclosed publicly on January 16, 2026 that 303,965 people were affected. This is a separate incident from the Illinois Department of Human Services exposure recorded earlier in this quarter; aggregate reporting that combines the two into a single "nearly one million people" figure is conflating them.

**Entry.** A legitimate login. The user was authorised to use MnCHOICES and authorised to see a limited set of data in it. Nothing was exploited, phished or stolen — the account simply asked the system for more than its job needed, and the system answered.

**Escalation.** The credential was the escalation. MnCHOICES authenticated the user correctly and then applied no meaningful per-record authorisation on top: a provider-affiliated account that should have been able to see its own clients' assessments could instead read across the population. That is an overscoped credential in its purest form, with no attacker, no malware and no second step.

**Blast radius.** 303,965 individuals, with names, addresses, email addresses, dates of birth, phone numbers, Medicaid identifiers, the last four digits of Social Security numbers, and the sensitive assessment context that a long-term care eligibility system necessarily holds: ethnicity, race, education, income, benefit and program-eligibility details. These are people assessed for disability and long-term care support — among the least able to absorb an identity-fraud problem.

**Human factor.** Every records system has a role model that was designed for the common case and a query surface that was designed for the hard case, and the gap between them is where this lives. The person who wrote the access rule assumed provider users would look up their own clients; nobody tested what happened when one did not. Detection came from the vendor after the fact rather than from a control at the time, which means the system had no notion of what a normal volume of lookups looks like for a single provider account.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-02 (authorisation checked server-side, per object, on every request — a provider account asks for a record and the system decides whether that provider has a relationship with that person), DATA-12 (cap what one account can reach, in volume as well as in kind), DATA-11 and OBSV-02 (bulk or unusual-volume reads treated as privileged, logged and alerting, so 300,000 records do not leave through a normal user session unremarked), AUTH-08 (access inventoried, owned and expiring, particularly for external provider-affiliated accounts), HUMAN-09 (insider risk monitored by behaviour rather than by trust), OBSV-01 (authorisation decisions and exports centrally logged so a review can answer "what did this account actually read?").

**What happened.** Moltbook launched as an "agent-first, human-second" social network where autonomous AI agents post to each other, and grew fast enough to attract high-profile attention. Researchers disclosed on January 31, 2026 that its entire production database was readable and writable by anyone with a browser. The exposure covered 35,000 email addresses and 1.5 million agent API tokens across roughly 770,000 active agents.

**Entry.** Moltbook shipped its Supabase project URL and publishable ("anon") key inside the client JavaScript bundle — which is normal and safe only if Postgres row-level security is enabled. It was not. With no RLS policy on the tables, that publicly visible key granted full read and write authority over every row in the database, exactly as if the service-role key had been published.

**Escalation.** None required, and this is the whole finding. There was no second step: the key visible in the page source was already the key to the database. Worse, the data was layered — private messages between agents contained plaintext third-party credentials, including OpenAI API keys agents had shared with one another, stored in the same unencrypted table as the tokens needed to hijack the agent itself.

**Blast radius.** 1.5 million agent API tokens (each of which allows impersonation of that agent), about 35,000 owner email addresses, owner-to-agent relationship mappings, claim tokens and verification codes, and private agent-to-agent messages containing live third-party API keys. No malicious exploitation was confirmed before the researchers reported it; the fix was two SQL statements enabling row-level security.

**Human factor.** A product built and shipped at high speed treated a Supabase default as a security decision that had already been made. Row-level security is opt-in, the publishable key is designed to be public, and the combination is only safe when someone explicitly writes the policy — nobody did, and nobody ran the one test that would have caught it.

Two ordinary omissions — an unlisted file pattern and a bucket default — combined into a complete source disclosure, and the publish pipeline had no step that looked at what was actually in the tarball before it went out.

**Escalation.** None required and none possible: the artifact was published to the world's most-mirrored package registry, which distributes at the speed of `npm install`. Within hours the code was irretrievable in the literal sense — a clean-room rewrite built from it became one of the fastest-growing repositories GitHub has recorded, and the original mirror accumulated tens of thousands of forks. Unpublishing the version changed nothing.

**Blast radius.** No customer data and no credentials were exposed; Anthropic was explicit on both points, and no evidence has emerged to the contrary. What was lost was commercial and defensive: the implementation of a widely used coding agent, including its context-management pipeline and its tool definitions, which researchers immediately noted makes targeted jailbreaks and guardrail bypasses substantially easier to construct. The measurable harm arrived from a different direction — within days, criminals were distributing trojanised builds of Claude Code containing infostealers and cryptocurrency miners through repositories that looked like the mirrors everyone was already cloning.

**Human factor.** This is the most ordinary failure in the corpus and it happened at one of the most security-attentive companies in the industry, which is precisely why it is worth recording. Releases are automated so that they are boring, and the automation's default behaviour changed beneath a team that had no reason to look. Nobody runs `tar -tzf` on their own release artifact, in the same way nobody reads their own outbound email attachments, and the packaging manifest is an allowlist that everyone writes once and no one revisits.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-03 (build provenance and artifact verification — an automated check that the published tarball contains exactly the file set the manifest declares, and no source maps, which is a ten-line CI step), HUMAN-07 (a release checklist that includes inspecting the artifact, rather than relying on a competent operator and a familiar command), CICD-09 (release approval by a second human who looks at the contents, not just the version number), CLOUD-01 and DATA-03 (nothing publicly reachable unless it was decided to be, and object storage private by default — the `src.zip` bucket was the second half of this chain and would have broken it on its own).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **Aqua Security published GHSA-69fq-xp46-6x23** naming safe versions (Trivy binary v0.69.2 and v0.69.3, `trivy-action` v0.35.0, `setup-trivy` v0.2.6) and the precise compromise windows, and Microsoft, Docker, Wiz, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, AppOmni and Legit Security each published detection, investigation and rotation playbooks within days — an unusually fast, coordinated downstream response to a compromise of a security tool.
- **The Trivy case made tag pinning a live argument rather than a theoretical one.** Because the attacker force-pushed existing version tags, teams that had pinned to `@v0.x` were hit and teams that had pinned to a commit SHA were not. CICD-04 moved from best practice to the difference between affected and unaffected.
- **Anthropic assigned and published CVEs for its own agent tooling** (CVE-2025-59536, CVE-2026-21852) and shipped fixes in Claude Code 1.0.87, 1.0.111 and 2.0.65 before Check Point's February 25 write-up — establishing, early, that agent configuration flaws get CVEs and coordinated disclosure like any other product vulnerability.
- **OpenClaw patched CVE-2026-25253 in version 2026.1.29 within days**, and national and institutional CERTs including Belgium's Centre for Cybersecurity and the University of Toronto issued patch-immediately advisories — the first time an AI personal-assistant vulnerability was treated by national CERTs as ordinary critical infrastructure guidance.
- **Snyk's ToxicSkills audit of 3,984 agent skills** (13.4% carrying at least one critical issue) and Antiy CERT's count of 1,184 malicious skills gave the agent-skill ecosystem its first empirical baseline, and pushed marketplace review and publisher verification onto the agenda of every agent platform.
- **Repeated internet-wide MCP scans** by Knostic, Trend Micro and others turned "expose an MCP server without authentication" from an abstract risk into a measured population, and drove the zero-trust framing for agent tooling: authenticate the caller of a tool endpoint the same way you would authenticate the caller of an admin API.
- **Supabase row-level security became the quarter's most-repeated remediation.** The Moltbook exposure was fixed with two SQL statements, and the case was widely cited afterwards as the canonical example of why a publishable client key is only safe when default-deny RLS is enabled first — a default that has to be chosen, not inherited.
