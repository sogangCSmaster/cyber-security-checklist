# 2016 Q3 — Credential Theft, Vendor Trust, Delayed Disclosure

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/unknown — four of ten records disclosed no public root cause; where one was confirmed, stolen, reused, or harvested credentials were the recurring thread (Oracle MICROS, Dropbox, Sage).
> - **New technique this quarter:** State-grade exploit tools entering public and criminal circulation — the Shadow Brokers' August 13 leak of NSA-linked firewall exploits had no real precedent at that scale.
> - **Recurring failure:** A narrow initial foothold reaching far more than it should have, through flat networks or unscoped credentials (Banner Health, Oracle MICROS, Sage).
> - **Records in this file:** 10

**What happened.** Banner Health, a nonprofit hospital system based in Arizona, disclosed on August 3, 2016 that attackers had reached systems touching 3.7 million patients, health plan members, and food-and-beverage customers. The company said the intrusion began around mid-June 2016 and was not caught until early July, by which point the attackers had moved well beyond the payment terminals they first touched.

**Entry.** Banner has never publicly detailed how the attackers first got in. Its own statements and the later civil litigation identify the earliest confirmed foothold as the payment-card processing systems at Banner's food and beverage outlets, without naming the initial technique.

**Escalation.** From the payment environment, the attackers reached servers holding electronic health records, insurance and claims data, and Social Security numbers. The HHS Office for Civil Rights later cited "long-term, pervasive noncompliance with the HIPAA Security Rule" at Banner, consistent with no meaningful separation between the retail payment network and the clinical data network.

**Blast radius.** 3.7 million patients, health plan members, and physicians had names, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, and health insurance and claims data exposed; roughly 30,000 food-and-beverage customers had payment card numbers exposed separately. A federal class action settled in 2020 for $8.9 million, including a $6 million victim compensation fund.

**Human factor.** Separating a cafeteria's card readers from a hospital's clinical network is a design decision, not an accident. Banner's payment environment and its patient-data environment were reachable from the same compromised position, and nothing suggests anyone had recently tested that they weren't.

**What would have stopped it.** Network segmentation between the payment card environment and clinical systems (CLOUD-05); centralized logging of admin and data-access events (OBSV-01); alerting on abnormal read or export volume from EHR systems (OBSV-02).

```

**What happened.** KrebsOnSecurity reported on August 8, 2016 that a Russian-speaking cybercrime group, suspected to be the Carbanak gang, had breached hundreds of systems inside Oracle's MICROS point-of-sale division, including the customer support portal used by businesses running an estimated 330,000 MICROS cash registers worldwide. Oracle confirmed it had "detected and addressed malicious code in certain legacy MICROS systems" and reset all support-portal passwords.

**Entry.** A single infected system inside Oracle's own corporate network was the attackers' initial foothold. Investigators found malicious code planted on the MICROS customer support portal itself, positioned to harvest the usernames and passwords of MICROS customers as they logged in for support.

**Escalation.** From that one infected system, the intruders moved laterally to more than 700 other systems inside Oracle's network, reaching the support portal used by its retail, hospitality, and food-service customer base — turning a single compromised vendor into a potential path into thousands of downstream merchants' own point-of-sale environments.

**Blast radius.** Oracle never disclosed how many of the roughly 330,000 MICROS-connected businesses had portal credentials stolen, or whether any of their own terminals were later compromised using those credentials. The confirmed loss is the support-portal credentials themselves, harvested at login; the downstream risk to customer card data was never publicly quantified.

**Human factor.** MICROS customers extended standing trust to a single vendor portal for support and software access. When that portal was quietly modified to harvest credentials, nothing in the login flow would have looked different to a legitimate customer — the victims here did nothing wrong in their own environments.

**What would have stopped it.** Internal network segmentation to contain a single infected host before it reaches customer-facing systems (CLOUD-05); mandatory credential rotation for any account that touched a breached vendor portal (CRED-03); active monitoring of vendor security advisories so downstream customers know when to act (VENDOR-06).

**What happened.** UK accounting and payroll software provider Sage began notifying customers on August 11, 2016 that an employee had used a legitimate internal company login to access payroll and personal data belonging to an estimated 200–300 of its UK business customers. City of London Police arrested a 32-year-old Sage employee on August 17 on suspicion of conspiracy to defraud.

**Entry.** No external hacking was involved. The individual had a working internal login to Sage's own systems and used it to view customer records outside the scope of their job.

**Blast radius.** Employee personal data — reportedly including bank account details — at up to 280 UK small and mid-sized businesses was exposed. Sage did not publicly specify the total number of individuals affected; the UK Information Commissioner's Office opened an inquiry under the Data Protection Act.

**Human factor.** One employee could reach hundreds of customers' payroll data through a single internal login. That is a provisioning decision, not a hacking technique — nobody had asked whether this role needed that reach before it was used to cause harm.

**What would have stopped it.** Default-deny, per-customer row-level access controls even for internal tooling (DATA-01); access reviews that tie each account's scope to current job need (AUTH-08); behavioral monitoring for insider access patterns rather than trusting anyone with a valid login (HUMAN-09).

```

**What happened.** On August 13, 2016, a group calling itself "the Shadow Brokers" posted roughly 300MB of firewall exploit code and hacking tools online, claiming to have stolen them from the "Equation Group" — a name researchers use for a toolset Kaspersky Lab had independently linked to the NSA. The group offered a larger cache for auction, demanding one million bitcoin, and released the first batch as proof it was real.

**Entry.** Neither the Shadow Brokers nor any government source has ever publicly disclosed how the tools were obtained. Theories range from an external intrusion into a staging server to an insider with direct access, and none was confirmed in the quarter this was disclosed; entry/unknown reflects that gap rather than a specific mechanism.

**Escalation.** Not established. Whoever obtained the material appears to have had direct access to a working exploit-development cache rather than needing to move laterally to reach it, though this is inference rather than a confirmed fact.

**Blast radius.** The immediate leak included exploit code for firewalls from Cisco, Juniper, and Fortinet; further batches followed over the next year. The tools' full significance became clear only in 2017, when leaked exploits — including what became known as EternalBlue — were repurposed in the WannaCry and NotPetya outbreaks, a direct downstream consequence of this quarter's disclosure.

**Human factor.** A government intelligence agency's offensive toolkit, built and retained specifically to stay undetected, ended up in public and criminal hands. Whatever the mechanism, nobody outside the group that took it has ever confirmed when or how it left.

**What would have stopped it.** Insider-risk monitoring and access review for anyone with reach into an exploit-development environment (HUMAN-09); inventoried, time-bound access to sensitive internal tooling rather than indefinite standing access (AUTH-08); alerting on abnormal access to or export from a sensitive code repository (OBSV-02).

**What happened.** On August 16, 2016, German cable and wiring-systems manufacturer Leoni AG disclosed that finance staff at its Bistrița, Romania factory had wired approximately €40 million (roughly $44 million at the time) to attacker-controlled bank accounts after receiving emails impersonating the company's German headquarters executives.

**Entry.** This was spear-phishing, not a generic scam: the attackers appear to have known that the Bistrița plant was the only one of Leoni's four Romanian factories authorized to execute international money transfers, and crafted their request to fit that authority.

**Escalation.** None required. A single approved transfer, authorized by the deceived employee, was the entire attack. No system was compromised and no malware was involved.

**Blast radius.** Approximately €40 million left the company; Leoni said its overall liquidity was not materially affected, but its share price fell roughly 5–8% on the news, depending on the report. The figure is sometimes given as $44–45 million once converted — a currency-conversion difference, not a factual dispute.

**Human factor.** Whoever authorized the transfer had no independent way to confirm the request was genuine: no callback to a known number, no second signer, no separate channel check before the money moved.

**What would have stopped it.** A second-person approval requirement on high-value wire transfers (HUMAN-02); a documented, checklist-driven process for authorizing large transfers regardless of who appears to be asking (HUMAN-07); verification of payment destinations through a channel independent of the request itself (HUMAN-10).

```

**What happened.** On August 25, 2016, security research firm MedSec published findings, released jointly with investment firm Muddy Waters Capital, describing vulnerabilities in St. Jude Medical's radio-frequency-enabled pacemakers, implantable defibrillators, and the Merlin@home home-monitoring transmitter that MedSec said could crash a device or drain its battery. Muddy Waters disclosed a short position in St. Jude's stock the same day.

**Entry.** This was disclosed research, not an exploited intrusion. MedSec said it tested purchased, available devices and found the wireless command interface would accept malformed or unauthenticated commands capable of affecting device behavior. No patient device is known to have been attacked using the technique; entry/zero-day is used here as the closest available tag for a disclosed-but-unexploited flaw, not as a claim of confirmed exploitation.

**Escalation.** None required in principle — if the claims held, an attacker within radio range would not need any further foothold, since the flaw was in the command interface itself.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed patient harm occurred. St. Jude disputed the findings and sued MedSec and Muddy Waters for false statements and market manipulation. An independent penetration tester (Bishop Fox) later said it replicated several of the attacks, while University of Michigan researchers disputed some specific claims. The FDA confirmed cybersecurity vulnerabilities in St. Jude's Merlin@home system in a January 2017 safety communication, followed by a firmware update.

**Human factor.** MedSec routed its findings through a short-selling partner rather than to the manufacturer or a coordinated disclosure body, correctly anticipating a stock-price reaction. That choice, not the underlying vulnerability, is what makes this record unusual: the incentive to disclose responsibly and the incentive to profit from disclosure pointed in different directions.

**What would have stopped it.** Cryptographic authentication on any wireless interface that can change an implanted device's behavior, so a nearby unauthenticated party cannot issue commands (AUTH-NEW: RF/wireless command interfaces on embedded and medical devices must authenticate the sender before accepting state-changing commands — not yet a numbered control in this checklist); a published, monitored channel for researchers to report vulnerabilities directly to the manufacturer (OBSV-06), which might have kept this out of a short-seller's hands in the first place.

**What happened.** On August 29, 2016, national reporting on an FBI "Flash" alert revealed that hackers had breached the Illinois State Board of Elections' voter registration database, downloading records on a disputed number of voters (early estimates of up to 200,000 were later revised toward roughly 90,000), and had separately targeted Arizona's voter registration system with malware in the months before. Illinois took its registration system offline for about ten days after discovering the intrusion in July.

**Entry.** Illinois election officials confirmed the attackers used SQL injection — a web form that failed to separate user input from database commands — to reach the voter database directly. Arizona's incident was reported as different in kind: malware associated with a compromised county official's login credentials rather than an injection flaw.

**Escalation.** None required for Illinois — the SQL injection flaw returned voter records directly once exploited, with no further privilege escalation described. Arizona's system was taken offline before any data was confirmed removed.

**Blast radius.** Illinois voter names, addresses, driver's license or state ID numbers, and party affiliation were exposed for an estimated 90,000 to 200,000 registered voters; Arizona reported no confirmed data loss. Federal officials said at the time they believed the same actors behind the DNC breach were involved; a 2018 U.S. indictment of Russian military intelligence officers later named the Illinois State Board of Elections breach specifically.

**Human factor.** A public-facing voter-lookup form accepted unsanitized input into a database query — the kind of flaw a basic security code review or automated scanner should catch, on a system that, in a presidential election year, was always going to be targeted.

**What would have stopped it.** Parameterized queries on every database-facing form, eliminating string-concatenated SQL entirely (INPUT-01); server-side input validation independent of any client-side checks (INPUT-02); centralized logging of database query failures and anomalous query patterns (OBSV-01).

```

**What happened.** On August 30, 2016, reporting confirmed that a file of roughly 68 million Dropbox usernames and hashed passwords, stolen in a 2012 breach, was authentic and circulating for sale. Dropbox forced a password reset for affected accounts still using the exposed credentials; the company had not previously disclosed the full scale of the 2012 incident.

**Entry.** A Dropbox employee had reused, on an internal company system, a password already exposed in the 2012 LinkedIn breach. Attackers used that reused credential to log into the employee's account.

**Escalation.** From the employee's account, the attacker reached a store of user email addresses and password hashes far larger than what Dropbox originally described. Dropbox's 2012 notice had mentioned only "a project document containing user email addresses" — understating what later surfaced as a 68-million-record credential set.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 68 million accounts' email addresses and password hashes were exposed, hashed with a mix of the weaker SHA-1 and the stronger bcrypt algorithm, both salted; no passwords are known to have been cracked at scale. Dropbox subsequently mandated two-factor authentication for employee accounts and adopted a password manager internally.

**Human factor.** One employee's password habits — reused across a personal LinkedIn account and a work system — were the entire difference between an isolated incident and a 68-million-record exposure, because nothing else stood between that password and a large internal credential store.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on every employee account with any production access, so a reused password alone cannot authenticate (AUTH-01); modern memory-hard password hashing applied uniformly rather than a mixed legacy scheme (DATA-06).

**What happened.** On September 22, 2016, Yahoo disclosed that "at least" 500 million user accounts had been stolen by what it described as a state-sponsored actor, in an intrusion that occurred in December 2014. Yahoo's own security team learned of the breach within days of it happening, according to a later SEC order — but the company did not tell the public for close to two years, disclosing only as it was finalizing its sale to Verizon.

**Entry.** Yahoo's September 2016 disclosure did not detail how the attacker first got in, and that specific mechanism is not independently confirmed here; entry/unknown reflects what Yahoo actually stated at the time rather than later, unverified claims.

**Escalation.** The attacker reached Yahoo's user database and account-management systems — a reach that, for a company Yahoo's size, implies movement beyond a single initial foothold, even though Yahoo did not describe the specific path.

In 2018 the SEC fined Yahoo $35 million specifically for misleading investors by failing to properly assess and disclose the breach in a timely way; separate consumer and shareholder litigation added further cost not attributed here with precision, since it covered this breach jointly with a second, separately disclosed 2013 breach.

**Human factor.** Yahoo's security team knew about this breach within days of it happening. The two-year gap between detection and disclosure was a decision about what to report and when, repeated through several rounds of internal review — not a failure to notice.

**What would have stopped it.** A written incident response plan with a named decision-maker, a communications owner, and a fixed disclosure clock that does not depend on unrelated business events like an acquisition (OBSV-05); a post-incident review process that produces an actual control change rather than an internal report that sits for two years (OBSV-09).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The FBI issued a Flash alert (August 18, 2016) to state election officials urging them to audit voter-registration systems for SQL injection and unauthorized access ahead of the November election, directly in response to the Illinois and Arizona intrusions.
- Cisco, Juniper, and Fortinet each issued advisories in the days after the Shadow Brokers leak, confirming and providing mitigation guidance for firewall flaws exposed in the leaked exploit code.
- Oracle forced a password reset across all MICROS customer support portal accounts and urged customers to rotate any credentials reused on other systems.
- Dropbox rolled out mandatory two-factor authentication for employee accounts and adopted a password manager company-wide, a direct response to the rediscovered scale of its 2012 breach.
- The UK Information Commissioner's Office opened a formal inquiry into the Sage Group breach under the Data Protection Act.
- The FDA opened a review of MedSec's pacemaker vulnerability claims following the August disclosure, a process that led to a safety communication and firmware update the following year.
