# 2016 Q1 — Payroll Fraud, a SWIFT Heist, Ransomware

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/spear-phishing — tax season produced CEO-spoofed payroll scams at Snapchat and Seagate, plus a malicious email attachment that took down a hospital.
> - **New technique this quarter:** macro-enabled Office documents used to deliver ransomware (not just banking trojans) at mass scale — Locky went from unknown to hundreds of thousands of daily infection attempts within two weeks of its mid-February debut.
> - **Recurring failure:** sensitive requests and exports were acted on without an independent check — spoofed executive emails (Snapchat, Seagate) and a legitimate-looking automated tool (IRS) were each trusted without a second look.
> - **Records in this file:** 11

```yaml
  - https://www.scmagazine.com/news/breach/irs-hackers-try-to-use-stolen-ssns-to-generate-e-file-pins
```

**What happened.** Starting in January 2016, automated bots hit the IRS's Electronic Filing PIN tool on IRS.gov, attempting to generate e-file PINs using Social Security numbers and other identity data stolen in prior, unrelated breaches. The IRS detected and shut down the activity on February 9, 2016.

**Entry.** The tool released a PIN in exchange for an SSN plus a few other identity details (date of birth, address, filing status) — details already circulating from earlier breaches. Scripts submitted roughly 464,000 stolen SSNs against the tool with no rate limiting or bot detection in front of it.

**Escalation.** None required — a successfully retrieved PIN is itself the credential needed to file a fraudulent return in the victim's name, so no further access inside IRS systems was necessary.

**Blast radius.** Of 464,000 SSNs attempted, about 101,000 returned a valid PIN before the tool was taken offline. The IRS said no data was taken from its own systems, since the input data was already stolen elsewhere; affected accounts were flagged for identity-theft monitoring.

**Human factor.** The identity-verification questions behind the tool were answerable from data already sold on criminal markets, and the endpoint had no meaningful limit on how many times it could be queried in a short window.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-06 (rate limiting, lockout, breached-data checks on the auth surface), AUTH-05 (a recovery path as strong as the login path), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal volume against a single endpoint).

```yaml
  - https://www.voanews.com/a/la-hospital-ransomware-attack-worries-cybersecurity-experts/3198112.html
```

**What happened.** On February 5, 2016, ransomware locked electronic medical records, email, and other systems at Hollywood Presbyterian Medical Center in Los Angeles. Staff reverted to paper charts and fax machines, and some patients were diverted to other hospitals. The hospital paid a ransom of 40 bitcoin (about $17,000) on February 18 to get a decryption key.

**Entry.** The hospital never publicly named the exact malware. Most press coverage called it Locky, a strain spread by email attachments disguised as invoices with malicious macros — but Locky's own first mass mailing is independently dated to February 16, eleven days after this outage began, so the strain was most likely a different, unidentified ransomware delivered the same way: a malicious email attachment opened by staff. No public source has conclusively named the strain.

**Escalation.** Once triggered on one machine, the ransomware reached systems across the hospital, consistent with internal systems and file shares that were not segmented from one another.

**Blast radius.** Every major clinical IT system was unavailable for over a week and the hospital declared an internal emergency. No theft of patient data was confirmed — the impact was encryption and unavailability, not exfiltration.

**Human factor.** A staff member opened and enabled content in a malicious attachment; nothing then stopped the payload from reaching other systems, and there was no tested offline backup that would have made paying unnecessary.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-08 (restore-tested, offline backups), HUMAN-05 (training against current malicious-attachment technique), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan that weighs restoring from backup against paying before a crisis, not during one).

**What happened.** An email impersonating Snap Inc. CEO Evan Spiegel asked a payroll employee for current and former staff W-2 data. The employee sent the file; about fifteen minutes after replying, the employee realized the request was fraudulent. Snapchat notified the FBI within four hours.

**Entry.** A spoofed email made to look like it came from Spiegel's own address was sent directly to a specific payroll employee with a plausible, time-pressured request — a targeted business email compromise, not a mass phishing blast.

**Escalation.** None required — the targeted employee already had the access and authority to produce and send the requested data in one step.

**Blast radius.** W-2 data (name, Social Security number, wages, stock-option gains, benefits) for about 700 current and former employees reached the attacker.

**Human factor.** The request read as ordinary, urgent executive business and was acted on without an independent check — a phone call or a second approver — before sensitive payroll data left the company.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-10 (confirming recipients and destinations before sensitive data leaves the org), HUMAN-02 (a second person required for bulk PII disclosure), HUMAN-05 (training that names CEO-spoofing payroll scams specifically).

**What happened.** On March 1, 2016, a Seagate Technology payroll/HR employee replied to an email spoofed to look like a request from the company's CEO and sent 2015 W-2 tax data for the US workforce to the sender. Seagate reported the incident to the IRS and FBI the same day it learned of it.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-10 (confirming recipients/destinations before sensitive data leaves the org), HUMAN-02 (a second person required for bulk PII disclosure), HUMAN-05 (training naming this exact scam pattern).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2016/05/24/479311978/-81-million-bangladesh-bank-heist-sparks-push-for-stepped-up-cybersecurity
  - https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/report-swift-hacked-by-bangladesh-bank-attackers-a-9061
```

**What happened.** Around February 4-5, 2016, attackers used Bangladesh Bank's own SWIFT credentials to send nearly three dozen fraudulent transfer instructions to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, attempting to move almost $951 million out of the central bank's account. Most transfers were blocked on suspicion, but five totaling $81 million reached mule accounts at a Philippine bank, opened about a year earlier under false identities, and were laundered through casinos. News broke internationally in early March 2016.

**Entry.** How the attackers first reached Bangladesh Bank's network has never been conclusively established in public reporting; a spear-phishing email is the most-cited theory but was not forensically confirmed in any public document. What is confirmed is that the computers used to access the SWIFT Alliance Access software sat on the bank's general network. Public attribution to North Korea's Lazarus Group came later in 2016-2018 (technical links by outside researchers, then a 2018 US Department of Justice indictment), not at the time of this quarter's disclosure.

**Escalation.** The bank's SWIFT terminals shared a network with its real-time gross settlement system and the open internet, with no firewall between them, so a foothold anywhere reached the systems that move money internationally. Malware later found on the terminals had altered SWIFT confirmation messages and suppressed printed transaction logs to delay discovery.

**Blast radius.** $81 million was stolen against an attempted ~$951 million; the bank's governor resigned, and the incident triggered years of litigation among Bangladesh Bank, the receiving Philippine bank, and casino operators.

**Human factor.** SWIFT access was treated as just another application on the general network rather than as the highest-value system in the building: no segmentation, no dedicated firewall, and no independent monitoring of an interface that can move hundreds of millions of dollars.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between SWIFT terminals and the general network), AUTH-07 (separate auth and full audit on high-privilege payment tooling), OBSV-01 (centralized logging and alerting on SWIFT message activity).


**What happened.** In mid-March 2016, malicious advertisements began appearing on some of the web's highest-traffic sites — the New York Times, BBC, AOL, MSN, Newsweek, The Hill, and others — silently redirecting visitors toward the Angler exploit kit. None of the publishers' own sites were compromised.

**Entry.** The attackers did not breach any publisher. They got malicious ad creatives approved by legitimate ad exchanges — Google, AppNexus, AOL, and Rubicon were all named — which the publishers' pages then served automatically as part of normal ad rotation. The path in was the ad-tech supply chain the sites depend on, not the sites themselves.

**Escalation.** The publishers' own scale did the rest: a single successful placement with an ad exchange was served to every visitor of every participating site, turning one vendor compromise into millions of impressions.

**Blast radius.** Visitors running a vulnerable browser or plugin were routed through the Bedep backdoor toward payloads including Cerber ransomware. Trend Micro, Trustwave, and Malwarebytes each independently observed the campaign and estimated tens of thousands of affected users; no publisher or ad network disclosed an official count.

**Human factor.** Publishers extended trust to their ad exchanges to vet the creatives shown on their pages, and that vetting did not catch malicious code before it reached readers.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-05 (minimizing/isolating third-party scripts on high-traffic pages), VENDOR-01 (an inventory and review of ad-tech vendors), VENDOR-06 (monitoring vendor advisories for this exact abuse pattern).

**What happened.** Researcher Chris Vickery found a MongoDB database belonging to Kinoptic, a discontinued iOS photo app, sitting open on the internet with no authentication required. He tried to reach Kinoptic's developers and Apple to get it secured; neither ever replied.

**What happened.** In March 2016, a seller on a cybercrime forum advertised a database of roughly 1.5 million Verizon Enterprise customer contact records for $100,000 (or $10,000 per 100,000-record batch), and separately offered to sell details of the underlying website vulnerability. **What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data), DATA-01 (default-deny, per-object authorization), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal export volume, which would flag roughly 1.5 million records leaving through one flaw).


**What happened.** On March 27, 2016, the group Anonymous Philippines defaced the website of the Philippine Commission on Elections (COMELEC), demanding stronger security for vote-counting machines ahead of the May 2016 election. The same day, a second group, LulzSec Pilipinas, posted download links to what it said was COMELEC's full voter database, roughly 340 GB of files.

**Entry.** The attackers used SQL injection: unescaped user input was concatenated directly into database queries on COMELEC's public website, giving direct access to the backend voter database.

**Escalation.** None required — the injection point reached the voter database directly.

**Blast radius.** Personal data for an estimated 55 million registered Philippine voters was exposed, including names, birthdates, addresses, passport numbers, and, in some records, fingerprint data — described at the time as the largest breach of government data in Philippine history. COMELEC moved its website to the government's Department of Science and Technology hosting and brought in outside security help, including Microsoft. A 20-year-old IT graduate student was later arrested by the National Bureau of Investigation as a suspect.

**Human factor.** The public-facing voter portal accepted unescaped input into database queries — a well-understood, long-fixable input-handling gap on a system holding the country's entire voter roll, including biometric data.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-01 (parameterized queries, no concatenated SQL), INPUT-02 (server-side validation on every input), OBSV-06 (a published channel for reporting the vulnerability before it was exploited).

**What happened.** On March 28, 2016, ransomware later attributed to the SamSam (Samas) family took down clinical and administrative systems across MedStar Health's ten Baltimore-Washington-area hospitals. MedStar said it paid no ransom and had most systems back online about a week later.

**Entry.** SamSam's operators scanned the internet for JBoss application servers vulnerable to a flaw that had been public, with a vendor patch available, since 2007 — nearly a decade before this attack — and used it to gain remote code execution on an internet-facing MedStar server.

**Escalation.** Once inside, the malware harvested passwords from the compromised host and used them to move across MedStar's network, encrypting files on additional systems instead of staying confined to the entry point.

**Blast radius.** MedStar took systems offline across all ten hospitals and affiliated facilities for about a week; the company did not publicly disclose whether patient data was copied, only that clinical operations were disrupted. US prosecutors later charged individuals in connection with the broader SamSam campaign, of which this was one of many victims.

**Human factor.** A known, patched vulnerability was left unaddressed on an internet-reachable system for years after fixes and public warnings existed, and the internal network had no segmentation to stop one compromised host from reaching the rest of the hospital system.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (regular enumeration of what is actually reachable from the internet), CLOUD-08 (admin/management interfaces not exposed publicly), CLOUD-05 (segmentation to contain lateral movement once one host is compromised).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **US-CERT/CISA and the Canadian Cyber Incident Response Centre** issued a joint ransomware alert (TA16-091A, March 31, 2016) naming Locky and Samas specifically and recommending offline/segmented backups, application whitelisting, macro-disabling, and prompt patching.
- **Microsoft shipped a new Office 2016 admin control** (announced March 22, 2016) letting enterprises block macros in documents that originate from the internet — a direct response to a macro-malware wave the company said accounted for 98% of Office-targeted threats.
- **The IRS suspended the abused Electronic Filing PIN retrieval function**, marked the roughly 464,000 affected SSNs for identity-theft monitoring, and continued its multi-year "Security Summit" push (with states and tax-software vendors) for stronger taxpayer identity checks ahead of the rest of the filing season.
- **Two CEO-spoofed W-2 frauds at named companies within four weeks (Snapchat, Seagate)**, on top of a broader wave hitting other employers that tax season, pushed "verify by phone before sending payroll data" from a suggestion into standard HR/finance practice at many companies.
- **COMELEC moved its website to Philippine government hosting** (Department of Science and Technology) and brought in outside security expertise, including Microsoft, after the SQL-injection breach of its voter database.
- **Ad networks and independent researchers** (Trend Micro, Trustwave, Malwarebytes) mapped and helped take down the malicious domains and creatives behind the Angler-EK malvertising campaign, renewing scrutiny of how major publishers vet third-party ad-tech.
- **The Bangladesh Bank heist** — discovered to have exploited SWIFT terminals with no firewall separating them from the general bank network — began the conversations between SWIFT and member banks that led, later in 2016, to SWIFT's mandatory Customer Security Programme.
