# 2016 Q2 — Recycled Credentials, Ransomware, and Unpatched Portals

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/phishing and entry/spear-phishing — three of ten records began with a deceived employee acting on an email (a W-2 impersonation, two ransomware droppers).
> - **New technique this quarter:** four-year-old breach dumps (LinkedIn among them) resurfacing on dark-web markets at their true scale and being fed directly into credential-stuffing tools against unrelated services.
> - **Recurring failure:** internet-facing software — a CMS plugin, a cloud database, a vendor's remote-access tool — left unpatched, unauthenticated, or unscoped long after a fix or a lock already existed.
> - **Records in this file:** 10


**What happened.** On April 3, 2016 the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists published the Panama Papers: 11.5 million documents (2.6 TB) taken from Panamanian law firm Mossack Fonseca, exposing decades of offshore company records for clients worldwide. Mossack Fonseca said it was the victim of an external hack but never gave a detailed public account of how the intruder got in.

**Entry.** Security researchers who examined the firm's public infrastructure after the leak found its main website ran a WordPress install three months out of date with a vulnerable Revolution Slider plugin, and its client document portal ran Drupal 7.23 — nearly two years unpatched and still exposed to the 2014 "Drupalgeddon" SQL injection vulnerability. Either was a plausible entry point; the firm never confirmed which, so this remains a researcher finding rather than an official post-mortem.

**Escalation.** The firm's web server and mail server appeared to share the same network with no segmentation between them, and the WordPress configuration file held database credentials in plain text where a web-level compromise could read them directly. Once inside, defeating the Drupal portal's access control gave reach to any client's confidential files with no further privilege escalation needed.

**Blast radius.** The leak itself is the only public accounting of what was taken: emails, PDFs, and corporate records covering roughly 214,000 offshore entities and their beneficial owners. The firm closed permanently two years later, citing the reputational and financial fallout.

**Human factor.** A firm whose entire business depended on client confidentiality ran its public-facing CMS and client portal on software with known, patchable vulnerabilities for months to nearly two years, and did not encrypt sensitive client email.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (vulnerability scanning that flags known-exploited CMS bugs before they age two years), CLOUD-05 (network segmentation so a web-server compromise cannot reach the mail server), CLOUD-09 (an asset inventory that would have caught a nearly-forgotten, badly outdated client portal).

```yaml
  - https://threatpost.com/sql-injection-attack-is-tied-to-election-commission-breach/122571/
```

**What happened.** On March 27, 2016, weeks before the Philippines' national election, hacktivist group Anonymous Philippines defaced COMELEC's website; days later a second group, LulzSec Pilipinas, posted the election commission's entire voter database online. By mid-April, outside researchers had made the leak fully searchable, exposing names, addresses, birth dates, and — for many voters — fingerprint images and passport numbers for more than 55 million registered voters, at the time the largest government data breach on record.

**Entry.** Security researchers tied the breach to a SQL injection vulnerability in the COMELEC website that allowed direct querying of the voter database through the public-facing application.

**Escalation.** None required: the injection point gave direct read access to the full voter database, letting the second group dump and republish it without any further foothold or privilege escalation.

**Blast radius.** More than 55 million voter records, including 1.3 million overseas voters, millions of fingerprint images, and COMELEC's own administrative account list, were posted online and remained freely searchable for weeks.

**Human factor.** COMELEC was publicly criticized for being slow to acknowledge and detail the breach even as the dumped data was already circulating and being indexed by outside researchers, leaving voters to learn of their own exposure from third parties rather than the agency.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-01 (parameterized queries against a decades-old, well-understood vulnerability class), CLOUD-01 (regular enumeration of what the public election website actually exposed), OBSV-06 (a published channel for researchers to report the flaw before activists found and used it instead).


**What happened.** In late March 2016, someone in Sprouts Farmers Market's payroll department received an email impersonating a senior company executive and, believing it genuine, emailed back the 2015 IRS Form W-2 for every current and former employee — more than 20,000 people. Several employees subsequently had fraudulent tax refunds filed in their names. A class action followed on April 20, 2016.

**Entry.** A single spoofed email addressed to payroll, impersonating a named executive and requesting W-2 data — no malware, no stolen credentials, no technical exploit, just a request that looked legitimate enough to be answered.

**Escalation.** None required: payroll held the complete W-2 file for the entire company and sent it in one reply, so the first successful deception was also the entire breach.

**Blast radius.** Full W-2 data — names, addresses, Social Security numbers, and wages — for more than 20,000 current and former employees went directly to the attacker, who used at least some of it to file fraudulent tax refund claims before employees or the IRS caught on.

**Human factor.** Payroll had no independent way to verify an executive's identity before emailing a company-wide file of Social Security numbers, and no policy required a second person to approve a bulk release of that data.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (verify the requester through a channel independent of the email itself before acting), HUMAN-02 (a second-person check before any bulk release of SSNs or tax data), HUMAN-05 (training that names executive-impersonation payroll scams specifically, already a known pattern that tax season).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.csoonline.com/article/555855/mongodb-configuration-error-exposed-93-million-mexican-voter-records.html
  - https://www.databreaches.net/personal-info-of-93-4-million-mexicans-exposed-on-amazon/
  - https://www.theregister.com/2016/04/25/mexico_voter_data_breach/
```

**What happened.** Security researcher Chris Vickery found a 132 GB MongoDB database sitting on Amazon's cloud with no authentication or encryption of any kind, containing the complete Mexican federal voter roll: names, addresses, voter ID numbers, birth dates, and parents' names for 93.4 million citizens. The database had been reachable since roughly September 2015; Vickery reported it privately and it was taken offline April 22, 2016, with public reporting following April 25.

**Entry.** Whoever stood up the database — never publicly identified — deployed MongoDB on an AWS instance with authentication switched off, so anyone who found the IP address and port could read the entire voter file directly. No credentials, no exploit, no attacker skill beyond knowing where to look.

**Escalation.** None required: the database was directly and fully readable the moment it was found.

**Blast radius.** All 93.4 million records in Mexico's national voter file were exposed for at least seven months before discovery. No confirmed malicious access or downstream fraud was ever attributed to this specific exposure — it was a researcher find, not a reported breach — but who else located the same open database in those months is unknown.

**Human factor.** Whoever copied the country's entire voter registry onto a cloud host did so without enabling the database's own authentication, and nobody monitoring that account noticed an unauthenticated, unencrypted store of tens of millions of citizens' data sitting open to the internet for months.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases not reachable from the public internet, authenticated or not), CLOUD-01 (routine enumeration of what an organization's cloud accounts actually expose), OBSV-06 (a clear channel for a researcher to report an exposure quickly — Vickery had to work to find the right recipient before it was pulled down).

```yaml
  - https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/wendys-reaches-50-million-breach-settlement-banks-a-12032
```

**What happened.** On May 11, 2016, Wendy's confirmed that malware on point-of-sale systems at franchised restaurants — first reported in January as a small, contained incident — was in fact far larger, and disclosed that attackers had used a remote-access credential belonging to the company's POS service provider to install card-stealing malware. Investigation eventually traced infections across 1,025 U.S. locations, active at various restaurants between December 2, 2015 and June 8, 2016.

**Entry.** The attacker did not touch Wendy's own code or network directly; they compromised the remote-access credentials of a third-party vendor that serviced Wendy's franchise point-of-sale systems, then used that legitimate access to push malware onto the terminals.

**Escalation.** The same compromised vendor credential worked against POS systems at more than a thousand separately owned franchise locations, since the vendor's remote-access account was not scoped per restaurant or individually attributed — one stolen secret unlocked payment systems company-wide rather than at a single location.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 18 million payment cards were exposed, with fraud losses reported by roughly 7,500 financial institutions. Wendy's reached a $50 million settlement with card-issuing banks and credit unions, announced in a February 2019 SEC filing, to resolve the resulting litigation.

**Human factor.** Wendy's extended broad, apparently unscoped and unmonitored remote access to a POS vendor, and neither Wendy's nor the vendor detected months of anomalous access before card networks flagged the fraud pattern from outside.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-08 (vendor remote access that is time-boxed and separately monitored, not standing and silent), CRED-06 (a vendor credential scoped to one job and one restaurant's systems rather than reusable across the whole franchise base), AUTH-01 (MFA on the vendor's remote-access account, since a single stolen credential appears to have been sufficient on its own).

```yaml
  - https://www.securityweek.com/vietnams-tien-phong-bank-victim-swift-based-attack/
  - https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/vietnamese-bank-blocks-1-million-online-heist-a-9105
```

**What happened.** Vietnam's Tien Phong Bank disclosed on May 15, 2016 that in the fourth quarter of 2015 it had caught and blocked an attempt to move more than €1 million (about $1.1 million) out of the bank using fraudulent SWIFT payment messages — the same interbank-messaging technique used in February 2016's $81 million theft from Bangladesh Bank. Researchers at BAE Systems said the tooling resembled the Bangladesh case closely enough to suspect a shared actor or toolkit, though that link is an inference, not a confirmed attribution.

**Entry.** The fraudulent transfer requests were sent through the infrastructure of an outside, unnamed third-party vendor that TPBank had hired to connect its systems to the SWIFT network; the bank said malware was likely installed on an application run by that vendor rather than on TPBank's own core systems.

**Escalation.** Once the vendor's connecting application was compromised, it functioned as trusted tooling able to originate authenticated SWIFT payment instructions — the attacker did not need to separately breach TPBank's own systems to attempt the transfer.

**Blast radius.** TPBank said the attempt caused no losses and had no effect on customer transactions; the fraudulent transfer was interrupted before funds moved. The larger significance was confirming that the Bangladesh Bank technique was being reused against other SWIFT-connected banks.

**Human factor.** TPBank extended its SWIFT connectivity through a third-party vendor's application without, evidently, monitoring or restricting what that application was trusted to do on the bank's behalf.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party with a path into systems that can move money), VENDOR-08 (vendor access time-boxed and separately monitored rather than a standing trusted channel), HUMAN-02 (a second, independent check on any payment instruction before it leaves the bank, regardless of which system originated it).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2016/05/18/117-million-linkedin-emails-and-passwords-from-a-2012-hack-just-got-posted-online
  - https://www.forbes.com/sites/thomasbrewster/2016/05/18/linkedin-2012-password-hack-gets-much-worse/
```

**What happened.** On May 18, 2016 a hacker using the alias "Peace" began selling what turned out to be the full scope of LinkedIn's 2012 breach — 117 million email and password combinations — on a dark web market, nearly twenty times the roughly 6.5 million accounts LinkedIn had disclosed and reset back in 2012. LinkedIn confirmed the larger figure and forced a second password reset for every affected account that had not changed its password since 2012.

**Entry.** The original 2012 intrusion is widely believed, though never officially confirmed by LinkedIn, to have used a SQL injection vulnerability against LinkedIn's site to reach the user credential database; this record's entry mechanism should be read as a credible but unconfirmed reconstruction.

**Escalation.** None required: the injection reportedly gave direct access to the stored password hashes, which is what let the theft scale to the full user database rather than a subset.

**Blast radius.** The stored password hashes were unsalted SHA-1, already considered weak in 2012; within days of the original leak, researchers had cracked the large majority of the initial sample. By 2016, the same unrotated hashes — for many accounts still unchanged after four years — were sold and rapidly weaponized, directly fueling credential-stuffing attacks against unrelated services that same quarter.

**Human factor.** LinkedIn's 2012 response reset passwords only for the subset of accounts it then believed were affected, leaving well over 100 million other account hashes valid and crackable for four more years until the true scope surfaced.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-06 (a memory-hard password hashing algorithm instead of unsalted SHA-1, which would have made the 2012 hashes far harder to crack even after theft), CRED-07 (credentials that expire rather than remaining valid indefinitely), AUTH-06 (breached-password checks that would flag reused LinkedIn credentials at other services before they enabled account takeover elsewhere).

**What happened.** Kansas Heart Hospital in Wichita was hit by ransomware on May 18, 2016 after an employee, in the middle of converting to a new backup system, opened a phishing email. The hospital paid an initial, undisclosed ransom expecting full decryption; the attackers unlocked only part of the data and then demanded a second payment. Hospital president Greg Duick declined to pay again, calling it unwise, and the hospital restored the rest from backups instead.

**Entry.** A phishing email opened by hospital staff, arriving while attention was already on an in-progress backup-system migration.

**Escalation.** None required beyond execution: the ransomware encrypted reachable systems directly from the infected endpoint.

**Human factor.** A staff member acted on a phishing email during a period of unusual IT activity when vigilance was already stretched, and the hospital's existing backups were not complete or current enough to make paying any ransom unnecessary.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training current enough to catch the lure, especially during known high-risk periods like system migrations), DATA-08 (backups that are restore-tested and current enough that a ransom payment is never the only path back), OBSV-05 (the written incident plan the hospital did have, which is why the second extortion attempt was refused rather than paid out of panic).

**What happened.** On June 19–20, 2016, Citrix disclosed what it called a "very sophisticated password attack" against its GoToMyPC remote-desktop service and forced a password reset for every user. Citrix attributed the attack to credential stuffing: usernames and passwords leaked in unrelated earlier breaches — including the LinkedIn dump resurfacing that same quarter — were tried against GoToMyPC accounts, and where a customer had reused a password, the login succeeded.

**Entry.** Valid GoToMyPC usernames and passwords obtained from outside breach dumps, tried directly against GoToMyPC's login, with no exploit of GoToMyPC's own code required.

**Escalation.** None required: a successful login with a reused password granted full remote-desktop access to that user's computer immediately.

**Blast radius.** Citrix did not disclose how many accounts were successfully accessed, only that it reset all customer passwords as a precaution and pushed users toward unique, stronger credentials.

**Human factor.** GoToMyPC customers reused passwords across services, and the login process itself had no rate limiting, breached-password check, or mandatory second factor to blunt automated credential stuffing once those passwords were exposed elsewhere.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (mandatory MFA on a tool that grants full remote control of a computer), AUTH-06 (rate limiting, lockout, and breached-password screening on the login itself, independent of what customers choose as passwords).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Wordfence's public dissection of the Panama Papers breach (April 2016) named the specific unpatched software — a Revolution Slider plugin and a nearly two-year-old Drupal install still open to 2014's "Drupalgeddon" SQL injection — and touched off a wave of "check your CMS patch level" advisories across the security press.
- LinkedIn (May 2016) and Citrix's GoToMyPC (June 2016) both forced blanket password resets after old breach dumps resurfaced and were weaponized via credential stuffing, and both pushed users toward unique passwords; the GoToMyPC episode became a widely cited case for why password reuse, not just the original breach, drives account takeovers years later.
- After Tien Phong Bank's disclosure, SWIFT wrote to member banks describing the PDF-report-tampering malware technique used against both Bangladesh Bank and TPBank, pressing banks to scrutinize the local software and third-party vendors connecting their back office to SWIFT rather than treating the network itself as the only thing to secure.
- Security researcher Chris Vickery's handling of the exposed Mexican voter database — privately notifying Amazon and Mexican electoral authorities and getting it taken down before public disclosure — was widely cited as a model for responsible disclosure of a found, unexploited exposure.
- The Sprouts Farmers Market W-2 case was one of several payroll-phishing incidents that spring to draw class-action suits under state data-breach statutes, adding civil-liability pressure — on top of existing IRS and FBI warnings about executive-impersonation W-2 scams — for companies to require out-of-band verification before releasing bulk employee tax data.
- Kansas Heart Hospital's and the University of Calgary's ransomware episodes, both publicized in late spring 2016, became early, concrete cautionary examples that paying a ransom does not guarantee full recovery or stop attackers from asking for more.
