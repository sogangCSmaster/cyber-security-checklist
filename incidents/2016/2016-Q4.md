# 2016 Q4 — IoT Botnets, Bank Fraud, Billion-Account Breach

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/default-credentials — Mirai's IoT botnet was the quarter's single biggest event, but weak or absent authentication (factor/weak-auth) recurred across bank cards, a lottery login, and an internal carrier tool too.
> - **New technique this quarter:** IoT devices, not servers or PCs, weaponized at internet-infrastructure scale for the first time — Mirai's botnet of hijacked cameras and routers took down a major DNS provider and, with it, large parts of the visible internet.
> - **Recurring failure:** factor/weak-auth — default, reused, stuffed, or simply over-privileged credentials did more damage this quarter than any novel software exploit.
> - **Records in this file:** 17


**What happened.** In October 2016, security researcher Chris Vickery found that a Sheet Metal Workers union local covering Northern California had left a backup of its membership database openly readable on the internet. He reported it to the union directly by phone, and the exposure was closed within a day or two.

**Entry.** The union — or a contractor acting on its behalf — ran an rsync file-sharing service with no password or other authentication configured, so anyone who connected to it could list and download its contents directly, no exploit required.

**Escalation.** None required: once connected to the open share, the entire backup was readable in one step.

**Blast radius.** The backup covered more than 24,900 current and former union members: names, addresses, ethnicities, Social Security numbers, and pension/benefit-plan beneficiary details. Vickery found no evidence anyone had accessed the data before he reported it, and the union secured the server shortly after his call. No financial cost was disclosed.

**Human factor.** Whoever set up the union's backup routine pointed it at an authentication-free rsync share rather than a private one — the same class of mistake behind this quarter's other exposed-backup case, an error invisible until an outside scan happens to find it.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and backup services should not be reachable from the public internet at all), CLOUD-01 (a routine external-exposure inventory would have caught the open rsync port), DATA-09 (retaining more than a decade of members' Social Security numbers in a live, reachable backup went well past what any single restore would need).

```yaml
  - https://scroll.in/latest/829004/debit-card-breach-malware-compromised-our-systems-says-hitachi-payment-services
```

**What happened.** In October 2016, India's largest banks — including State Bank of India, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Yes Bank, and Axis Bank — began blocking and reissuing debit cards en masse after discovering that malware planted on Hitachi Payment Services' payment-switch network, which routes ATM and point-of-sale transactions for many Indian banks, had silently captured card data months earlier. An independent forensic audit by SISA Information Security traced the malware's active window to roughly May 21–July 11, 2016.

**Entry.** No bank's own systems were directly breached. Malware was injected into the network of Hitachi Payment Services, the shared third-party vendor whose switch processes transactions on behalf of many Indian banks at once — a single vendor compromise that touched every bank routing traffic through it.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial foothold: sitting on the shared switch, the malware could directly observe card data from every transaction passing through it, across all client banks simultaneously.

**Blast radius.** An estimated 3.2 million debit cards were compromised (roughly 2.6 million Visa/Mastercard, 600,000 RuPay); State Bank of India alone blocked and reissued over 600,000 cards. The breach was only caught after banks noticed fraudulent withdrawals in China and the United States on cards whose holders were in India at the time. Nineteen banks logged 641 customer fraud complaints totaling about ₹1.3 crore (roughly $190,000) by the time of first disclosure — an early, partial figure, not a final total, since it predates the full card-reissuance response.

**Human factor.** Banks extended trust to a shared payment-processing vendor's security without evident independent verification, and the malware's months-long undetected run reflects a monitoring gap in transaction infrastructure that sits outside any single bank's own network.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of, and security requirements for, every third party — including shared payment switches — that touches card data), VENDOR-08 (vendor-side access and activity monitored independently rather than trusted by default), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal transaction geography and volume, which is in fact how the fraud was ultimately noticed).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2016/10/14/heightened-ddos-threat-posed-mirai-and-other-botnets
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Dyn_cyberattack
```

**What happened.** On October 21, 2016, three waves of DDoS traffic hit Dyn, a managed DNS provider that resolved domain names for a large share of the internet's most-visited sites. Dyn's service became intermittently unreachable for most of the day, which made Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, Spotify, PayPal, Amazon, SoundCloud, and dozens of other major platforms unreachable for large parts of the US and Europe.

**Entry.** The traffic came primarily from Mirai, a botnet built by continuously scanning the internet for IoT devices — mostly home routers, DVRs, and IP cameras — reachable on Telnet, then logging in with a hardcoded list of around 60 factory-default username/password pairs (admin/admin, root/12345, and similar) that owners had never changed and, on many models, had no way to change.

**Escalation.** None required in the usual sense: each compromised device joined the botnet directly and scanned for further vulnerable devices itself, so the attack's capacity grew without further action by whoever was directing it.

**Blast radius.** Dyn described the attack traffic as involving "tens of millions" of distinct IP addresses; independent researchers put the actively-attacking Mirai botnet at roughly 100,000 devices — the two figures are not directly comparable and neither was independently audited, so treat both as estimates. No data was stolen; the impact was hours of intermittent unavailability across dozens of major platforms, not a records breach. Direct financial cost to Dyn or to the affected platforms was never disclosed.

**Human factor.** Millions of individual device owners — and the manufacturers who shipped the devices with unchangeable default logins in the first place — never changed a password that was never meant to stay default, a decision repeated by so many people it became an infrastructure-scale weapon.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-10 (IoT/embedded devices must force a unique credential on first boot and disable hardcoded defaults), AUTH-06 (rate limiting and lockout on the exposed Telnet/admin service would have slowed Mirai's scanning), OBSV-02 (Dyn and upstream ISPs alerting on abnormal traffic volume sooner).


**What happened.** On September 5, 2016, an employee of Precedent Communications, a digital agency contracted to run the Australian Red Cross Blood Service's donor website, created a backup of the site's testing database and saved it to a publicly reachable folder on the agency's own server instead of a secure location. The 1.74GB file sat there until an anonymous individual scanning the internet for exposed servers found it on October 25 and passed it to security researcher Troy Hunt, who alerted the Blood Service the same day.

**Entry.** This was not an intrusion in the usual sense: a contractor's staff member misplaced a live copy of donor data onto an open part of a web server, where a routine scan for directory listings and `.sql` backup files found it. Australia's privacy regulator (OAIC) later confirmed the immediate cause was human error by the Precedent employee, occurring outside that employee's assigned duties.

**Escalation.** None required — the file, once found, was directly downloadable in full; no credentials or further access were needed.

**Blast radius.** The file held roughly 1.28 million donor records spanning 2010–2016, covering about 550,000 individual donors: names, addresses, dates of birth, contact details, and answers to blood-donation eligibility questions, some of a sensitive nature (sexual behavior, pregnancy status). It was reachable online for about seven weeks before discovery. The OAIC found no evidence of misuse beyond the finder who reported it, and Hunt called it, by record count, Australia's largest reported data breach to that point.

**Human factor.** Beyond the contractor's one-off mistake, the OAIC found the Blood Service itself had not required Precedent to meet adequate security standards for the data it held, and had let donor records — including from people who had not donated in years — accumulate in a test environment long past when they were needed.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage and web-server file shares private by default, with legacy content periodically audited), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of, and enforced security requirements for, every third party holding donor data), DATA-09 (retention limits so years-old test copies of live donor data do not linger indefinitely).

```yaml
factor: [factor/weak-auth, factor/process-failure]
scale: 20000 accounts
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: 0
confidence: high
controls: [INPUT-09, AUTH-06, OBSV-05]
sources:
  - https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-tesco-bank-failures-2016-cyber-attack
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/tesco-bank-fined-16-million-by-fca-for-2016-cyber-attack/
```

**What happened.** Over a weekend in November 2016, criminals moved money out of roughly 20,000 Tesco Bank current accounts in a 48-hour spree, taking £2.26 million in total. Tesco Bank froze all online debit transactions for several days while it investigated — the first time a UK bank had to do so because of a live, in-progress fraud attack.

**Entry.** Tesco Bank had issued debit cards with sequential, predictable card numbers. Attackers used an algorithm to generate strings of valid-format numbers within Tesco Bank's ranges, then ran them through as contactless "Mag-Stripe Data" (MSD) transactions — a fallback contactless mode that, unlike chip-and-PIN or standard contactless, needs no PIN, signature, or dynamic CVV, and carries no built-in limit on transaction count or value. Tesco Bank had not disabled this mode even though its cards were never meant to be used this way.

**Escalation.** None required — once the attackers could generate a valid card number, each one could be used directly for a fraudulent MSD transaction; no further system compromise or privilege was needed.

**Blast radius.** £2.26 million moved out of customer accounts in the 48-hour window before Tesco Bank froze online debit transactions. The UK Financial Conduct Authority found Tesco Bank's financial-crime controls, fraud-detection rules, and its Financial Crime Operations Team's response were all deficient, and fined the bank £16.4 million in October 2018 — reduced from a provisional £33.5 million for prompt customer redress and an early settlement.

**Human factor.** Tesco Bank's own card-design and risk teams chose a predictable numbering scheme and left a weakly-authenticated contactless mode enabled, decisions made long before the attack and never revisited until they were exploited at scale.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-09 (card and account identifiers should not be sequentially guessable), AUTH-06 (rate limiting and anomaly-based lockout on card-present transaction volume), OBSV-05 (a faster, rehearsed incident response — the FCA specifically cited a slow, under-resourced reaction).

**What happened.** Casino Rama Resort, a single-site resort casino in Ontario, became aware on November 4, 2016 that an intruder had stolen years of customer, employee, and vendor records and was demanding payment not to release them. When the casino did not meet the demand, the attacker began publishing files publicly on November 11, and threatened to release far more.

**Human factor.** A successful phishing attempt against casino staff opened the door to years of accumulated records — customer, employee, and vendor data going back more than a decade — that had no apparent reason to all still be reachable from the same compromised foothold.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing-specific training and simulation for staff with access to sensitive systems), DATA-09 (retention limits so a decade of HR and customer records is not all live and reachable at once), OBSV-05 (a written incident and extortion-response plan with a named decision-maker, given the live ransom deadline the casino faced).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2016/11/13/friendfinder-hack-412-million-accounts-breached
```

**What happened.** In mid-November 2016, breach-indexing site LeakedSource reported obtaining more than 412 million account records from FriendFinder Networks' family of sites — AdultFriendFinder, Cams.com, Penthouse.com, and others — making it, by record count, the largest breach reported that year.

**Entry.** Multiple outlets that reviewed the leaked data attributed the breach to a local file inclusion (LFI) flaw in FriendFinder Networks' web applications. An LFI flaw lets an attacker make the application load and execute a file of the attacker's choosing rather than the one intended; here it was reportedly leveraged into broader command execution and direct database access.

**Escalation.** The same flaw appears to have given the attacker reach across multiple, nominally separate FriendFinder Networks brands and their databases rather than staying contained to one site — indicating the properties shared infrastructure without meaningful separation.

**Blast radius.** LeakedSource's count — 412,214,295 accounts, an indexer's figure that was never independently audited — included more than 339 million from AdultFriendFinder alone and, notably, email addresses and passwords for over 15 million accounts users had already deleted, which FriendFinder Networks had continued to retain. Most passwords were hashed with the long-obsolete SHA-1 algorithm, with some reportedly stored in plain text.

**Human factor.** FriendFinder Networks kept deleted users' credentials rather than purging them, and protected live users' passwords with a hashing scheme already considered weak years before this breach.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-06 (no inclusion or execution of attacker-influenced file paths), DATA-06 (modern, memory-hard password hashing), DATA-09 (deleting account data — including "deleted" accounts — on an enforced retention schedule).


**What happened.** Security firm Kryptowire found that update-agent firmware pre-installed on inexpensive BLU Products Android phones sold via Amazon and Best Buy was copying the full contents of users' text messages, call logs, contacts, and location data, encrypting it, and sending it to servers in Shanghai every 72 hours — with no disclosure to the user, and by BLU's own account, without BLU's knowledge of the behavior.

**Entry.** No attacker broke in. The data collection was built into firmware supplied by Shanghai Adups Technology, which BLU Products — and, by Adups' own claim, many other device makers — incorporated as a routine software-update mechanism. The exposure was in a trusted upstream component, not in BLU's own code.

**Escalation.** None required — the firmware ran with full system privileges on the device by design from the moment it left the factory, so no further access needed to be gained once a customer owned an affected phone.

**Blast radius.** Kryptowire's researchers found the behavior after buying a single BLU R1 HD phone for an unrelated trip and reverse-engineering it; the exact number of affected devices was never disclosed. Adups said its software ran on more than 700 million active devices worldwide, but did not say how many had this specific collection feature enabled. BLU said it removed the capability via a firmware update after being notified.

**Human factor.** BLU Products — and, by Adups' own account, many other device makers — incorporated a third party's firmware update agent without auditing what it actually did once installed on customers' phones.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of, and review of, exactly what a supplied firmware or software component does before it ships to customers), DEPS-09 (vendor trustworthiness and track record as a selection criterion), VENDOR-09 (contractual and technical verification that a bundled update agent does not exfiltrate user content).


**What happened.** Fraudsters obtained a valid, authorised login to Three UK's customer upgrade-ordering system and used it to look up customer records and trigger bogus handset upgrades, aiming to intercept new premium phones before they reached the genuine customer. Three confirmed 133,827 customer accounts were actually accessed, after early press reports — based on what the compromised login could theoretically reach — warned that up to two-thirds of Three's customer base might be at risk.

**Entry.** Attackers used a single compromised employee or authorised login to Three's upgrade system. Neither Three nor UK press ever publicly disclosed how that credential itself was obtained — whether phished, purchased, or taken from a compromised device — only that it was a legitimate, working login.

**Escalation.** None beyond the initial credential was needed: that one login could query and action upgrades across Three's full customer base, far more reach than a single account-management task should have required.

Around 400 handsets were separately stolen via related store burglaries, and at least 8 were fraudulently obtained through the compromised upgrade process. The UK National Crime Agency arrested three men in connection with the case in November 2016.

**Human factor.** A single login credential carried enough privilege to reach Three's entire customer upgrade database, with no additional check on the unusually high volume of lookups and upgrade orders that followed its misuse.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-07 (admin/impersonation-grade tooling like the upgrade system needs its own separate authentication and full audit trail), AUTH-08 (access reviewed and scoped to what each login actually needs), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job and one resource rather than the whole customer base).

Muni's fare gates and ticket machines went down, and the agency let riders travel free rather than try to collect fares manually.

**Human factor.** The initially compromised server ran software with a vulnerability that had a patch available for roughly a year; nobody had applied it, and the internal network was flat enough that one infected server's harvested credentials could reach thousands of other machines.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (know what is internet-facing and patch it — SFMTA was found via a routine internet-wide scan, not a targeted campaign), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between the server that was hit and the thousands of desktops it reached), OBSV-01 (centrally logged admin/PsExec-style activity would have surfaced the lateral spread before it reached 2,112 machines).

```yaml
  - https://thehackernews.com/2016/11/mirai-router-offline.html
```

**What happened.** On November 27, 2016, more than 900,000 Deutsche Telekom broadband customers across Germany lost internet connectivity within hours of each other, as a new Mirai botnet variant scanned the internet trying to hijack home routers. This came five weeks after the same Mirai malware family took down the DNS provider Dyn, showing its operators moving toward directly recruiting more devices.

**Entry.** The variant targeted TR-069 (CWMP), a protocol some ISPs use to remotely manage customer routers over port 7547. Deutsche Telekom's affected Speedport models (made by Taiwan's Arcadyan) were not actually vulnerable to the specific exploit, but the flood of exploitation attempts aimed at that exposed management port overwhelmed the routers regardless — researchers described the outage as collateral damage rather than successful infection.

**Escalation.** Where the exploit did succeed against genuinely vulnerable devices elsewhere, it was self-propagating in the Mirai pattern: each newly hijacked router began scanning for and attacking further vulnerable routers with no further action from whoever launched it. Researchers estimated roughly five million internet-connected devices worldwide shared the same exposed, exploitable TR-069 configuration.

**Blast radius.** Around 900,000 Deutsche Telekom customers — about 4% of its German fixed-line base — lost internet access, some for a day or more, until Deutsche Telekom pushed emergency firmware updates and filtered the exploit traffic. No customer data was stolen; the impact was denial of connectivity, not a records breach. No direct financial cost was disclosed.

**Human factor.** Routers across many ISPs, not just Deutsche Telekom's, shipped with a remote-management port reachable from the open internet and no rate limiting on connection attempts — a design and deployment choice made by device makers and ISPs long before this specific botnet variant went looking for it.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (know and minimize what is reachable from the internet — the WAN-side management port had no business being open to arbitrary scanners), CRED-10 (embedded/IoT devices should not accept unauthenticated remote-management commands by default), OBSV-02 (ISP-side alerting on abnormal connection-attempt volume against customer-premises equipment).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA/US-CERT published Alert TA16-288A on October 14, 2016 — a week before the Dyn attack — describing Mirai's default-credential scanning and recommending IoT owners change default passwords, disable UPnP, and watch for its known scanning ports; it became the standard reference advisory once the Dyn attack made the threat concrete.
- Several device makers named as sources of Mirai-infected hardware, including Hangzhou Xiongmai, responded to the Dyn attack by recalling or patching affected webcams and DVRs sold in the US.
- DHS, ODNI, and FBI's joint statements and the December 29 GRIZZLY STEPPE report set a new precedent: formal US government technical attribution of a cyber campaign to a nation-state actor, published alongside indicators of compromise for network defenders.
- Yahoo's back-to-back 2016 disclosures (500 million accounts in September, a further one billion in December) put acquirer due-diligence for undisclosed breaches on the map, contributing to a $350 million reduction in Verizon's purchase price for Yahoo.
- Camelot's credential-stuffing incident, following a similar one at Deliveroo weeks earlier, added momentum in the UK toward screening new passwords against known-breached-password lists rather than relying on complexity rules alone.
- Kryptowire's Adups/BLU Products disclosure triggered an FTC investigation into undisclosed data collection on consumer devices, foreshadowing the 2017–2018 settlement that required BLU to run an ongoing, independently-audited security program.
- The FCA's investigation into Tesco Bank, opened after this quarter's attack, became the basis for the UK's first major regulatory fine specifically for cyber-attack-related control failures at a bank, sharpening scrutiny of contactless transaction authentication industry-wide.
