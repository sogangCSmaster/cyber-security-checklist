# 2022 Q4 — One Stolen Credential, Total Access

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — malware on a personal or under-managed device (a contractor's synced browser at Medibank, an unofficial download at Continental, a DevOps engineer's unpatched home media server at LastPass) handed over one valid credential, and that one credential was enough on its own
> - **New technique this quarter:** OWASSRF — Rackspace's attackers chained two already-patched Exchange CVEs (CVE-2022-41080 + CVE-2022-41082) specifically to bypass Microsoft's own interim URL-rewrite mitigation for ProxyNotShell, a bypass CrowdStrike documented for the first time this quarter
> - **Recurring failure:** once a single credential was obtained, nothing else stood in the way — MFA was missing outright (Medibank), never specified (MyDeal), or present but defeated by a keylogger (LastPass), and the access that credential carried was scoped far past what the job needed
> - **Records in this file:** 11

  - https://blog.gitguardian.com/toyota-accidently-exposed-a-secret-key-publicly-on-github-for-five-years/
```

**What happened.** In December 2017, a development subcontractor building Toyota's T-Connect connected-car app uploaded a portion of its source code to a GitHub repository set to public. The code contained a hardcoded access key for the backend server holding T-Connect customer records. The repository stayed public for almost five years until Toyota's own discovery on September 15, 2022; Toyota made it private, rotated the key on September 17, and apologized publicly on October 7, 2022.

**Entry.** A subcontractor's developer committed source code containing a live server access key to a repository that was never restricted to private, leaving valid credentials to Toyota's customer-data server openly readable by anyone on the internet for years.

**Escalation.** None required — the leaked key alone was sufficient to query the data server directly; no further privilege escalation was needed by anyone who found it.

**Blast radius.** The key could reach records tied to 296,019 T-Connect customers who registered between July 2017 and September 2022, limited to email addresses and internal customer management numbers; Toyota said names, phone numbers, and payment data were not stored in the exposed database. Toyota's own server logs could not confirm whether the key had actually been used by an outside party during the nearly five-year window, and could not rule it out either.

**Human factor.** A subcontractor's individual commit went unreviewed for secrets, and neither the subcontractor nor Toyota had a process to periodically scan public repositories for Toyota's own leaked credentials — the same gap persisted, undetected, for nearly five years.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no live secret in a repository, public or private), CRED-05 (secret-pattern scanning before the first commit, which would have caught this in December 2017), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of what subcontractors publish and access under a company's own product name, so this kind of exposure is found in days, not years).

```

**What happened.** Medibank, Australia's largest private health insurer, disclosed on October 13, 2022 that it was investigating a cyber incident, and within a week confirmed that a criminal actor had stolen data and was demanding a ransom. The eventual scope covered current and former customers of Medibank, ahm, and its international student policies — about 9.7 million people — including, for roughly half of them, health claims data revealing specific diagnoses and procedures. Medibank refused to pay, and the attacker published the stolen data, including claims data, on the dark web in batches through November and December 2022.

**Entry.** According to the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner's (OAIC) civil penalty case, an employee of a Medibank IT-helpdesk contractor had signed into a personal browser profile on his work computer; that browser synced his saved Medibank login to his personal computer, which was separately infected with credential-stealing malware around early August 2022. The malware harvested the synced credentials, and on August 12, 2022 the attacker used them to log into Medibank's Microsoft Exchange server and then its Palo Alto Networks GlobalProtect VPN.

**Escalation.** The stolen account carried administrative-level access reaching, in the OAIC's words, "most if not all" of Medibank's systems — network drives, management consoles, and remote-desktop access to jump-box servers used to reach internal databases — far beyond what a helpdesk contractor's login should have granted. The VPN itself did not require multi-factor authentication, so the stolen username and password were sufficient on their own.

**Blast radius.** The OAIC alleges the intrusion ran roughly nine weeks before Medibank's security team identified unusual activity around October 12, 2022. The attacker exfiltrated data on approximately 9.7 million current and former customers, including names, dates of birth, Medicare and passport numbers, and, for about half of those affected, health claims data. The OAIC filed Federal Court civil penalty proceedings in June 2024 seeking penalties of up to AU$2.22 million per contravention of the Privacy Act.

**Human factor.** A contractor mixing a personal browser profile with a work login, combined with Medibank's own decision not to require MFA on a VPN that reached administrative systems, meant a single home-computer malware infection was sufficient to open Medibank's entire network — the OAIC's case treats this as a foreseeable, avoidable gap rather than a sophisticated attack.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the VPN would have made a bare stolen credential insufficient), CRED-06 (a helpdesk contractor's credential scoped to helpdesk tasks rather than admin reach across the whole estate), HUMAN-04 (contractors held to the same access-control standard as employees, including restrictions on mixing personal and work browser profiles on a work device).

**What happened.** MyDeal, an online marketplace majority-owned by Australian retail group Woolworths, notified customers on October 14, 2022 that an unauthorized party had used a compromised login credential to access its customer relationship management (CRM) system. The intrusion became public in the same week as Medibank's, part of a cluster of Australian breaches that quarter, and data on roughly 2.2 million customers was later advertised for sale on a hacking forum.

**Entry.** MyDeal's notification said only that "a compromised user credential" was used to log into its CRM; it did not disclose whether the credential was phished, reused from an unrelated breach, or stolen by malware, so this record tags the entry mechanism as unknown rather than guessing at it.

**Human factor.** A single login, with no stated additional control such as MFA, IP restriction, or export-volume alerting, was sufficient to reach 2.2 million customer records in a marketing CRM; MyDeal's public notice describes no compensating control that slowed or flagged the export.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (MFA on CRM logins so a credential alone is insufficient), AUTH-06 (rate limiting and anomaly detection on authentication attempts), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read or export volume, which would have flagged an export covering millions of records).

```

**What happened.** Security research firm SOCRadar privately reported a misconfigured, publicly reachable Microsoft Azure Blob Storage endpoint to Microsoft on September 24, 2022. The endpoint held business-transaction files — records of interactions between Microsoft or its partners and prospective enterprise customers. Microsoft published an investigation update on October 19, 2022 confirming the misconfiguration and its fix, while disputing SOCRadar's characterization of the exposure's scope as, in Microsoft's words, exaggerated.

**Entry.** No attacker action was needed to reach the data — the storage endpoint accepted unauthenticated requests, so anyone who found the URL structure could read files inside it. SOCRadar built a public lookup tool, which it called "BlueBleed," to let organizations check whether their own data was in the exposed set.

**Escalation.** None required; the misconfigured endpoint was directly, publicly readable with no further access needed.

**Blast radius.** SOCRadar reported files touching roughly 65,000 entities across 111 countries, including names, email addresses, phone numbers, company names, and in some cases attached project files and email content tied to sales and partner discussions. Microsoft said its own analysis found extensive duplication — the same emails, projects, and users referenced repeatedly — and that the true number of distinct affected entities was materially smaller than SOCRadar's count; neither party published an agreed final figure. No confirmed malicious access or exploitation was reported by either side.

**Human factor.** A storage endpoint used for business-to-business support and sales data was left reachable without authentication, and it took an outside research firm, not Microsoft's own monitoring, to find and responsibly report it.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (regularly enumerate what is publicly reachable so an unauthenticated storage endpoint does not go unnoticed for weeks), CLOUD-03 (least-privilege configuration so a support/sales data store defaults to private), OBSV-06 (a clear vulnerability-reporting channel — which worked as intended here, since SOCRadar's report reached Microsoft and the endpoint was fixed).

```

**What happened.** In early October 2022, Dropbox employees received phishing emails impersonating CircleCI, a CI/CD tool Dropbox uses, directing them to a fake CircleCI login page. GitHub flagged suspicious activity on a Dropbox-linked account on October 14 and notified Dropbox, which confirmed an attacker had used one employee's stolen GitHub credentials to access roughly 130 internal code repositories. Dropbox disclosed the incident publicly on November 1, 2022.

**Entry.** The phishing page collected the victim's GitHub username and password, then prompted for the one-time code from their hardware authentication key and relayed it to the real GitHub login in real time before it expired — letting the attacker's session ride in behind a legitimate MFA approval rather than needing to defeat MFA outright.

**Escalation.** No further technical escalation was required: the compromised employee's GitHub account already had read access to about 130 repositories, including Dropbox's modified copies of third-party libraries, internal prototypes, and some of the security team's own tools and configuration files — more reach than a single phished developer session should have carried.

**Blast radius.** The attacker accessed source code, some internal prototypes, and developer API keys, plus, by Dropbox's own account, "a few thousand" names and email addresses belonging to employees, past and current customers, sales leads, and vendors. Dropbox said its core applications and infrastructure sit behind separate, more tightly controlled access and were not reached, and that no customer account contents, passwords, or payment data were exposed.

**Human factor.** The phishing kit specifically impersonated a real, current internal tool with a convincing lookalike login page and a code-relay step timed to beat a one-time code's expiry; generic phishing-awareness training aimed at obvious lures would not necessarily have flagged a page mimicking software the employee used every day.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant, origin-bound MFA such as WebAuthn/FIDO2 cannot be relayed the way a one-time code can), CRED-01 (no live API keys or secrets sitting in repositories a single developer account could reach), HUMAN-05 (phishing training and simulations that specifically cover lookalike logins for tools the team actually uses).

**What happened.** On November 23, 2022, India's All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) in New Delhi — the country's premier public hospital and medical research institute — found its e-Hospital servers down in what its IT team identified as a suspected ransomware attack. Outpatient registration, billing, lab reporting, and appointment systems fell back to manual, paper-based processes for roughly two weeks while multiple government agencies worked to restore service. Public reporting and parliamentary statements describe the outage, the emergency response, and the ransom demand, but not how the attacker first reached AIIMS's network, so this record tags entry as unknown rather than adopting any of the unconfirmed theories that circulated in Indian press.

Unverified Indian press estimates put the number of exposed patient records in the tens of millions, but neither AIIMS nor the government confirmed a specific headcount, so this record treats it as undisclosed rather than adopting a disputed press figure.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and admin actions across hospital servers, to catch lateral movement into a second and third server sooner), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between hospital-management and clinical systems so a foothold in one cannot reach the others), DATA-08 (restore-tested, isolated backups — which AIIMS did have, and which is the reason it recovered without paying).

**What happened.** A Twitter API flaw let anyone submit an email address or phone number and learn which account it belonged to, without needing to already know the account. A researcher reported it through Twitter's bug bounty program in January 2022 and Twitter patched it, but by then the flaw had already been used to build large scraped datasets pairing account identities with non-public contact details. A 5.4-million-account dataset that had circulated privately since mid-2022 was published for free on a hacking forum on November 24, 2022. On December 23, 2022, a seller using the handle "Ryushi" separately advertised a much larger dataset — claimed at over 400 million accounts — for sale, and in January 2023 another dataset covering roughly 200 million accounts was posted for free.

**Escalation.** None required — the endpoint returned matching account data directly to any caller who queried it, with no further access or privilege needed.

The 400-million and 200-million-account figures that circulated in December 2022 and January 2023 were sellers' own marketplace claims, not confirmed by Twitter: the company's January 2023 investigation found no evidence the later postings came from a new exploit of its systems, and outside researchers noted the larger sets were duplicate-laden repackagings of the same underlying scrape. This record's scale reflects Twitter's confirmed figure; the larger claims are attributed to the sellers and carry low confidence on their own.

**Human factor.** No single decision stands out — the flaw was a design gap in an account-lookup feature that was reported responsibly once and patched, but not accompanied by a proactive search for evidence the same flaw had already been abused before the fix, which is why data kept resurfacing more than a year after patching.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint should return user data, the root cause here), DATA-10 (a feature mapping one piece of contact information to an account is exactly the kind of fan-out feature that needs rate limiting and abuse monitoring), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal per-caller query volume would have flagged mass enumeration long before outside researchers did).

**What happened.** On December 2, 2022, Rackspace Technology's Hosted Exchange email platform went down; the company confirmed on December 6 that a ransomware incident, isolated to that one product line, was the cause. Rackspace later confirmed the Play ransomware gang was responsible and, rather than rebuild the platform, permanently discontinued Hosted Exchange, migrating its roughly 30,000 remaining customers to Microsoft 365.

**Entry.** CrowdStrike's subsequent analysis, cited by Rackspace, found the attackers used a technique it named OWASSRF to bypass the URL-rewrite mitigation Microsoft had published for the September 2022 "ProxyNotShell" Exchange vulnerabilities. The bypass chained CVE-2022-41080 (an Exchange privilege-escalation flaw) with CVE-2022-41082 (remote code execution). Microsoft had shipped an actual patch for both in its November 8, 2022 cumulative update — weeks before Rackspace's environment was compromised — but servers that had applied only the earlier interim URL-rewrite workaround, rather than the full patch, remained exposed to this bypass.

**Escalation.** CVE-2022-41080 is itself a privilege-escalation vulnerability; chaining it with the RCE flaw let the attacker go from a network-reachable Exchange server directly to elevated code execution without a separate credential-theft step.

**Blast radius.** Email service was disrupted for an extended period for Rackspace's roughly 30,000 Hosted Exchange customers, predominantly small and mid-sized businesses that relied on Rackspace to run their corporate email. Rackspace ultimately discontinued the product line entirely. The company said the incident was contained to Hosted Exchange and did not affect its other product lines; it did not disclose a specific direct cost, though Hosted Exchange had generated roughly $30 million in annual revenue before the shutdown.

**Human factor.** The gap between Microsoft's actual patch (November 8) and Rackspace's compromise (detected December 2) points to a patch-management lag — treating a vendor's interim mitigation as equivalent to its real fix — on infrastructure thousands of other businesses depended on for email continuity.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-NEW (a patch-and-mitigation SLA requiring a vendor's actual fix, not just an interim workaround, to be applied within a defined window on internet-facing mail infrastructure — not yet a named control in this checklist), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of admin actions on Exchange servers, to catch a privilege-escalation exploit faster), DATA-08 (restore-tested backups and a rehearsed migration path, which is ultimately what let Rackspace move customers off the platform).

**What happened.** German automotive supplier Continental AG was compromised starting July 1, 2022; Continental detected the intrusion on August 4 and said it stopped further attacker activity by August 5, but the LockBit ransomware group had already exfiltrated data and, after failing to encrypt Continental's systems, shifted to pure data-theft extortion — publishing sample screenshots and threatening a full leak. On December 12, 2022, Continental publicly confirmed for the first time that "despite established security measures, the attackers were also able to steal a subset of data from affected IT systems," after roughly a month of ransom negotiations (reportedly opening at $50 million and later reduced to $40 million) that had themselves leaked publicly.

**Entry.** According to reporting on internal accounts of the incident, an employee downloaded and ran an unofficial, unapproved browser application from the general internet rather than a sanctioned software source; the download delivered credential-stealing malware that captured that employee's Continental account password.

**Escalation.** With one account's credentials in hand, the attacker obtained login data for additional, more privileged accounts over the following weeks, expanding from a single compromised employee login to broader network access before Continental detected and contained the intrusion in early August.

**Blast radius.** Continental said no systems were ultimately encrypted — LockBit's ransomware payload never successfully executed — but the group did exfiltrate an unconfirmed volume of internal data. LockBit itself claimed roughly 40 terabytes during negotiations, a figure Continental never independently confirmed, so this record treats the volume as undisclosed rather than adopting the attacker's own claim. LockBit added Continental to its leak site and threatened wider publication.

**Human factor.** A single employee downloading and running an unapproved application from outside any sanctioned software channel was, by the available account, the opening move in a chain that reached privileged credentials and a month of extortion negotiations at a multibillion-euro company.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the account, so a stolen password alone would not have reached further systems), HUMAN-05 (training and technical controls — such as application allow-listing — against downloading and executing unapproved software), CRED-06 (credentials scoped narrowly enough that one compromised employee account could not itself become a path to more privileged ones).

**What happened.** LastPass disclosed on December 22, 2022 that a threat actor had used information stolen in an August 2022 breach of its development environment to access a third-party cloud storage service and copy customer vault backups. A further LastPass update on March 1, 2023 detailed how that access was obtained: the attacker used source code and technical information from the August incident to specifically target one of a small number of DevOps engineers who held credentials able to decrypt the cloud storage.

**Entry.** The attacker compromised that senior DevOps engineer's home computer by exploiting a vulnerability in an unpatched third-party media-streaming software package the engineer was running. That foothold let the attacker run a keylogger on the engineer's personal machine, which captured his LastPass master password as he typed it — despite the account being protected by MFA — because the keylogger captured the credential itself rather than needing to separately defeat the second factor.

**Escalation.** The compromised engineer's corporate LastPass vault held encrypted secure notes containing the access and decryption keys for LastPass's AWS S3 production backups and other cloud storage resources — meaning one engineer's credentials, once unlocked, reached the decryption material for customer vault backups across the company, not just that engineer's own work.

**Blast radius.** Using the stolen keys, the attacker accessed cloud storage between August 12 and October 26, 2022, and copied backups containing both unencrypted metadata (company names, end-user names, billing addresses, email addresses, phone numbers, and IP addresses) and the encrypted customer password vaults themselves, protected only by each user's own master password. LastPass said vault fields remain protected by 256-bit AES encryption decryptable only with a user's master password, and urged customers with weaker master passwords to rotate stored credentials; the company did not disclose an exact count of vaults copied, describing the exposure in terms of the cloud storage environment rather than a specific customer number.

**Human factor.** A single engineer's unmanaged home computer, running unpatched consumer software unrelated to his job, sat on the other end of credentials that could unlock backup decryption for LastPass's entire customer base — one missed home-software update became a company-wide incident.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (developer and engineer machines used for privileged access should be managed and patched, even personal ones, and free of unrelated high-attack-surface consumer software), CRED-04 (decryption keys for production backups injected at runtime from a secrets manager rather than sitting in any individual's personal vault, however senior), DATA-08 (backups access-controlled so no single compromised credential, however senior, is sufficient on its own to decrypt them).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The OAIC filed Federal Court civil penalty proceedings against Medibank (ongoing into 2024) centered specifically on the absence of MFA on its VPN — a regulatory action naming a missing control, not just the breach itself, as the violation.
- Rackspace permanently discontinued its Hosted Exchange product line rather than rebuild it, migrating its roughly 30,000 remaining customers to Microsoft 365.
- CrowdStrike's public write-up of the OWASSRF bypass technique (January 2023) let other Exchange operators check whether they had applied Microsoft's actual November 8, 2022 patch, rather than just the earlier interim URL-rewrite mitigation for ProxyNotShell.
- Dropbox's incident, part of a wider wave of CircleCI-impersonation phishing that hit other companies the same month, drew industry attention toward phishing-resistant WebAuthn/FIDO2 keys, which cannot be relayed the way a one-time code from an OTP-style hardware key can.
- SOCRadar's public "BlueBleed" self-check tool for the Microsoft exposure became a template other researchers reused: letting organizations check their own exposure in a large, disputed-scope leak without waiting on the vendor's own confirmation.
- Toyota's five-year-old exposed key, alongside a string of other 2022 secrets-in-GitHub incidents industry-wide, fed increased adoption of automated secret-scanning as a default check on code pushed to any repository, private or public.
