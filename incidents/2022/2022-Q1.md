```

**What happened.** On January 2, 2022, the Lapsus$ extortion group knocked the web presence of Impresa — Portugal's largest media conglomerate, owner of the Expresso newspaper and SIC television network — offline, defacing its sites with a ransom note and threatening to leak internal data. Impresa said it had regained control of the compromised Amazon Web Services account within hours; radio and cable broadcasts continued uninterrupted throughout.

**Entry.** Impresa's ransom note said the attacker had obtained access to the company's AWS account; neither Impresa nor Lapsus$ disclosed how that account itself was compromised, so the initial access technique is undocumented.

**Escalation.** No further escalation was needed — the compromised AWS account itself held the keys to Impresa's entire public-facing infrastructure, letting the group deface every site the company operated from a single point of entry.

**Blast radius.** Expresso's website and all SIC television channels' web and streaming presence went dark; Lapsus$ also hijacked Expresso's Twitter account to post a fake announcement and sent taunting text messages to some of Impresa's own audience. No ransom payment or confirmed data leak followed.

**Human factor.** A single cloud account carrying enough privilege to take down a national media group's entire online footprint is an access-design failure, not a technical exploit — whoever provisioned that account did not need it scoped that broadly for day-to-day operation.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-03 (least-privilege IAM so no single credential reaches every property), CLOUD-08 (the AWS console kept off a path reachable from a compromised credential alone), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the account, which would have blocked reuse of a stolen credential regardless of how it was obtained).

  - https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2022/01/15/destructive-malware-targeting-ukrainian-organizations/
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa22-057a
```

**What happened.** Microsoft's Threat Intelligence Center disclosed on January 15, 2022 that it had found destructive malware, later named WhisperGate, on the systems of multiple Ukrainian government agencies and technology organizations, first observed two days earlier. Disguised as ransomware, it was in fact a wiper: a first stage overwrote the master boot record with a fake ransom note, and a second stage corrupted files matching 191 extensions, destroying data with no real means of recovery. The intrusion landed in the same window as the defacement of a number of Ukrainian government websites, roughly six weeks before Russia's invasion.

**Entry.** Neither Microsoft's disclosure nor CISA's subsequent joint advisory named a confirmed initial-access vector. Outside researchers floated candidate theories — a compromised software supply-chain path through a Ukrainian IT vendor, separately a content-management-system vulnerability — but none was confirmed as the actual path for WhisperGate specifically, so this record does not adopt either as fact.

**Escalation.** The wiper was deployed against systems at multiple government agencies within a short window, and researchers assessed the attackers likely held access to at least some victim networks for an extended period beforehand — consistent with lateral movement across a poorly segmented government network well before the destructive payload ran.

**Blast radius.** Microsoft confirmed WhisperGate on "dozens" of impacted systems across multiple Ukrainian government, non-profit and IT organizations; neither Microsoft nor CISA published a precise host or agency count, and no organization-by-organization damage assessment was made public.

**Human factor.** None identified with certainty at the level of any single organization — the wiper's near-simultaneous reach across multiple agencies points to systemic gaps in Ukrainian government network segmentation and detection capacity heading into a period of acute geopolitical risk, rather than one traceable decision.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and admin actions, which might have surfaced the extended access researchers suspect preceded the wipe), DATA-08 (offline backups not writable by production credentials — the real recovery path once a wiper executes), OBSV-08 (log retention long enough to reconstruct a dwell period measured in months rather than days).

scale: 515000 people

sources:
  - https://www.icrc.org/en/document/cyber-attack-icrc-what-we-know
  - https://www.icrc.org/en/document/sophisticated-cyber-attack-targets-red-cross-red-crescent-data-500000-people
```

**What happened.** The ICRC disclosed on January 19, 2022 that an attacker had accessed servers holding personal data on more than 515,000 people — including people separated from family by conflict or migration, missing persons, and detainees — collected by at least 60 Red Cross and Red Crescent national societies worldwide. A cybersecurity firm hired by the ICRC detected the intrusion on January 18, 2022, more than two months after the attacker first got in.

**Entry.** The attacker exploited an unpatched critical authentication-bypass vulnerability, CVE-2021-40539, in Zoho's ManageEngine ADSelfService Plus tool, running on a server operated by an external contractor in Switzerland that stored data on the ICRC's behalf. A vendor patch existed, and the flaw had already been flagged as under active exploitation elsewhere months before this contractor was hit.

**Escalation.** None required — the authentication-bypass vulnerability granted admin-level access directly, with no separate privilege-escalation step needed to reach the humanitarian data it held.

**Blast radius.** Data on more than 515,000 highly vulnerable people — names, locations, contact information, and details of their separation from family or detention — sat exposed for 70 days before detection. The ICRC took the servers offline immediately and said it found no evidence the data had been leaked or sold publicly, but could not rule out access by additional parties during the dwell window.

**Human factor.** A patch for the exploited vulnerability existed and was not applied on a system holding some of the most sensitive data any humanitarian organization collects, at a contractor one step removed from the ICRC's own security team — a patching and vendor-oversight gap rather than a novel attack technique.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding sensitive data, paired with visibility into what they run), VENDOR-06 (contractually requiring the contractor to act on vendor security advisories, since CVE-2021-40539 had public guidance well before November 2021), CLOUD-01 (regularly enumerating what a contractor exposes to the internet rather than assuming it is managed).

  - https://www.cpomagazine.com/cyber-security/crypto-com-hack-originating-from-2fa-bypass-exceeds-30-million-forcing-refunds-and-new-security-measures/
```

**What happened.** On January 17, 2022, the cryptocurrency exchange Crypto.com detected unauthorized withdrawals from 483 customer accounts. Attackers moved out 4,836.26 ETH (about $15.2 million) and 443.93 BTC (about $18.6 million), plus a smaller amount in other tokens, for a total of roughly $34.65 million, without those accounts' two-factor authentication codes being entered.

**Entry.** Crypto.com confirmed that withdrawals were "approved without 2FA authentication input from the user" but never publicly detailed the underlying mechanism that let a transaction bypass the check — whether a flaw in the verification logic itself or a compromised internal control point. Lacking that detail, this record tags the class of failure (authentication logic circumvented) rather than a specific named technique.

**Escalation.** None required — once the 2FA check was bypassed for a given account, the withdrawal itself was the end state; no further internal privilege escalation was needed to move funds out.

**Blast radius.** Roughly $34.65 million in cryptocurrency was withdrawn across 483 accounts before Crypto.com paused withdrawals platform-wide. The funds were routed through the Tornado Cash mixer, cutting off further tracing. Crypto.com fully reimbursed all affected customers and absorbed the loss itself.

**Human factor.** A withdrawal-approval workflow that could, for a subset of accounts, complete without the second factor it was designed to require is a process failure in how the control was implemented and tested, not a case of users being tricked or passwords being guessed.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (MFA enforced with no code path that can silently complete a state-changing action like a withdrawal without it), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal withdrawal volume per account, which could have caught the pattern faster than user reports did), HUMAN-02 (a second, independent approval step for high-value or newly initiated withdrawals rather than a single automated check).

```

**What happened.** On January 20, 2022, an attacker ran a multi-hour brute-force campaign against Thalia's online bookstore, systematically trying large numbers of username-and-password combinations against the login form until some succeeded. Thalia said no orders were placed and no customer data was altered while the accounts were accessible, reset all customer passwords as a precaution, and notified affected users by email.

**Blast radius.** An unspecified subset of Thalia's German customer base had their accounts accessed for several hours; Thalia stated no purchases were made and no data was changed, though what an attacker could view inside a live session is not itself nothing.

**Human factor.** The gap was organizational, not individual: Thalia's login page had no intrusion-prevention control — no lockout, no rate limit, no CAPTCHA escalation — so an attack a basic control would have interrupted within minutes instead ran for hours.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-06 (rate limiting, account lockout, and breached-password screening on the login form itself — the single control this incident is a textbook case for), OBSV-02 (alerting on an abnormal volume of failed logins per minute, surfacing the attack in progress rather than after it succeeded).

**What happened.** Starting around January 29, 2022, BlackCat (ALPHV) ransomware hit IT systems at oil storage and terminal operators across northwestern Europe: Oiltanking GmbH and Mabanaft in Germany, SEA-Invest in Belgium, and Evos in the Netherlands, disrupting 17 port terminals in the Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp refining hub and at Hamburg. German newspaper Handelsblatt broke the story on February 5 after reviewing internal documents from the country's Federal Office for Information Security (BSI).

```

**What happened.** A security researcher examining the source code of GiveSendGo's fundraising page for Canada's "Freedom Convoy" protest found a publicly readable Amazon S3 bucket holding more than 50 gigabytes of files, including passport and driver's license scans donors had uploaded for payment verification since the campaign launched on February 4, 2022. GiveSendGo secured the bucket the same day TechCrunch contacted it, February 8. Days later, a separate and unrelated actor defaced GiveSendGo's site — redirecting it to a page condemning the convoy — and gave the transparency collective DDoSecrets a database covering roughly 92,000 Freedom Convoy donors, followed shortly after by data spanning every campaign ever run on the platform.

GiveSendGo never publicly explained how the later, separate breach that produced the full donor database was carried out, so that portion of the incident is tagged as an undisclosed vector rather than guessed.

**Blast radius.** The bucket held identity documents for the subset of Freedom Convoy donors who had uploaded them since February 4; the follow-on breach exposed names, email addresses, zip codes, donation amounts and limited payment data for roughly 92,000 Freedom Convoy donors, plus an unquantified set of records spanning the platform's full campaign history. GiveSendGo said no full credit card numbers were taken and no funds were stolen.

**Human factor.** A donation platform that requires identity documents for verification left the bucket storing them publicly readable for at least four days during its highest-profile campaign to date — a storage-configuration check a routine pre-launch or periodic audit should have caught, especially since a similar misconfiguration had reportedly been flagged on the same bucket years earlier.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy and campaign-specific buckets audited on a schedule, not just at creation), CLOUD-01 (regular enumeration of what is actually reachable from the internet), HUMAN-07 (a launch checklist for high-traffic, high-sensitivity campaigns, given the bucket handled government ID scans).

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-NEW (DDoS scrubbing and traffic-absorption capacity provisioned and load-tested in advance against nation-state-scale volumetric attacks, not just commodity botnet traffic — the checklist does not yet have a dedicated control ID for DDoS resilience), OBSV-05 (a rehearsed incident-communication plan, which Ukrainian officials visibly used to reassure the public quickly that funds were safe despite the outage).

**What happened.** Lapsus$ posted screenshots on March 22, 2022 appearing to show internal Okta systems, prompting Okta to confirm that a support engineer at Sitel — a subprocessor (via its Sykes unit) providing Okta customer support — had had their account compromised in January, giving an attacker remote access to the engineer's laptop for roughly five days. Okta said it had been told of the Sitel incident at the time but did not learn its full extent, or notify affected customers, until Lapsus$'s public post two months later; the company later said it "should have moved more swiftly."

**Entry.** The attacker used stolen credentials to attempt a login to the Sitel engineer's account, which triggered a legitimate MFA push notification; the engineer approved it, and that approval let the attacker reach the engineer's thin-client desktop over RDP. This combines a compromised subprocessor employee's device (third-party access) with a successful MFA-fatigue, push-approval social-engineering step.

**Escalation.** The attacker did not compromise Okta's core service or infrastructure — they operated within the same limited support-engineer tooling the legitimate engineer had, which Okta says could view customer tenant user lists and reset a limited set of user passwords and MFA factors, but could not create or delete users, download customer databases, or reach Okta's source code.

**Blast radius.** Okta said roughly 366 organizations — about 2.5% of its customer base — had their tenants accessed during the window, primarily allowing the viewing of user lists and support tickets and, for some, password or MFA-factor resets; Okta found no evidence of unauthorized configuration changes or data exfiltration at the scale Lapsus$'s screenshots implied, but the two-month gap between Sitel's private notice and public customer notification was itself widely criticized.

**Human factor.** A support engineer approving an MFA push they had not initiated is the proximate human action, but the deeper failure was organizational: Okta relied on a subprocessor's own incident response and did not independently verify the scope or push full customer notification for two months after being told an incident had occurred.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-09 (MFA that cannot be satisfied by a single tap — number matching or a hardware key would have defeated the push-approval step), VENDOR-08 (subprocessor access to support tooling time-boxed and separately monitored by Okta itself, not left to Sitel's own detection), HUMAN-05 (training that specifically covers unsolicited MFA prompts as a live social-engineering technique, for staff at any organization with access to customer environments).

**What happened.** Microsoft confirmed on March 22, 2022, in the same blog post where it detailed the tactics of DEV-0537 (Lapsus$), that a single employee account had been compromised, giving the group "limited access" to Microsoft's systems. Lapsus$ published roughly 37 GB of source code, including approximately 90% of the code for Bing, plus portions of Bing Maps and Cortana. **Entry.** Microsoft's post detailed DEV-0537's typical initial-access toolkit broadly — credential-stealing malware, purchased credentials and session tokens, paying employees or suppliers for access, and social-engineering help desks — but did not say which of these, if any, was used against the specific account compromised at Microsoft; this record does not assume a specific technique on that basis.

**Human factor.** One compromised employee account was the entire chain — no further exploitation was needed once that credential was obtained — the clearest illustration this quarter of Lapsus$'s core method: the group repeatedly substituted low-cost credential acquisition for technical exploitation, and it worked.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every engineering account with source-repository access), CRED-06 (repository access scoped tightly enough that a single compromised account cannot reach 90% of a major product's codebase), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of large source-control read/export volume tied to one account).

**What happened.** On March 30, 2022, Buenos Aires-founded IT consultancy Globant confirmed that Lapsus$ had gained unauthorized access to "a limited section" of its code repositories, after the group posted a 70 GB torrent on its Telegram channel containing source code, internal documentation and credentials. Named folders in the leak referenced client engagements including Facebook, Apple's Health app, C-SPAN, Fortune, DHL and Arcserve, among others.

**Entry.** Globant did not disclose how Lapsus$ first gained access; this record uses entry/unknown.

**Escalation.** The leaked data included administrator credentials for Globant's own development platforms (Jira, Confluence, GitHub and Crucible), TLS certificate private keys and chains, cloud and third-party API keys, and roughly 150 SQL database files — a breadth of highly privileged material well beyond what Globant's "limited section" characterization suggests, and one that itself explains how Lapsus$ could reach as much as it did from a single point of entry.

**Blast radius.** The 70 GB leak spanned source code and internal artifacts tied to numerous corporate clients, plus over 3,000 customer-related documents and the credential and key material described above; Globant activated its security protocols and launched an investigation but did not publicly quantify how many client engagements were actually affected.

**Human factor.** The presence of private keys, certificates and admin credentials sitting inside source repositories — rather than in a secrets manager — is the same root pattern behind a large share of this file's other source-code breaches: developers treat the repository as safe storage for secrets because reaching it already implies some level of trust, and one compromise then cashes in everything stored that way at once.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no secret — key, certificate, credential — ever committed to source, config or repository history, which alone would have prevented the most damaging category of this leak), CLOUD-03 (least-privilege access so one compromised point cannot reach code and secrets for dozens of unrelated client engagements at once), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every account with repository access).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Microsoft's MSTIC published a detailed profile of DEV-0537 (Lapsus$) tactics — MFA-fatigue push-bombing, paying employees and contractors for access, and social-engineering help desks — that became a widely cited reference for defenders during and after this quarter's spree.
- City of London Police arrested seven people aged 16–21 on March 24, 2022 in connection with the Lapsus$ investigation, the first major law-enforcement action against the group.
- CISA, the FBI and NSA issued and repeatedly updated joint advisory AA22-057A on destructive malware targeting Ukraine, publishing indicators of compromise for WhisperGate, HermeticWiper and related wipers as each was discovered.
- Okta acknowledged it "should have moved more swiftly" in disclosing the Sitel incident and committed to tightening subprocessor incident-notification requirements.
- Crypto.com rebuilt its withdrawal-approval flow — a mandatory 24-hour delay before a newly whitelisted address can receive funds — and forced a global 2FA re-enrollment after reimbursing the roughly $34.65 million taken.
- The ICRC added two-factor authentication and new threat-detection tooling to the previously unpatched contractor system, and the breach renewed sector-wide scrutiny of how humanitarian organizations vet the security of subcontracted data hosts handling data on at-risk populations.
