# 2022 Q2 — Stolen Tokens and National Emergencies

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/public-database — every record this quarter used a different mechanism to get in; the closest thing to a theme is plain, unauthenticated exposure (a Turkish airline's flight-ops bucket, a Chinese police dashboard open for over a year) rather than any single broken defense

```yaml
sources:

  - https://edition.cnn.com/2022/04/07/tech/cash-app-investing-breach
```

**What happened.** A former Block, Inc. employee downloaded internal reports from Cash App Investing, the company's brokerage subsidiary, containing U.S. customer brokerage data. The employee had held legitimate access to these reports while employed but accessed them again on December 10, 2021, after their employment had already ended. Block disclosed the incident in an SEC filing on April 4, 2022 — roughly four months later — and began notifying approximately 8.2 million current and former Cash App Investing customers.

**Entry.** The former employee's access to Block's internal reporting systems was not cut off at termination, letting them pull reports they had routinely accessed as part of their job while still on staff.

**Escalation.** None required — the ex-employee already held the access needed to view and download the reports; no privilege escalation or lateral movement was necessary.

**Blast radius.** Downloaded reports held full names and brokerage account numbers for approximately 8.2 million people, and for some customers also included portfolio value, holdings, and a single day's stock trading activity. Block said Social Security numbers, usernames, passwords, dates of birth, payment card data, and bank account numbers were not included, and no confirmed misuse of the data was reported.

**Human factor.** Block's offboarding process did not revoke this employee's access to internal customer reports at termination, and the roughly four-month gap between the access and public disclosure became its own point of criticism and a focus of the resulting class-action litigation.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-03 (joiners/movers/leavers tracked with a maximum revocation time), AUTH-08 (access inventoried and expiring when unused), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a disclosure clock, given the four-month gap to public notice).


**What happened.** Mailchimp, an email-marketing platform owned by Intuit, identified on March 26, 2022 that an intruder had gained access to an internal tool used by its customer-support and account-administration teams after deceiving Mailchimp employees and contractors into handing over their credentials. The company said the attacker viewed 319 customer accounts and exported audience/contact data from 102 of them, disproportionately targeting cryptocurrency and financial-services businesses; hardware crypto-wallet maker Trezor confirmed its exported mailing list was used days later to send a convincing phishing email to its own customers.

**Entry.** A social-engineering attack against Mailchimp staff and contractors obtained their login credentials; Mailchimp did not publicly specify the delivery channel (email, phone, or otherwise), only that it succeeded against multiple employee and contractor accounts.

**Escalation.** The stolen employee credentials opened Mailchimp's internal customer-support and account-administration tool directly — a single internal tool that could view and export any customer's account and audience data — so no further escalation step was needed once one employee account was compromised.

**Blast radius.** 319 Mailchimp customer accounts were viewed and 102 had audience/contact-list data exported, with cryptocurrency and financial-services customers disproportionately represented. At least one downstream customer, Trezor, saw its exported list used in a follow-on phishing campaign against its own users days later; Mailchimp declined to name the other affected customers.

**Human factor.** This was the first of what became a pattern for Mailchimp — the company disclosed further, similar internal-tool intrusions via employee social engineering in August 2022 and January 2023 — suggesting the access model for its support tooling, not any one employee's mistake, was the recurring weakness.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every account with access to internal tooling), AUTH-07 (admin/support tooling behind separate, audited authentication rather than ordinary employee login), HUMAN-05 (training reflecting current social-engineering technique).

```yaml
  - https://thehackernews.com/2022/04/github-says-hackers-breach-dozens-of.html
```

**What happened.** GitHub Security disclosed on April 15, 2022 that an attacker had used stolen OAuth user tokens — originally issued to two third-party CI/CD integrators, Heroku and Travis CI — to authenticate to the GitHub API and download private repository contents from dozens of organizations. GitHub's own systems were not compromised; the tokens were taken from Heroku's and Travis CI's side and abused exactly as the legitimate integrations would be. npm, a GitHub-owned package registry, was among the affected organizations: the attacker used a compromised AWS API key found inside downloaded npm repository content to reach npm's production infrastructure, which is what led GitHub to detect the campaign on April 12.

**Entry.** The attacker did not attack GitHub directly. They obtained valid OAuth tokens that Heroku and Travis CI held for their GitHub integrations — third-party applications many organizations had authorized for CI/CD — and used those tokens to pull private repository contents, the same access the integrations themselves had.

**Escalation.** These OAuth tokens carried broad repository read access and, per GitHub's own account, did not expire, so a token stolen from a third party's infrastructure remained a fully valid, wide-reaching key into every customer who had ever authorized that integration.

**Blast radius.** GitHub said the attacker downloaded data from "dozens of organizations," an exact count it did not disclose. The attacker's behavior suggested they were mining stolen private repository contents for further secrets to pivot into other infrastructure — confirmed in npm's case, where a secret found in downloaded content gave the attacker a path into npm's own production systems.

**Human factor.** Every affected organization had made a routine, unremarkable decision — authorizing Heroku or Travis CI to access their GitHub repositories for CI/CD — with no visibility into, or control over, how long those third parties retained the resulting tokens or how well they protected them.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-02 (OAuth grants scoped, expiring, and reviewed, with unused ones revoked), CRED-07 (credentials expire; no indefinite token lifetimes), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of API access patterns, which is ultimately what surfaced the npm compromise).

```yaml
impact: [impact/fraud]
factor: [factor/process-failure]
scale: undisclosed

confidence: medium
controls: [HUMAN-02, OBSV-02, HUMAN-NEW]
sources:
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/beanstalk-defi-platform-loses-182-million-in-flash-loan-attack/
  - https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2022/04/17/attacker-drains-182m-from-beanstalk-stablecoin-protocol
```

**What happened.** On April 17, 2022, an attacker used one Ethereum transaction to take out roughly $1 billion in flash loans, used the borrowed funds to instantly acquire more than two-thirds of the voting power ("Stalk") in Beanstalk Farms' decentralized stablecoin protocol, and passed a malicious governance proposal transferring the protocol's collateral to their own wallet. The protocol lost approximately $182 million in collateral; sources differ on the attacker's realized profit after slippage and swap costs, with estimates ranging roughly $76–80 million, part of which was laundered through Tornado Cash while $250,000 was separately sent to a Ukraine crypto-donation address.

**Entry.** Beanstalk's own governance mechanism was used exactly as designed; the attacker needed no credential, bug, or code exploit beyond acquiring enough governance tokens, which a flash loan let them do for the span of a single transaction.

**Escalation.** Beanstalk's governance included an "emergencyCommit" function that let a proposal with supermajority support execute immediately, skipping the standard review delay other proposals were subject to; the flash-loaned voting power qualified for this fast path, so the malicious proposal both passed and executed inside the same transaction that borrowed the money.

**Blast radius.** The protocol's collateral, contributed by its liquidity providers, was drained in full — roughly $182 million in value — inside a transaction lasting seconds, and Beanstalk's BEAN stablecoin immediately lost its dollar peg.

**Human factor.** The protocol's governance design assumed acquiring a voting majority would take time and capital an attacker had to hold at risk; flash loans removed that assumption, and nobody at Beanstalk had revisited whether the emergency fast-path was still safe once instant, uncollateralized voting power became possible.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-02 (high-impact actions require a second, independent check), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal voting-power or fund-movement volume), HUMAN-NEW (a mandatory timelock between a governance proposal reaching quorum and its execution, so flash-loan-funded voting power cannot immediately move funds — Beanstalk's emergency path had no such delay).


**What happened.** Conti ransomware operators gained access to Costa Rica's Ministry of Finance (Hacienda) network around April 11, 2022, exfiltrated roughly 672 GB of data by April 15, and deployed ransomware that took tax and customs systems offline starting April 18 — the date Hacienda first announced the intrusion publicly. Conti demanded $10 million and threatened to leak the stolen data; the campaign eventually touched around 27 government institutions, and incoming president Rodrigo Chaves declared a national emergency on his first day in office, May 8, 2022. Weeks later, on May 31, 2022, the public health service (Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social, CCSS) was separately hit by Hive ransomware, taking hospital computer systems offline; researchers noted Hive's likely operational overlap with Conti, which was rebranding under sanctions pressure at the time.

**Entry.** Costa Rican authorities never publicly disclosed how Conti's operators first gained access to the Ministry of Finance network, nor how Hive later reached CCSS; official statements described discovery and impact, not the intrusion vector.

**Escalation.** Not detailed publicly, but reaching around 27 separate government institutions — including tax administration, customs, labor, meteorological, and health-fund systems — from what began as one ministry's compromise is consistent with lateral movement across shared or weakly segmented government network infrastructure.

**Blast radius.** Treasury digital services (tax collection, customs, government payroll and payments) were down for weeks; Conti said it had leaked the large majority of the stolen 672 GB. The government said the disruption was costing the country tens of millions of dollars per day in the private sector alone, though no single confirmed total cost was ever published. CCSS hospitals separately had to revert to paper records after the Hive attack in May.

**Human factor.** A fragmented, dozens-of-agencies government network had no apparent single incident commander or coordinated response plan, and a health agency already named as affected in the April wave was hit again by a second ransomware group in May — evidence that containment after the first intrusion was incomplete.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between agencies and tiers so one ministry's compromise cannot cascade to dozens of others), OBSV-05 (a written, government-wide incident plan naming a decider and a communicator, which the months-long, multi-agency response suggests did not exist), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on remote-access and admin accounts — standard against the credential-based initial access ransomware crews like Conti and Hive typically use, even though this record's own entry vector was never confirmed).

```yaml
  - https://www.cpomagazine.com/cyber-security/hackers-accessed-car-owners-personal-information-in-general-motors-credential-stuffing-attack/
```

**What happened.** General Motors detected suspicious login activity against its customer online-services platform between April 11 and April 29, 2022, and disclosed on May 23, 2022 that attackers had used credential stuffing — usernames and passwords leaked from unrelated, non-GM breaches — to log into GM customer accounts. Once inside, attackers viewed personal profile information and, in some cases, redeemed customers' accumulated loyalty reward points for gift cards.

**Entry.** GM stated plainly that "unauthorized parties gained access to customer login credentials that were previously compromised on other non-GM sites and then reused those credentials on the customer's GM account." No GM system was itself breached.

**Escalation.** Wherever a customer had reused a password GM's login accepted, the attacker inherited that customer's full account: name, email, address, family-member usernames and phone numbers, saved destinations and search history, vehicle service/mileage records, Wi-Fi hotspot passwords, and redeemable loyalty points.

**Blast radius.** GM notified at least 5,000 affected customers in California, the only jurisdiction with a public count via the state Attorney General's breach-notification process; the total national figure was not disclosed. GM said financial account numbers, Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers, and dates of birth were not exposed, and committed to restoring any loyalty points fraudulently redeemed.

**Human factor.** GM's login accepted whatever password a customer supplied, including one already known to be compromised elsewhere, with no breached-password check or MFA requirement standing between a reused password and full account access plus redeemable, monetizable reward points.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-06 (rate limiting, lockout, and breached-password checks on the login endpoint), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA that a stolen password alone cannot satisfy), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal reward-point redemption volume, which would have flagged the gift-card conversions in progress).

```yaml
  - https://www.securityweek.com/chinese-threat-actors-exploiting-follina-vulnerability/
```

**What happened.** Security researchers publicly identified a Microsoft Office zero-day, later named "Follina" and tracked as CVE-2022-30190, after independent researcher nao_sec found a malicious Word document, submitted from Belarus, that achieved code execution by abusing the `ms-msdt:` URI protocol handler — no macros required, and triggering even from Word's document-preview pane. Security researcher Kevin Beaumont publicized the finding on May 29, 2022; Microsoft acknowledged it the next day, May 30, and did not ship a patch until the June 14, 2022 Patch Tuesday. In the gap, multiple threat clusters used it in the wild, including the China-linked group TA413, which built lure documents impersonating the Central Tibetan Administration's "Women Empowerment Desk" to target Tibetan advocacy organizations with credential-stealing malware.

**Entry.** A crafted Word document containing a remote template reference triggered the Windows Support Diagnostic Tool (MSDT) via a URI handler, executing attacker-supplied PowerShell with no macro, no explicit "Enable Content" click, and no patch available at the time of exploitation.

**Escalation.** None required — the exploit chain delivered code execution directly in the context of the user who opened or merely previewed the document; no separate privilege-escalation step was needed for the payload to run.

**Blast radius.** Exact victim numbers were never comprehensively disclosed across the multiple, unrelated campaigns that used Follina before the patch. Confirmed targeting included Tibetan advocacy organizations (TA413, espionage-motivated) plus other campaigns that adopted it to deliver commodity malware once the technique became public knowledge; Microsoft's June 14 patch closed the hole for systems that applied it.

**Human factor.** Lure documents still required a person to open or preview a file from an unexpected sender; the zero-day removed only the "enable macros" step attackers previously needed, not the social-engineering step that got the document opened in the first place.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (training that still treats "no macros needed" documents as a risk, not a safety signal), INPUT-06 (no automatic template/content evaluation of untrusted documents — Microsoft's interim workaround was disabling the MSDT URL-protocol registry key entirely before a patch existed), OBSV-06 (a fast, published path for independent researchers like nao_sec to route findings to Microsoft, since the technique reportedly circulated in disguised form for weeks before it was recognized as a security bug).

**What happened.** Researchers at Safety Detectives found an unsecured, unauthenticated AWS S3 bucket belonging to PegasusEFB, the Electronic Flight Bag software used by Turkish low-cost carrier Pegasus Airlines, on February 28, 2022. The bucket held roughly 23 million files — about 6.5 TB — including EFB software source code; researchers reported it to Pegasus on March 1 and again on March 20 before the airline secured it around March 24, 2022. The findings were published in early June 2022, with no confirmed evidence any other party had accessed the data before it was closed.

**Entry.** The S3 bucket required no authentication and was reachable by anyone who found or guessed its address; no credential theft or exploit was involved, only an unauthenticated cloud storage bucket serving pilot navigation and flight-operations software.

**Blast radius.** Around 23 million files (about 6.5 TB) were exposed, covering flight-navigation and takeoff/landing software, source code, and staff data. No confirmed malicious access was identified — a research firm found and reported it rather than an attacker exploiting it — so impact is recorded as none-confirmed rather than a breach.

**Human factor.** A vendor supplying safety-relevant flight-operations software to a national airline left a storage bucket at public, unauthenticated settings, and took over three weeks and two follow-up messages from outside researchers before securing it.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (nothing publicly reachable unless deliberately decided, checked on a recurring basis), DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with legacy buckets audited), CRED-01 (no plaintext secret keys or passwords in source code, which would have contained the damage even had the bucket been found by an attacker instead of a researcher).

**What happened.** On June 30, 2022, a BreachForums user calling themselves "ChinaDan" advertised what they said was roughly 23 TB of data from the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau's database, covering more than 1 billion Chinese residents' names, addresses, birthplaces, national ID numbers, and phone numbers, plus billions of police case records, for sale for 10 bitcoin (about $200,000). Months earlier, in April 2022, independent security researcher Bob Diachenko had already found and reported the same exposure — a Kibana dashboard for the database left reachable on the open internet with no password, apparently for over a year — but it remained open until shortly before the forum listing appeared.

**Entry.** A Kibana management dashboard sitting in front of the underlying police database was reachable from the public internet without any authentication, letting anyone who found its address query the data directly.

**Escalation.** None required — the dashboard gave direct query and export access to the underlying records; no further privilege escalation was needed once the dashboard itself was reachable.

**Blast radius.** The seller's claim of 1 billion residents' records and several billion case records was never confirmed by Shanghai or Chinese national authorities, who did not publicly acknowledge the incident and whose domestic platforms censored discussion of it. Journalists who examined a released sample of 750,000 records found dozens of entries that matched real individuals when contacted, evidence the underlying data is genuine without confirming the seller's full-scale claim. Because the headline figure originates entirely from the seller's own marketplace listing, this record carries low confidence.

**Human factor.** A researcher had already identified and reported the identical exposure roughly two months before the criminal listing appeared, and — so far as is publicly known — nothing was done to close it in that window; whatever process was supposed to route an outside researcher's report to action either did not exist or did not work.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and their management dashboards not reachable from the public internet, regardless of whether the database itself is separately authenticated), CLOUD-01 (recurring enumeration of what is actually publicly reachable, which would have caught a dashboard open for a year without anyone inside noticing), OBSV-06 (a published, working channel for an outside researcher's report to reach someone able to act on it — its apparent absence here turned a fixable exposure into a mass leak).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- GitHub published guidance urging every organization to review and prune authorized OAuth/GitHub App grants, and began treating CI/CD integrator tokens as high-value secrets requiring the same scoping and expiry as any other credential.
- Microsoft published an interim workaround for Follina — disabling the `ms-msdt` URL-protocol handler via a registry edit — two weeks before its June 14, 2022 patch shipped, and it became a widely adopted stopgap across enterprise security teams in the interim.
- The U.S. State Department's Rewards for Justice program offered up to $10 million for information on Conti's leadership days after the Costa Rica attack, mirroring Conti's own $10 million ransom demand; Conti's leak infrastructure went dark within weeks as the group dissolved into smaller, rebranded cells (including, researchers believe, elements of Hive).
- Costa Rica's national emergency declaration (Decree No. 42542, May 8, 2022) was among the first instances of a national government formally invoking emergency powers specifically in response to a ransomware attack, and drew offers of incident-response assistance from other governments.
- The Beanstalk exploit became a widely cited case study across DeFi protocols for adding mandatory timelocks between a governance vote passing and its execution, closing the "instant flash-loan quorum" pattern the attacker used.
- Mailchimp's repeated internal-tool compromises (this incident, and further ones later in 2022 and in January 2023) pushed customers in regulated and crypto-adjacent industries toward requiring hardware-key MFA and IP allowlisting from any vendor holding their contact and audience data.
