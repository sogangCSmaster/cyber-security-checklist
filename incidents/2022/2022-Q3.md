# 2022 Q3 — Social Engineering Beats Software Defenses

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — a phished, purchased, or synced human identity was the recurring way in at Twilio, Cisco, Uber, and Rockstar, not a software flaw
> - **New technique this quarter:** MFA push-bombing paired with a vishing or "just approve the next one" pretext, used against Cisco and Uber within weeks of each other
> - **Recurring failure:** access controls that had silently failed months or years earlier — Optus's API since 2018, a police dashboard left open a year or more, a Log4j remnant nine months unpatched — went unnoticed until someone else finally looked
> - **Records in this file:** 12

## Incidents

```yaml
  - https://www.theregister.com/2022/07/05/shanghai_police_database_for_sell/
```

**What happened.** On or around July 3, 2022, a poster using the handle "ChinaDan" advertised roughly 23–24 terabytes of data on a hacking forum, claiming it came from the Shanghai police and covered names, addresses, birthplaces, national ID numbers, phone numbers, and case records for what they described as over 1 billion Chinese residents, priced at 10 bitcoin (about $200,000). Chinese authorities never publicly confirmed the breach; discussion of it was actively censored on domestic platforms, and the "1 billion" figure comes solely from the seller's own listing.

**Entry.** Researchers who examined leaked samples concluded the data sat behind a Kibana dashboard — the web interface to an Elasticsearch cluster — that had been reachable on the open internet with no password for at least a year, hosted on Alibaba Cloud. No credential theft or exploit chain was required; the dashboard itself was the vulnerability, open to "anyone with relatively basic technical knowledge."

**Escalation.** None required — the dashboard gave direct query and export access to the underlying police case-management data to anyone who found it.

**Blast radius.** Portions of the leaked sample were independently judged authentic by multiple outlets, but the full billion-person, multi-billion-case-record claim rests entirely on the seller's own description and was never corroborated by an official source, which is why this record's scale is presented as a claim and confidence is low.

**Human factor.** A dashboard exposing a national police database sat open to the internet for a year or more without the agency, its cloud provider, or any third-party audit catching it; whether it was an inherited legacy setup or a forgotten test deployment, nobody was checking.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and their management interfaces are not reachable from the public internet, regardless of cloud provider), CLOUD-01 (recurring enumeration of what is actually internet-facing would have caught this in days, not years), AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated interface — including an administrative dashboard — should return user data).

```yaml
  - https://therecord.media/cisa-iranian-hackers-spent-14-months-in-albanian-govt-network-before-launching-ransomware
```

**What happened.** On July 15, 2022, Iranian state actors operating under the persona "HomeLand Justice" launched a destructive attack against the Albanian government, combining a ransomware-style file encryptor with disk-wiping malware that took government websites and digital public services offline. Albania's government confirmed the attack around July 18; on September 7, 2022 it formally severed diplomatic relations with Iran over it, a rare state-to-state response to a cyberattack, and a second wave of intrusions followed in September that CISA and the FBI attributed to the same actors retaliating for that public attribution.

**Entry.** The joint CISA/FBI advisory (AA22-264A, published September 22, 2022) states the actors first gained access to Albanian government networks roughly 14 months before the destructive payload was triggered, but does not publicly specify the initial technique, so this record is tagged entry/unknown rather than guessed.

**Escalation.** Over that roughly 14-month dwell, the actors moved across the government's network, deploying web shells and living-off-the-land tools to keep access before detonating the wiper broadly rather than against a single system.

**Blast radius.** Multiple Albanian government websites and digital services were taken offline; the government did not publicly quantify systems or records destroyed, so scale is recorded as undisclosed, but the disruption was severe enough that Albania cut diplomatic ties with Iran entirely over it.

**Human factor.** Roughly fourteen months of unnoticed presence in a national government's network is a detection failure at every layer, not a single mistake — logging, alerting, and periodic threat hunting all had the same span of opportunity to catch this and did not.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and lateral-movement indicators across government networks), OBSV-03 (monitoring the monitors — over a year of dwell time in a nation-state target is itself a sensor failure), CLOUD-05 (segmentation so a compromised foothold cannot reach every service the wiper ultimately hit).


**What happened.** A hacker began selling what they described as Neopets' entire user database schema on a hacking forum in July 2022; the exposure, covering usernames, email addresses, and passwords for an estimated 69 million accounts, was reported publicly around July 20–21, 2022. Neopets confirmed a breach and forced a password reset for all users on August 1, 2022; credit card numbers and physical addresses were not part of the exposed schema.

**Entry.** Neopets did not publicly describe how the attacker first obtained access to the database, and no credible independent technical account of the entry mechanism has been published, so this record is tagged entry/unknown rather than guessed.

**Escalation.** None required beyond whatever initial access was obtained — the claim was of a full database-schema export, meaning the data was directly readable and exportable once inside rather than requiring a further privilege-escalation step.

**Blast radius.** An estimated 69 million accounts' usernames, emails, and passwords were exposed and offered for sale; this figure comes from analysis of the leaked database and press reporting rather than a precise count Neopets itself published, which is why confidence here is medium rather than high.

**Human factor.** A legacy web platform that has changed corporate ownership multiple times (most recently to NetDragon Websoft in 2021) went an unknown, evidently long period with unauthorized access to its core user database before anyone — inside the company — noticed; the forced reset only followed the attacker's own decision to start selling the data.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of database access and export volume, which a legacy platform under changing ownership is especially likely to have let lapse), OBSV-03 (verifying detection capability itself still works after an acquisition or platform handoff), DATA-06 (modern, memory-hard password hashing so an exported credential table is not immediately useful even when the database itself is reached).

### I2022Q3-04 · Twilio 0ktapus SMS-phishing campaign

```yaml
id: I2022Q3-04
name: Twilio 0ktapus SMS-phishing campaign
org: Twilio
date_occurred: 2022-08
date_disclosed: 2022-08-07
region: global
sector: [sector/tech, sector/saas]
actor: [actor/scattered-spider]
entry: [entry/smishing]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/pii, impact/credentials]
factor: [factor/social-engineering, factor/weak-auth]
tech: [tech/okta, tech/mobile]
scale: 209 customers
confidence: high
controls: [AUTH-09, AUTH-01, HUMAN-05]
sources:
  - https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/august-2022-social-engineering-attack
  - https://www.group-ib.com/blog/0ktapus/
  - https://techcrunch.com/2022/08/08/twilio-breach-customer-data/
  - https://sec.okta.com/articles/scatterswine/
```

**What happened.** Starting in early August 2022, attackers sent text messages to Twilio employees impersonating the company's IT department, linking to fake single-sign-on pages built to look like Twilio's own login. Twilio confirmed on August 7 that the campaign had tricked multiple employees into entering their Okta credentials and one-time codes; by the time Twilio cut off the last unauthorized access on August 9, 209 of its 270,000-plus customers had had data viewed, along with 93 Authy two-factor accounts. Group-IB showed this was one piece of a single campaign it dubbed "0ktapus" (Okta called it "Scatter Swine," later associated by researchers with the actor cluster known as Scattered Spider), which used the same kit against more than 130 organizations and harvested roughly 9,931 credentials and 5,441 MFA codes since March 2022; downstream users of Twilio's SMS platform, including Signal (I2022Q3-06) and DoorDash (I2022Q3-08), were affected in turn.

**Entry.** Employees who clicked the smishing links landed on a real-time phishing proxy that immediately relayed whatever username, password, and one-time code they typed straight through to Okta's actual login — an adversary-in-the-middle pattern that defeats any MFA method not bound to the device or origin.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the phished session itself — the stolen Okta identity already carried the internal console access the attacker used to view customer contact information and register unauthorized devices on Authy accounts.

**Blast radius.** Twilio said it found no evidence the attacker reached customer console credentials, API keys, or auth tokens; confirmed exposure was limited to contact information for 209 customers and unauthorized device registrations on 93 Authy accounts. The wider campaign's reach — over 130 organizations — made it one of the largest coordinated identity-phishing operations reported that year.

**Human factor.** The phishing kit's URLs spelled out "Twilio," "Okta," and "SSO" and mimicked a routine IT message; trained employees at a company whose core business is authentication and messaging still entered live credentials and codes into it, which is the strongest evidence that awareness training alone was never going to be sufficient.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-09 (MFA that cannot be satisfied by typing a code into a phishing page — hardware security keys or passkeys bound to the origin), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA specifically, which Twilio rolled out as FIDO2 keys after this incident), HUMAN-05 (training current for smishing that spoofs internal IT, not just generic email phishing).

```yaml
sources:

  - https://thehackernews.com/2022/08/cisco-confirms-its-been-hacked-by.html
  - https://www.cpomagazine.com/cyber-security/cisco-network-breach-traced-back-to-compromised-personal-google-account-sophisticated-attacker-used-combination-of-voice-phishing-and-mfa-fatigue/
```

**What happened.** On August 10, 2022, Cisco confirmed that an attacker had gained access to its corporate network on May 24, 2022, and attributed the intrusion to an initial access broker working with the Yanluowang ransomware gang. Cisco's own Talos research team traced the initial foothold to an employee's personal Google account, which had Chrome password sync enabled and held that employee's saved Cisco credentials.

**Entry.** Having obtained the synced Cisco password through the compromised personal account, the attacker still had to clear MFA, so they ran a sustained voice-phishing campaign impersonating trusted organizations by phone, combined with MFA push-notification fatigue, until the employee accepted one of the repeated prompts and granted VPN access.

**Escalation.** Once inside the VPN in the context of that employee, the attacker enrolled additional MFA devices, escalated to administrative access, and moved laterally using living-off-the-land binaries and remote-access tools, eventually reaching Citrix servers and domain controllers before Cisco's security team identified and evicted them.

**Blast radius.** Cisco said it found no evidence that sensitive customer data, employee data, or Cisco's products and services were affected. The attacker did exfiltrate a limited set of non-sensitive files from a Box folder tied to the compromised employee before Yanluowang publicly posted a partial file listing to pressure the company.

**Human factor.** Corporate credentials living in a personal browser profile are only as safe as that person's personal email account, and Cisco had no technical control treating a synced consumer Google account as part of its attack surface — until this incident forced one.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (managed endpoints with no personal credential sync between consumer and corporate accounts), AUTH-09 (MFA that resists both push-fatigue and vishing — number matching or hardware keys, not a bare accept/deny tap), HUMAN-05 (training that names vishing and MFA-fatigue specifically, not only email phishing).

```yaml
  - https://techcrunch.com/2022/08/15/signal-phone-number-exposed-twilio/
```

**What happened.** Signal, which uses Twilio to deliver the SMS codes people enter when registering a phone number, disclosed on August 15, 2022 that the attacker behind the Twilio smishing breach (I2022Q3-04) had used their access to Twilio's customer support console to look up phone numbers and verification codes tied to Signal accounts. Signal said about 1,900 users were affected; the attacker explicitly searched for three of those numbers, and one user reported their account was fraudulently re-registered on another device.

**Entry.** No flaw in Signal's own client, server, or protocol was involved. The path in ran entirely through Twilio, a vendor Signal depended on for SMS delivery, whose support console the 0ktapus attacker had already compromised.

**Escalation.** None required — Twilio's own console access, already obtained through the phishing campaign, was sufficient to look up any phone number's associated SMS history without a further exploit.

**Blast radius.** For up to 1,900 users, either the fact of their phone number being registered to Signal, or the SMS verification code itself, was exposed. Signal said message history, contacts, profile data, and block lists were never at risk, and it force-unregistered affected devices and required re-registration as a precaution.

**Human factor.** Signal's own security engineering was not the weak point; the decision every messaging app that relies on SMS verification has to make — trusting a third-party telecom API with a piece of the authentication chain — was.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of exactly what a vendor like Twilio can see or do on your behalf, so the blast radius of their compromise is known in advance), VENDOR-08 (vendor support-console access to customer data time-boxed and separately monitored, which is what let the attacker search freely once inside Twilio), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows and active monitoring of vendor security advisories, which is how Signal learned of this at all).

```yaml
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/lastpass-developer-systems-hacked-to-steal-source-code/
```

**What happened.** LastPass disclosed on August 25, 2022 that an unauthorized party had used a single compromised developer account to access portions of its development environment over a four-day window, taking source code and proprietary technical documentation; the company said at the time it had seen no evidence that customer data or encrypted vaults were accessed. LastPass's later investigation, detailed in a March 2023 update, traced that developer-account compromise back to the engineer's home computer.

**Entry.** That later investigation found the engineer was running an outdated version of Plex Media Server on a home computer — about 75 releases behind a patch Plex shipped in May 2020 for CVE-2020-5741, a deserialization flaw allowing arbitrary code execution. Through that unpatched software, the attacker installed a keylogger that captured the engineer's LastPass corporate credentials.

**Escalation.** Several of the 14 source-code repositories the attacker reached contained cleartext embedded credentials and stored digital certificates, plus an encrypted key used to secure production backups in AWS S3 — hardcoded secrets already sitting in repositories the compromised developer account could read, rather than anything requiring a separate escalation step.

**Blast radius.** LastPass confirmed source code and internal technical documentation were taken but maintained that customer vaults and account data were not accessed at this stage. That assessment held only for this disclosure window; a related, more damaging intrusion followed in the next quarter using material obtained here, outside the scope of this record.

**Human factor.** One engineer's unpatched personal media-server software, running on the same home computer used to reach a password manager's source code, was the entire gap between "development environment" and "the vendor of record for millions of people's credentials."

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no cleartext credential or certificate should sit in a source repository regardless of who can read it), CICD-07 (developer endpoints — including a remote engineer's home machine — managed, patched, and kept separate from personal software), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime from a manager rather than baked into repositories an engineer's laptop can reach).

```yaml
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/doordash-discloses-new-data-breach-tied-to-twilio-hackers/
```

**What happened.** DoorDash disclosed on August 25, 2022 that an attacker had used credentials stolen from an unnamed third-party vendor to access some of its internal tools, reaching names, emails, delivery addresses, and phone numbers for customers and, for a smaller subset, partial payment card data; names, phone numbers, and emails for delivery workers ("Dashers") were also exposed. DoorDash and Twilio both stated Twilio itself was not the vendor involved, but researchers tied the underlying credential theft to the same 0ktapus phishing campaign (I2022Q3-04) that hit Twilio and 130-plus other organizations.

**Entry.** The attacker did not touch DoorDash's own code or infrastructure directly; they reached it through a vendor employee whose login had already been phished elsewhere in the 0ktapus campaign, then used that vendor's standing integration access into DoorDash's internal tools.

**Escalation.** None required — the vendor's existing access already reached the internal tools holding customer and Dasher contact and partial payment information.

**Blast radius.** DoorDash declined to specify how many customers or Dashers were affected, which vendor was compromised, or exactly when the access was discovered, in response to press questions — a level of disclosure thin enough that this record's confidence is medium despite DoorDash's own acknowledgment of the breach.

**Human factor.** DoorDash's own systems and code were not at fault; the exposure existed because a vendor held standing access into DoorDash's internal tools that DoorDash neither scoped nor monitored closely enough to limit the damage once that vendor's own employee was compromised.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (a maintained inventory of every vendor holding a token or standing access into internal systems), VENDOR-08 (vendor access time-boxed and separately monitored rather than an always-on integration), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the vendor's side would have stopped the upstream compromise DoorDash had no direct control over, which is itself the argument for the other two controls).


**What happened.** Suffolk County, New York declared a state of emergency on September 8, 2022 after the BlackCat/AlphV ransomware group encrypted systems across county government, taking the county clerk's office, the sheriff's office, 911 dispatch support, traffic-ticket payment, and other services offline for months. A county legislative investigation later found the attackers had first gained access through the county clerk's office in December 2021 — roughly nine months before the ransomware was detonated — by exploiting the Log4j vulnerability disclosed that same month.

**Entry.** The county had attempted to patch Log4j when the vulnerability became public, but remnants of it went unaddressed in at least one system, leaving a known, publicly patched flaw exploitable for months afterward.

**Escalation.** From the initial foothold in the clerk's office, the attackers moved laterally across county systems over the following months before triggering ransomware broadly enough to disrupt 911 support, courts, and multiple agencies at once, consistent with a network that did not contain the intrusion to where it started.

**Blast radius.** BlackCat/AlphV later leaked roughly 400GB of stolen data, including thousands of Social Security numbers and driver's license numbers tied to about 470,000 moving-violation records, plus sheriff's office and court materials. The county's own accounting put remediation and related costs above $25 million, and some services remained degraded for months.

**Human factor.** An FBI agent called the county in June 2022 to warn about suspicious traffic consistent with malware, and the county's own security tooling separately generated alerts about the intrusion — but the county's own investigation into its response found no chief information security officer in place and no incident response plan to act on either warning.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (dependency and vulnerability scanning that verifies a patch actually removed every instance of a known-exploited flaw, not just the ones checked first), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a named decider — the county had neither a CISO nor a plan when the FBI called), CLOUD-05 (segmentation so a nine-month-old foothold in one office cannot reach county-wide 911 and court systems).

```yaml
  - https://thehackernews.com/2022/09/uber-blames-lapsus-hacking-group-for.html
```

**What happened.** On the evening of September 15, 2022, an attacker linked to the Lapsus$-affiliated group behind earlier high-profile intrusions gained access to Uber's internal network, posting messages to a company-wide Slack channel and altering internal tools to announce the compromise; Uber issued its first public update the next day. Uber said the attacker had bought a contractor's corporate password on the dark web after malware had infected the contractor's personal device.

**Entry.** The attacker used that purchased password to repeatedly trigger Uber's two-factor push prompts on the contractor's phone. Each attempt was initially declined, but after roughly an hour of MFA-fatigue bombing — reinforced, Uber said, by a message posing as Uber IT support telling the contractor to approve the next prompt to make it stop — the contractor accepted one, and the attacker was in.

**Escalation.** From that single contractor identity, the attacker reached other employee accounts and found a PowerShell script on an internal network share with administrative credentials for Uber's privileged access management tool hardcoded into it; that access in turn unlocked elevated permissions across AWS, Google Workspace, Slack, and Uber's internal finance and vulnerability-management tools.

**Blast radius.** Uber said the attacker did not reach production systems powering its apps, user account databases, or the datastores holding sensitive rider and driver information. Confirmed access was limited to internal Slack messages, an invoicing tool used by the finance team, Uber's HackerOne bug-bounty dashboard, and some internal source code repositories, none of which Uber said were altered.

**Human factor.** A PowerShell script with hardcoded admin credentials to the very tool meant to control privileged access, left on a network share reachable from a single compromised contractor account, meant Uber's privileged-access model rested on one forgotten script.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-09 (MFA that cannot be satisfied by a tap under social pressure — number matching or a hardware key), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime from a manager, never hardcoded into a script sitting on a share anyone with basic access can browse), AUTH-07 (admin and privileged-access tooling behind its own separate, audited authentication rather than reachable from a standard employee account).

**What happened.** On September 18, 2022, an account using the handle "teapotuberhacker" posted roughly 90 clips (about 50 minutes) of early, unfinished Grand Theft Auto VI development footage to a public forum, claiming to have also obtained source code for GTA V, GTA VI, and multiple Red Dead Redemption titles. Rockstar Games confirmed the following day that "an unauthorized third party illegally accessed and downloaded confidential information from our systems, including early development footage."

**Entry.** Public reporting, corroborated by a later UK criminal case against a teenager identified as Arion Kurtaj, describes the attacker social-engineering a Rockstar employee based in India to obtain access to the company's Okta identity platform — the same general technique the same actor had used against Uber (I2022Q3-10) days earlier. Researchers caution that the precise technical sequence rests substantially on the attacker's own claims and investigative leads rather than a Rockstar-published technical post-mortem, which is why this record's confidence is medium.

**Escalation.** Okta access reached into Rockstar's internal Slack, from which the attacker was able to browse and download source code and video files staff members had shared in Slack channels — internal admin and collaboration tooling doing the work of reaching data that was never meant to be Slack-accessible.

**Blast radius.** Source code for multiple flagship titles and unreleased Grand Theft Auto VI footage were exposed publicly; Rockstar did not disclose a precise technical scope, and no player account or payment data was reported taken. Kurtaj, a minor already facing charges over the Uber-linked intrusion, was later found by a UK court to have carried out the Rockstar hack while subject to release conditions from that earlier case, and was ultimately ordered detained indefinitely under the Mental Health Act.

**Human factor.** The same actor breached two major companies within days of each other using the same basic move — convince one employee at one company to hand over enough access to reach an identity platform — which says as much about how little friction stood between "one phished employee" and "the company's Slack and source repositories" as it does about either victim specifically.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-09 (phishing- and fatigue-resistant MFA on the identity platform itself), AUTH-07 (cross-tool identity access, like Okta-to-Slack reach, kept behind its own separately audited authorization rather than inherited wholesale from a single employee login), HUMAN-05 (training current on targeted social engineering, refreshed the moment the same technique was publicly used against Uber days earlier).

**What happened.** Optus, Australia's second-largest telecommunications carrier, detected suspicious network activity on September 20, 2022 and publicly disclosed two days later that a customer identity-verification API had been queried without authorization for several days, exposing personal information belonging to millions of current, former, and prospective customers. Optus's own initial figure was as many as 9.8 million customers; the Australian Information Commissioner's later Federal Court filing put the number at approximately 9.5 million, alleging a Privacy Act contravention for each individual.

**Entry.** The API required no authentication at all. It had been internet-facing since 2020 for what should have been a limited, controlled purpose, but the access controls meant to gate it had been silently broken by a coding error dating back to 2018 — the endpoint had been capable of returning customer records to anyone who queried it, unauthenticated, for roughly two years before anyone queried it maliciously.

**Escalation.** None required — a single unauthenticated API call, supplied with a customer identifier, returned that customer's full record directly.

**Blast radius.** 2.1 million customers had a government-issued identity document number exposed, 1.2 million of them still valid and unexpired; names, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, and email addresses were exposed for the broader 9.5–9.8 million figure. A ransom demand for A$1.5 million in Monero was posted to a hacking forum on September 24 threatening daily data releases, then withdrawn within hours with an apology; separately, Australian Federal Police arrested a 19-year-old in October 2022 for using a sample of the leaked data to attempt to blackmail 93 individual customers directly — a distinct crime from the original API access. Optus set aside roughly A$140 million for identity-document replacement, credit monitoring, and an independent review.

**Human factor.** Optus initially described the incident as the work of a "sophisticated" attacker, but the Australian government and the OAIC's subsequent civil case both frame it as a basic engineering failure — an authentication check that stopped working in 2018 and was never caught by any code review, penetration test, or internet-facing asset audit in the two years the API sat exposed.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data — this API violated that rule for years before anyone exploited it), CLOUD-01 (nothing is publicly reachable unless deliberately decided to be, verified by regular enumeration, which would have caught an API nobody remembered was internet-facing), CLOUD-09 (an inventory of internet-facing assets and their owners, so a coding error from 2018 does not go unnoticed until 2022).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Cloudflare was targeted by the identical 0ktapus phishing kit used against Twilio and 130-plus other organizations and was not compromised, because it required FIDO2 hardware security keys for internal login rather than push- or code-based MFA — the quarter's clearest natural experiment in what actually stops credential phishing.
- Twilio rolled out FIDO2 hardware tokens, tightened VPN access controls, and ran mandatory security refresher training across the company in direct response to its breach.
- CISA and the FBI issued a joint advisory (AA22-264A) publicly attributing the Albania attacks to Iranian state actors and detailing their tactics, an unusually fast and specific public attribution for a destructive nation-state campaign against a national government.
- CISA separately warned organizations to patch the Plex Media Server vulnerability (CVE-2020-5741) once its role in the LastPass developer-laptop compromise became public — a rare case of a consumer media-server flaw becoming enterprise security guidance.
- The OAIC's pursuit of Optus and Suffolk County's own legislative post-mortem both became widely cited reference cases for "boring" failures — a silently broken authentication check and an incompletely applied patch — causing damage on par with the quarter's more sophisticated intrusions.
- Uber, Rockstar, and Cisco's shared exposure to tap-to-approve push MFA, defeated by fatigue or vishing in all three cases, pushed number-matching and hardware-key MFA from a "nice to have" toward a baseline industry recommendation this quarter.
