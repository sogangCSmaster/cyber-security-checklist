# 2019 Q2 — Unchecked Access and Misplaced Trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/idor — direct, unauthenticated access to records by guessing or incrementing an identifier or simply reaching an open service, the shape behind First American's sequential document IDs, Freedom Mobile's and the Facebook-app buckets' open databases, and Baltimore's exposed RDP port.
> - **New technique this quarter:** none — every mechanism here (IDOR, open cloud storage, phishing, exposed RDP, a dev/prod boundary bleed) was already well understood; what was new was the scale of what sat behind it.
> - **Recurring failure:** sensitive data entrusted to a vendor, app developer, or collections agency with no independent check on how they secured it — Freedom Mobile/Apptium, the Facebook-integrated app developers, Quest Diagnostics/LabCorp/AMCA, and Wipro's own clients all trace back to this.
> - **Records in this file:** 16

scale: 1300000 people
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: 97
confidence: medium
controls: [INPUT-01, CRED-06, CICD-02]
sources:
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/georgia-tech-data-breach-exposes-info-for-13-million-people/
  - https://thehackernews.com/2019/04/georgia-tech-data-breach.html
  - https://kirkpatrickprice.com/blog/georgia-tech-data-breach-web-app-vulnerability-leads-to-compromised-data/
```

**What happened.** In early 2019, Georgia Institute of Technology discovered that an outside party had been pulling records from its central database through a custom-built web application. The university disclosed in April 2019 that the personal information of about 1.3 million current and former students, faculty, staff and applicants — names, addresses, Social Security numbers, and birth dates — had been accessible.

**Entry.** Georgia Tech's own disclosure described only "unauthorized access to a web application"; outside technical analysis of the incident attributed it to a form on a custom application that was vulnerable to SQL injection, letting a submitted query reach the university's central database (medium confidence — the university itself never confirmed the specific vulnerability class).

**Escalation.** The web application's own database credential was scoped far beyond what that one form needed, so a successful injection reached the university's broader central database rather than being contained to the form's own data (`escalation/overscoped-credential`) — no separate privilege-escalation exploit was required.

**Blast radius.** Names, addresses, Social Security numbers, and dates of birth for roughly 1.3 million people, reachable for at least 97 days (December 14, 2018 to March 21, 2019) before anyone noticed.

**Human factor.** The vulnerable form shipped to production without the review that would have caught an unparameterized query, and the application's database credential was never narrowed to only what that form needed. The intrusion was only noticed by accident, when the extraction load visibly degraded the application's performance — nobody was watching for the abnormal query volume itself.

**What would have stopped it.** Parameterized queries on every database-facing form (INPUT-01), a database credential scoped to only the tables one application actually needs rather than the whole central database (CRED-06), and required review before a custom application reaches production (CICD-02).

sources:
  - https://www.upguard.com/breaches/facebook-user-data-leak
  - https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/03/facebook-records-exposed-server/
  - https://www.theregister.com/2019/04/03/upguard_facebook_details_exposed/
```

**What happened.** Security researchers at UpGuard found two Facebook-related datasets sitting in publicly readable Amazon S3 buckets. One, from Mexican media company Cultura Colectiva, held over 540 million records of comments, likes, reactions, account names and Facebook IDs. A second, smaller backup from a defunct app called "At the Pool" additionally included plaintext app account passwords for around 22,000 users. Neither bucket required any credential to read.

**Entry.** `entry/public-storage` — both were Amazon S3 buckets created by third-party app developers with no access policy restricting them to authenticated requests, so anyone who found the URL could download the full contents.

**Escalation.** None required — the buckets themselves held the complete datasets; no further access was needed once the object storage was reachable.

**Blast radius.** Over 540 million Facebook-associated records from Cultura Colectiva, plus an undisclosed additional number of records and roughly 22,000 plaintext passwords from At the Pool. UpGuard first notified Cultura Colectiva on January 10, 2019 and AWS on January 28; the Cultura Colectiva bucket was not secured until April 3, 2019 — after Bloomberg contacted Facebook for comment, 83 days after the first notice.

**Human factor.** Facebook's platform let third-party developers export user data (friends' likes, comments, profile fields) with no enforcement of what happened to it afterward. Both companies then stored that export in cloud buckets they never configured to restrict access, and one sat unfixed for months after being directly warned.

**What would have stopped it.** Object storage private by default with regular audits of legacy buckets (DATA-03), continuous enumeration of what is publicly reachable rather than waiting for a researcher to find it (CLOUD-01), and an inventory of every third party holding a copy of the data so exposure can be found and forced closed quickly (VENDOR-01).

cost_usd: undisclosed

confidence: medium
controls: [AUTH-01, HUMAN-05, VENDOR-08]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2019/04/experts-breach-at-it-outsourcing-giant-wipro/
  - https://www.csoonline.com/article/567161/wipro-breach-highlights-third-party-risk-from-large-it-services-providers.html
  - https://www.darkreading.com/cyberattacks-data-breaches/wipro-breach-actually-a-whole-campaign
```

**What happened.** IT outsourcing giant Wipro, which manages back-office and technology functions for hundreds of client companies, was compromised by a phishing campaign that was active by early March 2019. Investigators found more than 100 Wipro endpoints seeded with the legitimate remote-access tool ScreenConnect, which the intruders used to pivot from Wipro's own network into at least a dozen of Wipro's client organizations.

**Entry.** A phishing email compromised an initial set of Wipro employee credentials; the attackers then used those compromised mailboxes to send more convincing, internally-sourced phishing emails to other Wipro staff, eventually compromising at least 22 additional accounts.

**Escalation.** From the compromised accounts and endpoints, the attackers installed ScreenConnect for persistent access and moved laterally across Wipro's network (`escalation/lateral-movement`). Because Wipro employees had standing, trusted remote access into client systems as part of its outsourcing contracts, that same access let the attackers reach client networks directly — Wipro itself became the path into its customers (`escalation/supply-chain-cascade`).

**Blast radius.** Wipro never gave a public count. Researchers and reporters who reviewed the traffic traced network reconnaissance and phishing back to Wipro's network at roughly a dozen client organizations, with follow-on activity resembling gift-card fraud schemes run through some client accounts, per Krebs' reporting corroborated by Flashpoint. Wipro itself described it only as a "multi-month intrusion" and, without independent confirmation, an "assumed state-sponsored attacker."

**Human factor.** Employees at a company whose business model depends on trusted, privileged remote access into other companies' networks were phished with ordinary email lures, and the intrusion ran for weeks before Wipro's own security team caught it. It was Wipro's clients — noticing suspicious traffic from a partner connection — who forced the issue into the open.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on every account with client-network reach (AUTH-01), security training current on how one compromised mailbox is used to phish the next employee (HUMAN-05), and client-side time-boxing and separate monitoring of vendor remote access rather than standing, implicit trust (VENDOR-08).

```

**What happened.** Independent security researcher Rajshekhar Rajaharia found that Justdial, an Indian local-search and business-directory service, exposed the records of over 100 million users through an old API endpoint. He first posted about it publicly on April 12, 2019; wider coverage followed on April 17. On April 29 he found and reported a second, separate exposed API covering the company's reviewer database, which Justdial fixed the same day.

**Entry.** `entry/unauth-api` — the exposed endpoint required no authentication and had been reachable, according to Rajaharia's research, since at least mid-2015: a leftover from an older version of the app and website that the company had stopped using but never disabled or removed from its servers.

**Escalation.** None required — the API returned full user profiles directly to any caller who queried it; no additional access or privilege was needed to pull one record after another.

**Blast radius.** Names, email addresses, mobile numbers, physical addresses, gender, dates of birth, photos, occupation, and employer information for more than 100 million users were queryable in real time. Justdial disputed the scope of the reporting, saying the exposed data came from an older, deprecated version of its app rather than its live systems; no confirmed case of the data being harvested and misused has been reported, so this record is marked `impact/none-confirmed` alongside the PII exposure itself.

**Human factor.** Nobody at Justdial had gone back to decommission an API endpoint once the app version that used it was retired, so it sat reachable on the open internet for years without anyone noticing. When Rajaharia tried to report it responsibly, he could not find any listed channel to contact the company directly.

**What would have stopped it.** No unauthenticated endpoint returning user data in the first place (AUTH-03), an asset inventory that surfaces forgotten, unowned endpoints for removal rather than leaving them live indefinitely (CLOUD-09), and a published vulnerability-disclosure channel so a researcher who finds this has somewhere to report it besides a personal social media post (OBSV-06).

scale: 1565338 people
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [CLOUD-01, HUMAN-10, DATA-09]
sources:
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/may-2019-healthcare-data-breach-report/
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/mailing-error-sees-inmediata-breach-notification-letters-sent-to-incorrect-addresses/
```

**What happened.** Inmediata Health Group, a healthcare clearinghouse that processes claims and eligibility data on behalf of providers and insurers, discovered in January 2019 that an internal web page had been misconfigured to allow search-engine indexing, making patient information reachable to anyone online. Inmediata began mailing notification letters to affected individuals on April 22, 2019 — and a second failure then compounded the first: an unknown number of those letters were addressed to the wrong person at the intended recipient's address, some using years-old maiden names or details tied to a healthcare encounter a quarter-century earlier.

**Entry.** `entry/misconfiguration` — a web page meant to be reachable only from inside Inmediata's own network was instead left open to the public internet and indexed by search engines, so anyone with the right search terms could reach it directly with no credential of any kind.

**Escalation.** None required — the misconfigured page served the data directly; forensic investigators found no evidence the page had actually been visited by anyone unauthorized during the exposure window, though they could not rule it out either.

**Blast radius.** 1,565,338 individuals were named as affected, the largest healthcare breach reported that month; exposed data could include patient names, addresses, dates of birth, gender, treating doctors, medical claim information, and — for a smaller subset — Social Security numbers. Because Inmediata's own address records were stale for an unknown share of those 1.5 million people, some notification letters never reached the person they were actually about, leaving those individuals with no way to know their data had been exposed at all.

**Human factor.** The original exposure was a configuration a person set wrong and nobody caught before it went live; the notification mailing that followed compounded it by sending personal breach details to whoever now lived at an old address rather than the actual patient — sending sensitive information to the wrong recipient, at the exact moment the process was supposed to be making things right.

**What would have stopped it.** Nothing publicly reachable unless it was deliberately decided to be, checked on a regular cadence rather than discovered by accident (CLOUD-01), confirming a recipient and destination address before sensitive notification mail goes out rather than trusting whatever is on file (HUMAN-10), and enforced data-retention practices that catch and refresh stale contact records instead of mailing to whoever they last belonged to (DATA-09).

```

**What happened.** Freedom Mobile, a Canadian wireless carrier, confirmed that customer data had been exposed through an Elasticsearch database managed on its behalf by a third-party vendor, Apptium Technologies. The database was reachable on the open internet with no authentication from roughly March 25 to April 23, 2019.

**Entry.** `entry/public-database` — the Elasticsearch instance Apptium ran required no login and was directly reachable over the internet. Researchers Noam Rotem and Ran Locar found it during routine scanning for exposed databases and notified Freedom Mobile on April 18, 2019.

The database was secured on April 23, 2019, five days after notification.

**Human factor.** Freedom Mobile handed customer support data to a vendor without independently verifying how that vendor's database was configured, and nobody at either company noticed it was internet-facing until outside researchers found it by scanning for exactly this kind of exposure.

**What would have stopped it.** Databases never reachable from the public internet in the first place (DATA-04), an inventory of every vendor holding customer data with a defined security standard for it (VENDOR-01), and time-boxed, separately monitored vendor access to that data rather than a one-time handoff (VENDOR-08).

  - https://statescoop.com/robinhood-ransomware-knocks-out-city-services-in-baltimore/
```

**What happened.** On May 7, 2019, Baltimore's city government network was hit with RobbinHood ransomware that encrypted files across roughly 10,000 computers, knocking out email, online bill payment, real estate settlements and other services for weeks. The city refused to pay the roughly 13-bitcoin (about $76,000) ransom demand and instead spent an estimated $18 million on recovery and lost or deferred revenue.

**Entry.** Never forensically confirmed in public reporting. It has been widely, though not conclusively, reported to have involved an exposed Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) service reachable from the internet on outdated systems (confidence: medium). An earlier, widely repeated claim that the attack used the NSA-linked EternalBlue exploit was directly investigated and debunked — independent malware analysis found no EternalBlue code in the ransomware binary.

**Escalation.** Once inside, the ransomware spread across roughly 10,000 machines with nothing to contain it, reaching systems across nearly every city department (`escalation/flat-network`); reaching that many hosts also required privileged, likely domain-level, credentials along the way (`escalation/privilege-escalation`).

**Blast radius.** About 10,000 of the city's computers were encrypted; core services were down for weeks to months. The city's own combined estimate of recovery spending and lost or deferred revenue was $18 million. In 2025, an Iranian national pleaded guilty in U.S. federal court to helping develop and deploy the RobbinHood ransomware used in the Baltimore attack and others.

**Human factor.** City IT had a documented history of underinvestment and had reportedly been warned about weak security practices before the attack. Whatever the exact entry point, it landed on a network with no segmentation to contain it.

**What would have stopped it.** No admin or remote-access interface exposed directly to the internet (CLOUD-08), phishing-resistant MFA on any credential with that reach (AUTH-01), and network segmentation so one foothold cannot reach 10,000 machines (CLOUD-05).

```

**What happened.** On May 7, 2019, cryptocurrency exchange Binance detected a single large withdrawal — ultimately totaling 7,000 bitcoin, worth about $40.7 million at the time — moving out through its hot wallet in one transaction. Binance froze all withdrawals for about a week while it investigated.

**Entry.** Binance's own account said attackers had obtained "a large number of user API keys, 2FA codes, and potentially other info" through phishing emails and malware planted on individual users' devices, then used those legitimately issued but stolen API keys to authorize withdrawals as if they were the account owners.

**Escalation.** None required — a stolen API key with withdrawal rights was already sufficient to move funds out of an account; no separate privilege escalation on Binance's own infrastructure was needed. Binance said the attackers had "the patience to wait" and coordinated the withdrawal across multiple seemingly independent accounts to time it past routine checks.

**Blast radius.** 7,000 BTC, roughly 2% of Binance's total bitcoin holdings at the time, left the exchange in a single transaction before it triggered an alarm. Binance covered the loss itself through its SAFU insurance fund, so no user funds were ultimately lost, but the theft came directly from compromised user accounts rather than any disclosed flaw in Binance's own core systems.

**Human factor.** A large enough number of individual users fell for phishing emails or picked up credential-stealing malware that the accumulated stolen API keys and 2FA codes were sufficient to empty a meaningful slice of the exchange's hot wallet in one pass — and the transaction was large and unusual enough that, in hindsight, it should have been flagged before it cleared rather than after.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA that can't be satisfied by a code a phishing page can also capture (AUTH-01), breached-credential and anomaly checks on every authentication and withdrawal surface (AUTH-06), and alerting tuned to abnormal withdrawal volume per account rather than only after the fact (OBSV-02).

  - https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/hack-japanese-retailer-exposes-460000-customer-accounts-a-12482
```

**What happened.** Between April 23 and May 10, 2019, an outside party ran a credential-stuffing campaign against the UNIQLO Japan and GU Japan online stores, operated by Japanese retailer Fast Retailing. The company said it identified the activity after "multiple customer reports of weird account activity" and disclosed the breach on May 14, 2019, confirming 461,091 accounts had been accessed.

**Entry.** `entry/credential-stuffing` — attackers used username-and-password pairs leaked from unrelated prior breaches, tested automatically against the Uniqlo and GU login forms; wherever a customer had reused a password from somewhere else, the attacker logged straight in.

**Escalation.** None required — each successful login was already full access to that customer's stored profile and order history; no further exploit was needed once a working credential pair was found.

**Blast radius.** 461,091 accounts across the two storefronts were accessed, exposing names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, gender, dates of birth, purchase history, clothing measurements, and the first four and last four digits of stored payment cards (full numbers and CVVs were not stored). Fast Retailing disabled the passwords on all affected accounts, emailed customers to reset them, and reported the incident to the Tokyo Metropolitan Police.

**Human factor.** Login on both storefronts relied on a password alone, so credential reuse by customers — combined with unrelated breaches elsewhere supplying the password lists — was sufficient on its own; Fast Retailing only learned of the campaign from customers noticing strange activity on their own accounts, not from its own monitoring catching the automated login attempts as they happened.

**What would have stopped it.** MFA on customer accounts so a correct password alone is not enough to log in (AUTH-01), rate limiting and breached-password screening at the login form to blunt automated credential stuffing (AUTH-06), and alerting on abnormal login volume or failure-then-success patterns per account rather than waiting on customer reports (OBSV-02).

controls: [CLOUD-06, CICD-02, AUTH-02]

  - https://stackoverflow.blog/2019/05/17/update-to-security-incident-may-17-2019/
  - https://stackoverflow.blog/2021/01/25/a-deeper-dive-into-our-may-2019-security-incident/
  - https://thehackernews.com/2019/05/stack-overflow-databreach.html
```

**What happened.** A build deployed to Stack Overflow's development tier on May 5, 2019 contained a bug that let an outside party log in to the development environment. Stack Overflow publicly disclosed the incident on May 17, 2019, initially describing exposure of data on roughly 250 public-site users; a more thorough investigation published in January 2021 revised this to 184 confirmed users (names, IP addresses, and email addresses) and additionally found that portions of Stack Overflow's own source code had been accessed.

**Entry.** `entry/auth-bypass` — the buggy development-tier build let the intruder authenticate into the development environment without valid credentials.

**Escalation.** The development and production tiers were not properly isolated: from the compromised development tier, the intruder was able to escalate their own access on the live, public production version of stackoverflow.com (`escalation/non-prod-to-prod`), granting themselves privileged production access on May 11.

**Blast radius.** Confirmed exposure, per the January 2021 follow-up, covered 184 users' names, IPs and emails plus internal source code; the original May 2019 disclosure had estimated roughly 250 affected users. The intruder had access from May 5 to May 11 — six days — before the privilege-escalation attempt was detected and access was revoked.

**Human factor.** A defect in a routine development-tier deployment was not caught before release, and the boundary between "development" and "production" was porous enough that a foothold in one became privileged access to the other — exactly the gap a non-production environment should never have.

**What would have stopped it.** Non-production environments held to production-grade authentication or given no path into production at all (CLOUD-06), required review before a build reaches even the development tier (CICD-02), and server-side, per-object authorization checks that don't trust which environment a request claims to come from (AUTH-02).

**What happened.** First American Financial Corporation, one of the largest U.S. title insurers, left a section of its website (firstam.com) exposing an estimated 885 million title and escrow document images to anyone with a web browser — no login required. The documents included bank account numbers and statements, mortgage and tax records, Social Security numbers, wire transaction receipts, and driver's license images, some dating back to 2003. Brian Krebs disclosed the exposure on May 24, 2019, after being alerted by a Washington-state real estate developer; First American disabled external access to the application the same day.

**Entry.** `entry/idor` — the canonical case in this corpus. Each document was served by a URL containing a sequential, nine-digit record number with no server-side check on whether the requester was authorized to view that specific document. Incrementing or decrementing the number in a legitimately received link returned a different customer's complete file.

**Escalation.** None required — the flaw gave direct, unauthenticated read access to every document in the store; no additional foothold or privilege was needed to move from one person's records to the next.

**Blast radius.** An estimated 885 million documents, spanning transactions from 2003 through May 2019, were reachable without authentication; the SEC's subsequent order independently corroborated an exposure of "over 800 million" images. First American has not confirmed how many documents were actually viewed by someone other than their intended recipient.

**Human factor.** Per the SEC's June 2021 order, First American's own information security team had identified this exact class of vulnerability in a penetration test months before Krebs' report, and it should have been remediated under company policy. It was not, and the executives responsible for the company's public disclosures were never told, so they had no way to factor it into what the company said publicly — a failure the SEC penalized specifically as a disclosure-controls violation, separate from the underlying technical flaw.

**What would have stopped it.** No unauthenticated endpoint returning another customer's document (AUTH-03), unguessable, non-sequential identifiers so incrementing a number never yields someone else's record (INPUT-09), and a working process for escalating a known, internally-flagged vulnerability to both a fix and the people who decide what gets disclosed (OBSV-06).

**What would have stopped it.** An inventory of every vendor holding sensitive data (VENDOR-01), contractual notification windows and active monitoring of vendor security posture rather than waiting to be told (VENDOR-06), and alerting on abnormal data access or export volume so eight months of unauthorized access does not go unnoticed (OBSV-02).

**What happened.** The Australian National University disclosed on June 4, 2019 that an intruder had accessed and copied significant volumes of staff, student and visitor data — some records up to 19 years old — after breaking into the university's Enterprise Systems Domain, which houses HR, finance, student administration and e-forms systems. ANU's own incident report traced the initial access back to November 2018; the intrusion was only found months later, during a dedicated threat-hunting exercise, with the breach itself confirmed around May 17, 2019.

The Australian Signals Directorate called it the work of "a sophisticated actor"; outside analysts speculated about a state-linked group, most often naming China, but ANU and the ASD stopped short of formal attribution, so this record uses `actor/unknown`.

**Human factor.** A single staff member previewing one email was enough to start the chain, and the intruder's path ran straight through a server the university already knew was old and scheduled for retirement rather than through anything current — the breach persisted for roughly six months of cumulative activity before routine security work happened to surface it.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA so a harvested password alone isn't enough to gain a foothold (AUTH-01), a patch-and-decommissioning SLA that doesn't leave a known-vulnerable, soon-to-be-retired server reachable in the meantime (CLOUD-10), and centralized logging and alerting on authentication and admin activity sensitive enough to catch lateral movement into HR and finance systems before it reaches them (OBSV-01).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **BlueKeep (CVE-2019-0708):** Microsoft disclosed this pre-authentication, wormable Remote Desktop Services vulnerability on May 14, 2019, and — in a rare move — shipped patches even for out-of-support Windows XP and Server 2003, explicitly warning the flaw could enable an attack "of a similar scale" to WannaCry and NotPetya if left unpatched. It is a direct answer to the exposed-RDP pattern behind Baltimore's ransomware entry this same quarter. ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BlueKeep))
- **Vendor and collections-agency scrutiny after AMCA:** Quest Diagnostics, LabCorp, and AMCA all faced class-action suits and congressional inquiry within days of disclosure, pushing renewed attention onto how little oversight healthcare providers had of the billing and collections vendors holding their patients' data.
- **Third-party remote-access risk after Wipro:** Incident responders and trade press (CSO Online, Flashpoint) used the Wipro breach to argue that large IT-outsourcer remote access into a client's network should be treated as a distinct, separately monitored risk category rather than implicitly trusted internal access.
- **Renewed attention to IDOR and broken object-level authorization:** First American's exposure became a widely cited teaching example for why every object-serving endpoint needs a server-side "is this requester allowed to see this specific record" check — unguessability of an identifier is not, by itself, an authorization control.
- **Independent researcher disclosure as a detection layer:** UpGuard's and vpnMentor's work this quarter (the Facebook-linked S3 buckets, Freedom Mobile's Elasticsearch instance) is a reminder that routine scanning for exposed cloud storage and databases by outside researchers was, in both cases, the only thing that found the exposure — neither affected company caught it internally.
- **Transparent post-incident correction:** Stack Overflow's detailed May 2019 write-up, and its unusual January 2021 follow-up that revised its own original victim-count estimate downward once forensics were complete, has since been cited as a model for disclosure that is willing to correct itself rather than let an early estimate stand uncorrected.
