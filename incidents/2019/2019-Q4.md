# 2019 Q4 — Stale Access and Stolen Passwords

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/credential-stuffing` — reused and infostealer-harvested passwords, not novel exploits, opened Disney+ accounts and bank-aggregator-linked accounts, and a forgotten VPN profile without MFA gave attackers a path into Avast's network.
> - **New technique this quarter:** Ransomware operators increasingly aimed at managed service providers to reach many downstream victims through one compromise — VCPI's breach cut off patient-record access at 110 nursing homes it hosted for, none of which were touched directly.
> - **Recurring failure:** Long or unverified eradication — Imperva, VCPI, and Wawa each had compromises running undetected for months, and National Veterinary Associates was hit by the same ransomware family twice in one year after its first infection was never confirmed fully removed.
> - **Records in this file:** 22


**What happened.** Krystal, the Southern US fast-food chain, notified customers in late October 2019 that point-of-sale malware had been capturing card data from its payment terminals between roughly July and September 2019. The disclosure surfaced publicly alongside reporting that a batch of roughly 4 million stolen cards tied to breaches at Krystal and three separately-disclosed Focus Brands chains (Moe's, McAlister's Deli, and Schlotzsky's, notified in August 2019) had gone up for sale on a carding market — evidence the same buyers were fencing cards from more than one chain, not proof of a shared cause.

**Entry.** Krystal has not disclosed how the malware first reached its point-of-sale systems.

**Escalation.** The malware reached "nearly 50%" of the combined 1,750-plus locations across the affected chains, which is only possible through movement from an initial foothold across a shared corporate or franchise network rather than a single register being compromised.

**Blast radius.** The 4-million-card figure reported by Krebs spans all four restaurant chains together; Krystal's individual share was not broken out and no total specific to Krystal was independently confirmed.

**Human factor.** Malware sat on payment terminals for roughly two to three months before anyone caught it — long enough to harvest a large batch of cards before the chain's own monitoring, or a card network's fraud detection, caught the pattern.

**What would have stopped it.** Network segmentation between payment terminals and the rest of the corporate network so malware can't move from one to the other (CLOUD-05), and alerting tuned to unusual card-data read volume at the point of sale (OBSV-02).

**What happened.** In late October 2019, reports surfaced that malware — later identified as DTrack, a tool researchers have linked to North Korea's Lazarus Group — had been found on the network of India's Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant. NPCIL initially called the reports "false information" before confirming on October 30, 2019 that malware had indeed been identified, but on the plant's internet-connected administrative network rather than the isolated operational network that controls the reactors.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed data theft, and no impact on plant safety or operational systems, was ever reported. The intrusion's significance was that a nuclear operator's own network carried live malware for an unknown period before outside security researchers' public reporting forced NPCIL to investigate and then correct its own initial denial.

**Human factor.** NPCIL's first public response was to deny an attack had occurred, then reverse itself within two days once the malware could no longer credibly be disputed — a sequence that cost public trust it did not need to spend, and one only avoided by outside reporting rather than by NPCIL's own detection.

**What would have stopped it.** The segmentation between administrative and operational networks is exactly the control that kept this from becoming worse, and is worth naming as what worked (CLOUD-05); what didn't work was catching the intrusion internally rather than through outside reporting, which argues for detection that is itself monitored (OBSV-03) and a disclosure process that leads with confirmed fact rather than denial (OBSV-05).

```yaml
  - https://www.occrp.org/en/news/russia-huge-data-leak-hits-sberbank
```

**What happened.** Sberbank, Russia's largest bank, disclosed on October 3, 2019 that customer credit card data had leaked after a seller began offering a database on a cybercrime forum, claiming to hold records on up to 60 million cardholders. Sberbank's own security service traced the leak to an employee — described as heading one of the bank's divisions — who had legitimate access to the data as part of his job and had tried to sell it for personal gain; forensic analysts who bought a sample from the seller confirmed 200 records as genuine.

**Entry.** No technical entry was needed: the employee already had authorized access to the customer database he later sold from, which Sberbank said was why the leak could not have come from "external penetration" — its systems were isolated from the internet.

**Escalation.** None required — the access that let him view the data for his job was the same access he used to copy and sell it.

**Blast radius.** Sberbank confirmed only 200 records as authentic and said the leaked fields (personal details, employment history, card numbers) excluded CVV codes and could not alone be used to move money, since SMS verification is required for online purchases; a security researcher publicly disputed that reassurance, warning the data was still usable for targeted phone-fraud schemes. The seller's claim of a 60-million-record database was never independently verified.

**Human factor.** A single employee with standing access to card-holder data chose to monetize it, and the leak was only caught once a sample went up for sale on a criminal forum — not through any internal control watching what a privileged employee does with the data they're trusted to see.

**What would have stopped it.** Insider risk monitored by behavior rather than by trust in an employee's role (HUMAN-09), bulk data access and export treated as a privileged, logged, alerting action rather than an invisible part of a manager's daily job (DATA-11), and alerting tuned to abnormal read or export volume per account so a division head pulling unusual quantities of customer records stands out before it reaches a forum (OBSV-02).


**What happened.** Imperva disclosed on October 10, 2019 that email addresses, hashed and salted passwords, API keys, and SSL certificates belonging to some Cloud WAF (formerly Incapsula) customers had been accessed by an outside party. CTO Kunal Anand said "unauthorized use of an administrative API key in one of its production AWS accounts had occurred in October 2018" — roughly ten months before Imperva found it, on August 20, 2019.

**Entry.** An internal compute instance, created for testing during a 2017 AWS migration and left reachable from outside the network, was never decommissioned and carried a live administrative AWS API key.

**Escalation.** Whoever compromised that instance found the API key sitting on it and used it directly to pull an administrative database snapshot — no further privilege escalation was needed once the key was in hand.

**Blast radius.** Imperva has not disclosed a total victim count. In response it forced roughly 13,000 password resets, rotated about 13,500 SSL certificates, and regenerated around 1,400 API keys for affected accounts; CEO Chris Hylen resigned days after the disclosure, which he said was a personal decision unrelated to the incident.

**Human factor.** A test artifact from a two-year-old cloud migration was left running with production-equivalent access and a credential with no expiry, and no inventory process caught it before an outsider did.

**What would have stopped it.** An asset inventory that finds forgotten, externally reachable infrastructure (CLOUD-09), credentials scoped to a single purpose rather than broad administrative reach (CRED-06), and API keys that expire rather than persist indefinitely (CRED-07).

**What happened.** Avast disclosed in October 2019 that an attacker had reached its internal network through a "phantom" VPN account — one that had mistakenly been left active without requiring two-factor authentication. The target was infrastructure connected to CCleaner, the product whose build chain had been compromised in 2018; Avast said customers remained "protected and unaffected" once it acted.

**What would have stopped it.** Access that is inventoried, owned, and expires automatically when unused (AUTH-08), phishing-resistant MFA required on every account with network reach (AUTH-01), and credentials that expire rather than persist indefinitely (CRED-07).

**What happened.** NordVPN confirmed on October 21, 2019 that one of its rented servers in Finland had been breached between January 31 and March 20, 2018, through a remote-management system the datacenter provider had installed without NordVPN's knowledge. The intruder obtained that single server's TLS private key; NordVPN said it found no evidence of an actual man-in-the-middle attack and that no user credentials or activity logs were on the machine.

**Entry.** The datacenter operator NordVPN rented hardware from had left an insecure remote-administration tool enabled on the server — infrastructure NordVPN itself did not manage or know about.

**Human factor.** NordVPN extended trust to a datacenter vendor's remote-access practices without auditing them, then took roughly six months after learning of a confirmed breach to disclose it publicly.

**What would have stopped it.** Vendor access that is time-boxed and independently monitored rather than trusted by default (VENDOR-08), an inventory of every third party holding infrastructure access (VENDOR-01), and a written incident plan with a stated disclosure clock instead of an open-ended internal review (OBSV-05).

**What happened.** On October 24, 2019, a group calling itself the Shadow Kill Hackers breached systems belonging to the City of Johannesburg, South Africa's largest metro, and threatened to publish residents' finance and population data unless paid 4 bitcoin (about $30,000) by October 28. The city refused, shut down its website, e-services platform, and SAP-based billing and CRM systems as a precaution, and reopened call centers to keep serving residents manually.

**Human factor.** A near-identical attack had already hit an affiliated city entity that same year, and the October intrusion still reached multiple city systems three months later — evidence that the lesson from City Power's July incident was not applied city-wide before Shadow Kill Hackers found their own way in.

**What would have stopped it.** A post-incident review after the City Power attack that produced a citywide control change rather than a single-entity fix (OBSV-09), network segmentation between the public-facing website, e-services, and internal billing systems so one foothold can't reach all three (CLOUD-05), and backups proven restorable so a refusal to pay doesn't turn into an extended outage (DATA-08).

**What happened.** WhatsApp sued Israeli spyware vendor NSO Group in a California federal court on October 29, 2019, alleging that NSO's Pegasus spyware had been used to target approximately 1,400 WhatsApp accounts belonging to journalists, human rights defenders, diplomats, and other officials across at least 20 countries, in a campaign WhatsApp traced to around spring 2019. WhatsApp said it had already patched the underlying flaw and notified affected users directly.

**Entry.** The mechanism required no action from the victim at all: a flaw in WhatsApp's voice-call handling let a specially crafted call — including ones the target never answered — trigger a buffer overflow that gave the caller's infrastructure code execution on the victim's phone, after which Pegasus could be silently installed.

Once installed, Pegasus is designed to read messages, listen to calls, and track location, turning an infected phone into a full surveillance device.

**Human factor.** WhatsApp's own investigation found the flaw only after it had already been exploited against real targets for a period of weeks to months — a zero-day is not itself a human failure, but the gap between exploitation beginning and WhatsApp's detection of it is exactly the kind of dwell time worth tracking, even for a platform with WhatsApp's resources.

**What would have stopped it.** Rigorous, ongoing memory-safety testing of any code path that parses data arriving before a call is even answered, since that is exactly the kind of surface a zero-click exploit needs (INPUT-NEW: fuzz-test and memory-harden any parser reachable pre-authentication), and detection capable of catching exploitation of a live vulnerability from telemetry rather than relying on the flaw eventually being reported from outside (OBSV-03).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **AWS shipped IMDSv2 (November 19, 2019):** a session-oriented, token-gated method for requesting EC2 instance metadata that closes off the open-firewall, misconfigured-reverse-proxy, and SSRF paths attackers had been using to steal instance credentials from the metadata endpoint; IMDSv1 remained available alongside it rather than being removed. (Imperva above shows what a leftover cloud credential can cost even when SSRF isn't the vector.)
- **NordVPN's post-breach hardening:** a week after going public with its 2018 server breach, NordVPN announced on October 29, 2019 that it would commission additional independent security audits and launch a public bug bounty program, which went live in December 2019.
- **California's CCPA rulemaking advanced toward its January 1, 2020 effective date:** the state Attorney General published draft implementing regulations in the California Regulatory Notice Register on October 11, 2019, the same day Governor Newsom signed five amendments to the underlying law — pushing companies nationally to finish data inventories and consumer-rights handling before year-end.
- **Containment-by-disconnection as an emergency pattern:** NCR's decision to sever Mint's and QuickBooks' aggregator access to its Digital Insight platform rather than wait for a fix illustrates a blunt but fast containment option for credential-based account-takeover waves.
- **Two different single-point-of-failure patterns in healthcare-adjacent IT surfaced the same month:** VCPI's compromise reached 110 nursing homes through one shared MSP, while National Veterinary Associates was hit by the same Ryuk ransomware family twice in one year after its first infection was never confirmed fully evicted — together an argument for verifying eradication, not just restoring from backup, after any ransomware incident.
