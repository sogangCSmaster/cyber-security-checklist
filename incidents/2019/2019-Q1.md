# 2019 Q1 — Phishing, Misconfiguration, and a Trojanized Update

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/phishing — the quarter's two costliest incidents, UConn Health and Norsk Hydro, both trace back to a deceptive email reaching an employee inbox rather than to exploited code (Hydro's case specifically spear-phishing).
> - **New technique this quarter:** Operation ShadowHammer showed that a legitimate, validly-signed vendor update channel could be weaponized at scale while a hardcoded MAC-address allowlist kept the payload dormant for nearly everyone who received it.
> - **Recurring failure:** backups, business units, and mailboxes kept reachable from wherever an attacker first landed turned single footholds into organization-wide loss — from VFEmail's destroyed backups to Hydro's ransomware spreading across most business areas.
> - **Records in this file:** 9

## Incidents

```yaml
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/more-than-600000-michigan-residents-affected-by-wolverine-solutions-breach-warns-ag-nessel/
```

**What happened.** Wolverine Solutions Group, a Detroit-area print-and-mail vendor that produces benefit statements and other mailings for health plans, was hit by ransomware that encrypted files across its servers and workstations on September 23, 2018. Because Wolverine processed member data on behalf of Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan, Molina Healthcare, and other health plans, the attack became a breach for every one of its healthcare clients at once. Notifications went out in stages between December 2018 and March 2019 as each client determined what Wolverine had held on their members; Michigan's attorney general eventually put the total at more than 600,000 residents.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — Wolverine's breach notice describes the ransomware's effect (encryption of servers and workstations) but neither Wolverine nor its client-facing notifications ever named an initial access technique.

**Escalation.** `escalation/lateral-movement` — the ransomware reached "servers and workstations," meaning it moved beyond whatever system it first touched to encrypt shared infrastructure company-wide, consistent with a single compromised host pivoting across an unsegmented internal network rather than staying contained to one machine.

**Blast radius.** Demographic data, health plan contract numbers, and a limited amount of health and Social Security information belonging to over 600,000 people across multiple health plans' members. A forensic firm found no evidence of data exfiltration but could not rule it out.

**Human factor.** Every affected health plan had extended a mailing vendor access to member PHI as a routine, low-scrutiny business relationship, without the same security expectations — or visibility into Wolverine's own defenses — that they applied to their own systems.

**What would have stopped it.** A current inventory of what data each subprocessor holds and why (VENDOR-01); contractual security requirements and active monitoring of vendors handling PHI rather than waiting to be told of a problem (VENDOR-06); the same access controls for contractors as for employees (HUMAN-04); and minimizing how long a mailing vendor needs to retain full member records after a job completes (DATA-09).

### I2019Q1-02 · Fortnite account takeover vulnerability

```yaml

factor: [factor/legacy-debt, factor/process-failure]

scale: undisclosed
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [INPUT-01, INPUT-03, INPUT-08, AUTH-04]
sources:
  - https://research.checkpoint.com/2019/hacking-fortnite/
```

**What happened.** Check Point Research found that Fortnite's roughly 200 million accounts could be taken over with no password, using a chain of bugs on an old Epic Games subdomain left over from the 2004-era Unreal Tournament stats site. A crafted link, if a target clicked it, would hand the attacker the victim's Epic single sign-on token.

**Entry.** `entry/xss` — the subdomain `ut2004stats.epicgames.com` was vulnerable to SQL injection (bypassing its WAF via MySQL system variables) and reflected its "maps" search field into the page unsanitized, a classic cross-site scripting hole. Epic's SSO flow also failed to validate its `redirectUrl` parameter, so a login attempt could be pointed at that vulnerable subdomain.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — redirecting a victim's login through the XSS page let the attacker capture the authentication token directly from the SSO redirect, giving full account access in one step with no further privilege escalation needed.

**Blast radius.** Check Point demonstrated, but did not exploit, account takeover, access to stored personal information, and the ability to make purchases using saved payment methods, plus eavesdropping through in-game voice and text chat. Epic Games patched the flaw after coordinated disclosure; no evidence of in-the-wild exploitation was reported.

**Human factor.** A legacy subdomain from a game with no current support was left reachable on Epic's production infrastructure, still running old, unreviewed code, because nobody had gone back to decommission it once its original purpose ended.

**What would have stopped it.** Parameterized queries on every endpoint, including inactive ones (INPUT-01); output encoding so reflected input can't execute as script (INPUT-03); an allowlist on OAuth/SSO redirect targets so a login flow can't be pointed at an arbitrary subdomain (INPUT-08); and short-lived, revocable session tokens to limit what a stolen token is worth (AUTH-04).

```yaml
impact: [impact/wiper]

confidence: high
controls: [DATA-08, CLOUD-03, CRED-06]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2019/02/email-provider-vfemail-suffers-catastrophic-hack/
```

**What happened.** An unknown attacker deleted virtual machine disks and configuration across VFEmail's entire US-based hosting infrastructure — not just live mail stores but the offsite backup systems too — in what founder Rick Romero called "catastrophic destruction." No ransom was demanded; the intent appeared to be pure destruction of up to 18 years of customer email archives.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — never publicly identified. VFEmail's founder said the attacker had reached the virtual-machine management layer itself, but neither the initial foothold nor how it was obtained was disclosed.

**Escalation.** `escalation/flat-network` — no further pivot was reported beyond reaching that management layer, but the same access that reached production also reached the backup infrastructure, which should have been an isolated, independent system. That backups were destroyed alongside primary data in the same event is itself the finding.

**Blast radius.** All primary mail stores and backup systems in the company's US infrastructure were wiped; Romero said recovery might not be possible and the business's survival was in doubt. No user count was publicly disclosed.

**Human factor.** Whoever or whatever held credentials to the VM management plane could reach both production and backup storage from the same place, a single point of failure nobody had tested until it was exploited.

**What would have stopped it.** Backups that exist on infrastructure production credentials cannot write to, and that are restore-tested rather than merely present (DATA-08); least-privilege separation between management-plane access and the systems it can reach (CLOUD-03); and credentials scoped to one job and one resource rather than one identity that can touch everything (CRED-06).

```yaml

entry: [entry/misconfiguration]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/pii, impact/phi]
factor: [factor/human-error, factor/misconfiguration]
scale: 974000 people
cost_usd: 1000000
dwell_days: 22
confidence: high
controls: [CLOUD-01, HUMAN-07, CLOUD-09, OBSV-06]
sources:
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/phi-of-almost-1-million-uw-medicine-patients-exposed-online/
```

**What happened.** A misconfigured database server made internal UW Medicine files — including patient names, medical record numbers, and details of who their information had been disclosed to and why — readable over the open internet and indexed by Google for three weeks. A patient who searched their own name found the exposure and alerted UW Medicine.

**Entry.** `entry/misconfiguration` — a security control on the database server was disabled on December 4, 2018 while a developer was troubleshooting an unrelated error, and was never re-enabled. The files were then reachable by anyone, and search engine crawlers found and indexed them.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — once the control was off, the files were directly readable with no authentication and no further step needed.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 974,000 patients' data was exposed for 22 days (December 4–26, 2018). No Social Security numbers, financial data, or detailed clinical records were included, but some entries indicated what condition a patient was being tested for. UW Medicine's notification mailing alone cost roughly $1 million.

**Human factor.** A developer disabled a security control to work around an error and had no checklist or second reviewer to catch that it was never turned back on — a routine troubleshooting step became a three-week public exposure because nothing forced a review before or after.

**What would have stopped it.** Regularly enumerating what's actually reachable from the internet rather than assuming it matches what's intended (CLOUD-01); a checklist for sensitive configuration changes so a temporary fix can't quietly become permanent (HUMAN-07); an inventory that catches forgotten or drifted assets (CLOUD-09); and a visible, published way for someone like the patient who found this to report it faster (OBSV-06).

```yaml
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/class-action-lawsuit-filed-over-uconn-health-phishing-attack/
```

**What happened.** A phishing campaign compromised multiple UConn Health employee email accounts, discovered December 24, 2018. About 1,500 patients had names, addresses, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, and medical information exposed within those mailboxes; the wider notified population, whose more limited information appeared in the same accounts, reached roughly 326,000.

**Entry.** `entry/phishing` — employees were deceived into giving up their email credentials through phishing messages; UConn Health did not publicly detail the specific lure.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — the compromised mailboxes themselves contained the patient data, so reading the mail was the entire breach.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 326,000 patients notified, with about 1,500 of them exposed to full identity-theft-relevant data including Social Security numbers. A class-action lawsuit followed in March 2019 alleging inadequate safeguards and delayed notification.

**Human factor.** Employees with mailbox access to patient information were targeted with deceptive email and had no second authentication factor standing between a mistaken click and full account access.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on every account that can reach a mailbox with patient data (AUTH-01); training that reflects how phishing actually arrives today rather than a generic annual module (HUMAN-05); and centralized logging of authentication activity so a cluster of suspicious logins across several accounts is visible quickly (OBSV-01).

```yaml
scale: undisclosed

dwell_days: 146
confidence: medium
controls: [AUTH-01, AUTH-06, OBSV-01]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2020/02/hackers-were-inside-citrix-for-five-months/
```

**What happened.** The FBI notified Citrix in early March 2019 that intruders had been inside its internal network. A later review found the access had actually begun October 13, 2018 and continued intermittently until March 8, 2019 — nearly five months. Security firm Resecurity attributed the intrusion to an Iranian state-linked group it tracks as IRIDIUM; Citrix did not independently confirm that attribution, so it is recorded here as one vendor's claim rather than settled fact.

**Entry.** `entry/password-spray` — a small number of common passwords tried against many employee accounts, rather than many passwords against one, against accounts with no additional authentication factor.

**Escalation.** `escalation/lateral-movement` — with "intermittent access" sustained for months, the intruders moved through Citrix's internal network well beyond whatever account was first sprayed successfully, reaching business documents and files containing employee, contractor, intern, job-candidate, and dependent data.

**Blast radius.** Social Security and tax ID numbers, driver's license and passport numbers, financial account and payment card numbers, and limited health insurance information tied to Citrix's workforce and applicant pool; Citrix's notification did not give an exact victim count. Citrix said it found no evidence attackers remained in its systems once discovered.

**Human factor.** Employee accounts with a path into Citrix's internal network relied on passwords alone, so a small set of commonly reused passwords across a large employee base was enough to get a foothold that then went unnoticed for months.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on every account with internal network reach — notably absent at a company that sells remote-access security products to others (AUTH-01); rate limiting, lockout, and breached-password screening on authentication surfaces to blunt spraying before it succeeds (AUTH-06); and centralized logging of authentication failures so a spray attempt is visible before it becomes five months of standing access (OBSV-01).


**What happened.** Norwegian aluminium and energy producer Norsk Hydro was hit by LockerGoga ransomware in the early hours of March 19, 2019 (CET), compromising IT systems across most of the company's business areas and forcing plants into manual operation. Hydro chose to publish frequent, detailed public updates throughout the response rather than go quiet, which became a widely cited example of transparent ransomware incident communication.

**Entry.** `entry/spear-phishing` — Norwegian and international reporting attributed initial access to a spear-phishing email that reached an employee some time before the ransomware was triggered (confidence: medium — Hydro's own public statements described the attack's onset without naming a specific initial vector, so this detail rests on broader press attribution rather than the company's confirmation).

**Escalation.** `escalation/lateral-movement`, `escalation/flat-network` — the ransomware reached "most of the company's business areas" from a single foothold, consistent with widely reported accounts of attackers using compromised domain credentials to push LockerGoga broadly rather than the malware staying contained to one segment.

**Blast radius.** Aluminium extrusion and other plants worldwide were forced into manual and semi-manual operation for days to weeks. Hydro's own later quarterly reporting described a financial impact in the tens of millions of dollars, though this record does not restate an exact figure that could not be independently re-confirmed against a primary source here, so cost is recorded as undisclosed rather than an unverified number. Hydro stated it would not pay the ransom and restored operations from backups.

**Human factor.** One employee's interaction with a deceptive email, combined with a network where compromised credentials could reach systems across most business areas, turned a single email into a company-wide operational shutdown.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on the accounts that ultimately carried broad reach (AUTH-01); network segmentation so credential compromise in one business area cannot deploy ransomware in all of them (CLOUD-05); backups that are restore-tested and isolated from production credentials, which is what let Hydro recover without paying (DATA-08); and phishing training reflecting current lure techniques (HUMAN-05).

```yaml
factor: [factor/no-encryption, factor/process-failure, factor/human-error]
scale: 200000000-600000000 people

dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [OBSV-04, CRED-01, HUMAN-06]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2019/03/facebook-stored-hundreds-of-millions-of-user-passwords-in-plain-text-for-years/
```

**What happened.** Facebook disclosed that internal applications, built by its own engineers over several years, had been logging users' plaintext passwords to internal servers — searchable in plain text by more than 20,000 Facebook employees, with roughly 9 million internal queries logged against that data. Archives of the exposed data went back to 2012. Facebook said its security team found the practice during a routine review in January 2019.

**Entry.** `entry/secrets-in-config` — no external attacker was involved. Application code written over the years logged authentication data that should never have reached a log at all, matching a credential sitting in a reachable log or support artifact rather than being stolen through an attack.

**Escalation.** `escalation/none-required` — once passwords were logged in plaintext, any of the roughly 20,000 employees with access to those internal systems could search them directly.

**Blast radius.** Facebook said "hundreds of millions" of Facebook Lite users, "tens of millions" of other Facebook users, and "tens of thousands" of Instagram users had passwords logged this way; public accounts of the total have ranged from roughly 200 million to 600 million, and Facebook never published one precise figure, so this record gives the range rather than a disputed single number. Facebook said it found no evidence of internal abuse or external access to the logs and did not force a password reset.

**Human factor.** Engineers building internal diagnostic and debugging tools wrote authentication data into logs without anyone catching it in code review, and no automated safeguard caught it either, across multiple separate internal applications over roughly seven years.

**What would have stopped it.** Logging systems that structurally cannot capture secrets, tokens, or full identifiers, rather than relying on developers to remember not to log them (OBSV-04); treating a password field as a secret that must never reach source, config, or logs at any layer (CRED-01); and a culture where the engineers who eventually found and reported this internally faced no penalty for surfacing it, since punishing self-reported mistakes only teaches people to stop reporting them (HUMAN-06).

### I2019Q1-09 · Operation ShadowHammer (ASUS Live Update supply-chain attack)

```yaml

cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [CICD-03, CICD-08, DEPS-10]
sources:
  - https://securelist.com/operation-shadowhammer/89992/
```

**What happened.** Attackers compromised ASUS's own software update infrastructure and used it to distribute a trojanized version of the ASUS Live Update Utility, signed with genuine ASUS code-signing certificates, to ASUS computers worldwide. Kaspersky discovered the campaign in January 2019 and disclosed it publicly on March 25, 2019, naming it Operation ShadowHammer and attributing it to the BARIUM group, previously linked to the Winnti/ShadowPad umbrella of supply-chain intrusions.

**Entry.** `entry/supply-chain-update` — the vehicle was ASUS's own legitimate, automatic update channel, a mechanism users and endpoint security products alike are built to trust because it carries a valid vendor signature.

**Escalation.** `escalation/signed-artifact` — a validly signed update from ASUS's own servers bypassed the checks that would normally flag unknown or unsigned code, walking straight past the assumption that a signed binary from the vendor's own channel is safe; no further escalation was needed.

**Blast radius.** Kaspersky recorded at least 57,000 of its own users downloading and installing the backdoored utility and estimated the true worldwide total, across all security vendors, likely exceeded a million devices — an estimate the researchers themselves described as approximate. The backdoor checked each machine's network adapter MAC address against a hardcoded list of roughly 600 specific targets; only those machines were meant to receive a further payload, and what happened on those specific machines was never confirmed, so overall impact beyond the backdoor's presence is recorded as unconfirmed rather than assumed.

**Human factor.** ASUS's build and update infrastructure was treated as internal tooling rather than as production infrastructure worth monitoring at the same level as customer-facing systems, letting attackers operate inside the signing and distribution pipeline for months before anyone at ASUS or a security vendor noticed.

**What would have stopped it.** Build provenance and artifact signing that verifies what actually shipped matches what was built, not just that a valid signature exists (CICD-03); monitoring and treating the build/update system itself as production (CICD-08); and a rehearsed response plan for the moment a vendor an organization depends on turns out to be compromised (DEPS-10).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **Password reuse under renewed scrutiny:** Troy Hunt's January 2019 disclosure of the 2.7-billion-record "Collection #1–5" credential compilations drove a visible surge in adoption of Have I Been Pwned's k-anonymity Pwned Passwords API and renewed mainstream advice to use unique, managed passwords rather than memorized, reused ones.
- **Supply-chain verification tooling:** Kaspersky published a free lookup tool alongside its Operation ShadowHammer research so ASUS device owners could check whether their MAC address matched the attackers' target list, and pressed the industry toward update mechanisms that verify build integrity, not just a valid signature.
- **Transparent ransomware communication as a model:** Norsk Hydro's choice to publish frequent, specific public updates throughout its LockerGoga recovery, rather than going silent, was widely held up afterward as an example for how to communicate during a live ransomware incident, and its recovery from backups without paying the ransom reinforced industry advice on backup testing and isolation.
- **MFA pressure on vendors that sell security themselves:** Citrix's own breach by unauthenticated password spraying, at a company whose business is remote-access security products, sharpened the argument that phishing-resistant MFA belongs on every account with internal reach, not only on products sold to customers.
- **Healthcare vendor and email-account scrutiny:** the Wolverine Solutions Group, UW Medicine, and UConn Health disclosures together kept HHS Office for Civil Rights attention focused on HIPAA Security Rule risk analyses — specifically business-associate oversight, internet-facing systems, and email-account access controls.
- **Responsible disclosure as the reference case:** Check Point Research's coordinated disclosure with Epic Games became a widely cited example of responsible-disclosure timing for account-takeover bugs, and highlighted the risk of legacy, unmaintained subdomains left reachable on production game-platform infrastructure.
