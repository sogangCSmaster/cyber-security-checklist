# 2019 Q3 — Cloud Credential Theft and Open Databases

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/public-database` — a third of this quarter's records are unsecured, unauthenticated databases reachable from the open internet (Suprema's BioStar 2 biometric platform, an unattributed cache of scraped Facebook phone numbers, Ecuador's Novaestrat leak). The single most consequential record is different in kind: `entry/ssrf`, Capital One's chain from a misconfigured WAF into AWS's instance metadata service.
> - **New technique this quarter:** none newly invented, but SSRF against cloud instance metadata went from a known technique to the industry's defining one — Capital One's breach is the case that made AWS ship IMDSv2 that November.
> - **Recurring failure:** organizations that already knew what had happened chose not to say so plainly. StockX called a forced password reset a "system update," Zynga never published its own player count, and Facebook dismissed a cache of 220–419 million of its users' phone numbers as merely "old."
> - **Records in this file:** 17

sources:
  - https://www.engadget.com/2019-07-06-7-eleven-japan-app-security-loss.html
  - https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2019/07/13/national/media-national/convenience-store-operator-struggles-wake-security-fiasco/
```

**What happened.** Seven & I Holdings launched the 7pay mobile payment app for Seven-Eleven Japan stores on July 1, 2019. Within two days, attackers were taking over customer accounts and charging fraudulent purchases; Seven & I suspended new registrations and then the whole service by July 3, and on July 4 said roughly 900 accounts had lost a combined ¥55 million (about $507,000). The service never reopened.

**Entry.** `entry/auth-bypass` — 7pay's password-reset form let anyone request a reset using any email address, not only the one on the account, and verified the requester only with the account holder's email, date of birth, and phone number. Users who had not set a birthday were defaulted to January 1, 2019, making that check trivial to pass. Reporters later found 7pay's own president was unfamiliar with two-factor authentication when asked why the app lacked it.

**Escalation.** None required — resetting the password handed over the whole account, including its linked payment methods, in one step.

**Blast radius.** ¥55 million (about $507,000) in fraudulent charges across roughly 900 accounts in the app's first two days. Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry found Seven & I had not followed its own security guidelines before launch; police later arrested two Chinese nationals for using stolen 7pay IDs and said an organized group was likely involved.

**Human factor.** A large retailer shipped a payment product whose account-recovery flow was weaker than its login, and whose own executive did not know what two-factor authentication was — a gap that had nothing to do with the sophistication of the eventual attack.

**What would have stopped it.** Account recovery held to the same strength as the login path itself, rather than a weaker side door (AUTH-05); rate limiting and lockout on password-reset attempts (AUTH-06); and a launch checklist for a payment product that would have caught the missing 2FA and the guessable identity check before release (HUMAN-07).

  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/la-county-health-services-thousands-of-patients-data-exposed-in-email-breach/
```

**What happened.** An employee of Nemadji Research Corporation, a contractor handling eligibility and billing for the Los Angeles County Department of Health Services, responded to a phishing email on March 28, 2019. Nemadji's IT staff noticed unusual activity in the mailbox the same day; the attacker had access for several hours. Los Angeles County notified 14,591 affected patients on July 8, 2019.

**Entry.** Ordinary credential phishing against a single contractor employee. The mailbox held patient data because that is where billing correspondence lives — the attacker never needed to reach a clinical system.

**Escalation.** None required. Names, addresses, dates of birth, medical record numbers and Medi-Cal identification numbers were already sitting in the compromised inbox and its attachments.

**Blast radius.** 14,591 patients of a county health system, notified by the county rather than by the contractor. No downstream fraud was publicly tied to the exposure. Detection was same-day, which is why this record's dwell time is zero and why the damage stayed proportionate to one mailbox.

**Human factor.** One person clicked one link at an organisation most of the affected patients had never heard of. The county's own controls were not the ones that mattered here; its contractor's were.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on the contractor's mail accounts (`AUTH-01`), an inventory making clear which third parties hold patient data and what they can reach (`VENDOR-01`), training reflecting how a stolen mailbox is mined for the records already inside it (`HUMAN-05`), and the authentication-anomaly logging that here worked — Nemadji caught it the same day (`OBSV-01`).

  - https://www.pymnts.com/cryptocurrency/2019/hackers-steal-32m-crypto-japanese-exchange/
```

**What happened.** BitPoint, a Tokyo-based cryptocurrency exchange, noticed an irregularity in its outgoing transfer system on the night of July 11, 2019 and halted trading the next day. Its hot wallets — internet-connected wallets used for active trading — had been drained of Bitcoin, Bitcoin Cash, Ether, and Litecoin; an initial estimate of $32.2 million (¥3.5 billion) was later revised to roughly $28 million (¥3.02 billion) as BitPoint traced the funds, with about $2.3 million subsequently recovered on other trading platforms. Cold, offline wallets were untouched.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — neither BitPoint nor independent reporting disclosed how the attacker obtained the access needed to move funds out of the hot wallets, so this record does not guess at a mechanism.

**Escalation.** None described — whatever access was obtained was sufficient on its own to initiate withdrawals from every affected hot wallet.

**Blast radius.** Roughly $28–32 million across four cryptocurrencies, affecting about 50,000 customers whose holdings made up roughly two-thirds of the loss (the remainder was BitPoint's own capital). BitPoint committed to reimbursing every affected customer in-kind at a 1:1 rate and resumed partial service on August 6, 2019. Coverage at the time noted this was the eighth notable cryptocurrency exchange theft of 2019.

**Human factor.** Not identified from public reporting — the entry mechanism itself was never disclosed, which is itself the finding: an exchange holding tens of millions of dollars of customer funds in an internet-connected wallet gave the public no account of what actually failed.

**What would have stopped it.** Capping how much value a single hot-wallet compromise can reach, keeping the bulk of holdings cold the way BitPoint's own untouched wallets show is possible (DATA-12); alerting on abnormal outbound transaction volume, which is ultimately how the theft was first noticed (OBSV-02); and signing keys and wallet credentials scoped tightly enough that no single compromise reaches the whole hot-wallet balance (CRED-06).

  - https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/bulgaria-fines-tax-office-penetration-testers-charged-a-13000
  - https://www.euronews.com/2019/07/17/bulgaria-s-biggest-leak-suspect-arrested-after-cyber-attack
```

**What happened.** On July 15, 2019, Bulgaria's National Revenue Agency confirmed a breach after a hacker emailed local media detailing the theft and published 57 folders of CSV files — about 11 GB — naming roughly 5 million Bulgarian citizens, close to the country's entire adult population. Records included revenue, tax and social-security payment history, health and pension data, an online-gambling user registry, and company records dating to 2007. The intrusion is believed to have occurred in late June 2019.

**Entry.** `entry/sqli` — the attacker reached the data through a SQL injection vulnerability in a rarely used VAT-refund service for international transactions, an endpoint whose obscurity had apparently kept it out of routine security review.

**Escalation.** None described — the vulnerable service's own database access was broad enough to return records well beyond VAT refunds, with no further pivot reported.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 5 million people's tax and identity records exposed; the hacker claimed as many as 110 folders and 21 GB existed in total, a figure the NRA did not confirm (`confidence: medium` reflects both the disputed total scope and unresolved attribution). Bulgarian authorities arrested a 20-year-old, Kristiyan Boykov, on July 16; he was released two days later after police said the breach had not reached the NRA's critical databases, and he denied involvement. Other reporting named individuals working for a Sofia-based security contractor, TAD Group, as later charged in connection with the breach, and BankInfoSecurity subsequently reported the NRA itself was fined by Bulgaria's data protection authority, though the confirmed amount is not established here; earlier reporting had put the agency's potential exposure at up to roughly €20 million.

**Human factor.** A national tax authority left a legacy, little-used online service without the input validation given to its primary systems, and the question of who was actually responsible — an outside attacker, or contracted security testers who exceeded their brief — was never fully resolved in public reporting.

**What would have stopped it.** Parameterized queries on every endpoint, including ones rarely used, not only the ones under active development (INPUT-01); default-deny, row-level authorization so a single vulnerable endpoint cannot return records outside its own purpose (DATA-01); and a published channel for reporting vulnerabilities so a security tester's findings reach the agency through disclosure rather than a mass email to the press (OBSV-06).

```

**What happened.** Starting with Monroe City Schools on July 8, 2019 and following with Sabine Parish, Morehouse Parish, and Ouachita Parish school systems through late July, ransomware separately hit at least four Louisiana public school districts within about three weeks of each other. On July 24, Governor John Bel Edwards declared a state of emergency — the first time Louisiana activated cybersecurity-specific emergency powers — freeing state police, the National Guard, and the Office of Technology Services to assist the districts and barring IT vendors from price-gouging them during the response.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — none of the affected districts or the state's emergency declaration disclosed how the ransomware first reached district networks, so this record does not guess at a mechanism.

**Escalation.** Not described in public reporting. Sabine Parish reported that the attack disabled "some of our technology systems and our central office phone system" together, consistent with phone and data systems sharing a network, though no district published the technical path from initial foothold to that outcome.

**Blast radius.** Monroe City described "disruption to its computer systems"; Sabine Parish lost some technology and phone systems; Morehouse Parish reported no major network disruption. No district published a ransom amount, a payment, or a recovery cost, and the FBI and third-party responders were brought in alongside the state's own cybersecurity staff.

**Human factor.** Four separate small public school systems, each too small to run a dedicated security team, were hit in close succession — the state's response leaned on centralizing expertise (the National Guard, state police, and a state technology office) precisely because no individual district had it on staff.

**What would have stopped it.** Backups that are restore-tested and isolated from production credentials, which is what determines whether a district pays a ransom or simply restores (DATA-08); a patch SLA for the aging, often donated or under-funded infrastructure small districts commonly run (CLOUD-10); and centralized logging of authentication and file-access anomalies that could catch ransomware staging before mass encryption, which none of the four districts' public statements described having (OBSV-01).

```

**What happened.** City Power, the municipally owned utility that distributes electricity across Johannesburg, South Africa, announced on July 25, 2019 that ransomware had encrypted its databases, applications, and parts of its network. Residents could not buy electricity through the prepaid vending system, the utility's website and fault-logging tool went down, and suppliers could not upload invoices, though City Power said the operational technology network controlling the actual power grid was not affected.

**Escalation.** Not described. The clean separation City Power reported between its compromised IT network and its untouched OT network controlling the grid itself suggests that separation held, whatever the initial foothold was.

Most core systems, including prepaid vending, were restored the same evening, which City Power credited to having recent backups; full restoration of supplier-facing systems was expected within another day.

**Human factor.** Not identified — the entry mechanism was never disclosed, though the fact that OT and IT stayed separated, and that backups existed and worked, both point to some prior investment in resilience even without a description of what let the ransomware in.

**What would have stopped it.** Backups that are restore-tested and isolated from production credentials, credited here with the fast recovery (DATA-08); centralized logging of authentication and access anomalies across the IT estate to catch a ransomware operator before mass encryption (OBSV-01); and a patch SLA for internet-facing systems at a utility, a common entry path even though never confirmed in this case (CLOUD-10).

### I2019Q3-07 · Capital One cloud data breach

```yaml
id: I2019Q3-07
name: Capital One cloud data breach
org: Capital One Financial Corporation
date_occurred: 2019-03-22
date_disclosed: 2019-07-29
region: United States, Canada
sector: [sector/finance]
actor: [actor/criminal]
entry: [entry/ssrf, entry/misconfiguration]
escalation: [escalation/overscoped-credential]
impact: [impact/pii, impact/financial-data]
factor: [factor/misconfiguration, factor/no-least-privilege, factor/detection-failure]
tech: [tech/aws, tech/s3]
scale: 106000000 people
cost_usd: 80000000
dwell_days: 117
confidence: high
controls: [CLOUD-02, CLOUD-03, INPUT-04]
sources:
  - https://www.capitalone.com/facts2019/
  - https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2020/nr-occ-2020-101.html
  - https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/pr/former-seattle-tech-worker-indicted-federal-charges-wire-fraud-and-computer-data-theft
```

**What happened.** Capital One disclosed on July 29, 2019 that a former Amazon Web Services engineer, Paige Thompson, had accessed and copied data on approximately 100 million people in the United States and 6 million in Canada — mostly applicants and customers of Capital One's credit card business from 2005 through early 2019. Thompson was arrested the same day after a tip that she had posted about the theft on GitHub and Slack; a federal grand jury later indicted her on wire fraud and computer fraud charges, and evidence showed she had used similar scanning techniques against more than 30 other organizations.

**Entry.** `entry/ssrf` — Thompson identified a misconfigured web application firewall in front of a Capital One web application and used it to issue a server-side request forgery request that reached AWS's EC2 instance metadata service (IMDSv1), which at the time answered any request from the instance itself with no additional authentication (`entry/misconfiguration` on the WAF that made the SSRF reachable in the first place).

**Escalation.** The metadata service returned temporary security credentials for the IAM role attached to the instance. That role was scoped far beyond the WAF's own needs — it could list and read a large number of Capital One's S3 buckets — so the single SSRF request yielded broad object-storage access without any further exploit (`escalation/overscoped-credential`).

**Blast radius.** Roughly 140,000 Social Security numbers, about 80,000 linked bank account numbers, approximately 1 million Canadian Social Insurance Numbers, and names, addresses, credit scores, credit limits, balances, and payment history for the ~106 million affected individuals, plus fragments of transaction data spanning 23 days between 2016 and 2018. Capital One stated no credit card numbers or account log-in credentials were compromised. The intrusion ran from March 22–23, 2019 until a tipster alerted Capital One around July 17, 2019 — roughly 117 days undetected internally. The OCC and Federal Reserve later fined Capital One a combined $80 million and imposed consent orders over the bank's cloud risk practices; Capital One separately agreed to a $190 million class-action settlement with affected customers.

**Human factor.** The OCC's order found Capital One had not established effective risk-assessment processes before migrating significant IT operations to the public cloud, and did not fix known deficiencies in a timely way. The intrusion was not found by any of Capital One's own controls — it surfaced only because an outside party saw the attacker's own public posts about it.

**What would have stopped it.** Instance metadata requiring a session token rather than answering any local request unauthenticated — the exact gap AWS closed with IMDSv2 (CLOUD-02), least-privilege IAM so a WAF's role cannot list buckets it has no reason to touch (CLOUD-03), and outbound requests from applications restricted to an allowlist that excludes the metadata address entirely (INPUT-04).

```

**What happened.** Renown Health, a nonprofit regional health system based in Reno, Nevada, reported to federal regulators in August 2019 that a portable storage device holding patient information had been lost, affecting 27,004 individuals. Neither the device type nor the circumstances of the loss were detailed in public summaries of the filing.

**Entry.** `entry/physical` — the exposure mechanism was the loss of a physical storage device itself, not a network intrusion; no attacker or exploitation is described.

  - https://www.idtheftcenter.org/post/stockx-data-breach-beware-of-forced-password-resets/
```

**What happened.** Sneaker and streetwear resale marketplace StockX had more than 6.8 million user records stolen in May 2019. Rather than disclose a breach, StockX emailed customers on August 1, 2019 citing a vague "system update" as the reason for a forced password reset; TechCrunch confirmed with a data broker that the records were for sale on the dark web and published on August 3, 2019, after which StockX admitted an intrusion had occurred.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — neither StockX nor independent reporting ever disclosed the specific technical vulnerability or credential that gave the attacker access to the user database, so this record does not guess at one.

**Escalation.** None described — the user database appears to have been reachable and exfiltrated as a single dataset, with no separate escalation step reported.

**Blast radius.** Names, email and shipping addresses, usernames, purchase history, and MD5-hashed (salted) passwords for more than 6.8 million accounts. StockX stated no payment card data was affected. The company never published its own confirmed count, leaving the 6.8 million figure sourced to the breach broker and TechCrunch's reporting.

**Human factor.** StockX chose language designed to avoid the word "breach" when it forced the password reset, denying its own users the ability to judge their exposure until outside reporting forced a fuller account — exactly the disclosure-failure pattern this corpus tracks separately from the technical entry vector. Passwords were also stored with MD5, an algorithm not designed to resist offline cracking at scale.

**What would have stopped it.** A written incident-communication plan that names who decides what customers are told and on what clock, rather than ad hoc language chosen to minimize alarm (OBSV-05), and password hashing with a modern, memory-hard algorithm instead of MD5 (DATA-06).

**What would have stopped it.** Databases never reachable from the public internet in the first place (DATA-04), biometric and credential fields encrypted at rest so an open index does not equal a readable one (DATA-07), and continuous enumeration of what the organization has exposed rather than waiting for a researcher's scan to find it (CLOUD-01).

**What happened.** In the early morning of August 16, 2019, ransomware hit 22 mostly small Texas city and county governments within hours of each other (initial press reports put the count at 23). Analysts called it the first time a ransomware operation had struck that many local governments simultaneously rather than one at a time. The Texas Department of Information Resources activated its State Operations Center and coordinated state and federal responders; by August 23 every affected entity had moved from response into recovery, and officials said no ransom was paid.

**Entry.** `entry/supply-chain-vendor` — the mayor of Keene, one of the affected cities, said attackers had compromised "IT software used by the city and managed by an outsourced company," and that the same vendor served many of the other targeted towns. Neither the vendor nor the specific software was ever named publicly, but the pattern — one shared IT services provider, many small-government clients — is what let a single intrusion reach 22 separate networks in one morning.

**Escalation.** `escalation/admin-tooling` — whatever access the attackers gained inside the shared vendor's environment appears to have doubled as the vendor's own path for managing each client's systems, so no further exploit was needed per town; the compromised management channel did the work of deployment itself.

**Blast radius.** Ransomware, attributed to the REvil (Sodinokibi) group, hit specific agencies within each of the 22 governments rather than entire networks, and attackers collectively demanded $2.5 million for decryption keys. No government paid; more than half had returned to normal operations within about three weeks of the attack.

**Human factor.** Twenty-two separate, mostly small local governments — the kind least likely to run their own IT departments — had each delegated core system management to the same outside vendor, with no visibility into how that vendor secured its own access into each of their networks.

**What would have stopped it.** An inventory naming every vendor holding standing remote access into government systems, and how many separate customers share that one channel (VENDOR-01); vendor remote access that is individually scoped, time-boxed, and monitored per client rather than one administrative path reaching every customer network at once (VENDOR-08); and segmentation ensuring a compromised management tool cannot reach production systems at every client simultaneously (CLOUD-05).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **AWS ships IMDSv2 (November 2019):** Directly in response to the technique behind Capital One's breach, AWS released a second version of the EC2 Instance Metadata Service that requires a session to be established with a PUT request and a secret token presented on every subsequent call, with the token's own network hop capped (TTL=1) so it cannot be relayed through an open proxy. AWS's own guidance frames this explicitly as closing the SSRF-to-credential-theft path.
- **UK ICO's GDPR intent-to-fine notices (July 8–9, 2019):** The Information Commissioner's Office announced its intention to fine British Airways £183 million and Marriott £99 million over their 2018 breaches — the largest proposed GDPR penalties to date at the time, and a signal that "notice of intent" fines calculated as a percentage of global turnover were no longer theoretical. Both final fines were substantially reduced in 2020 (BA to £20m, Marriott to £18.4m), a gap worth noting for anyone using the initial headline figures to model regulatory risk.
- **Cloud risk-assessment scrutiny of financial institutions:** Capital One's breach immediately drew Congressional and bank-regulator attention to how large financial institutions govern a migration to public cloud infrastructure, foreshadowing the formal OCC and Federal Reserve consent orders that followed in 2020.
- **Independent researcher scanning as a detection layer:** vpnMentor's sweeps this quarter (BioStar 2, the Ecuador leak) continue a pattern already visible in Q2 2019 — routine, unsolicited scanning for exposed Elasticsearch and similar databases by outside researchers found what the affected organizations' own monitoring did not.
- **Pushback on vague breach notification:** StockX's "system update" language and Zynga's uncounted disclosure drew specific criticism from identity-theft and security commentators this quarter for denying affected users the information needed to judge their own exposure, adding to the case for notification standards that require a stated scope rather than euphemism.
- **Renewed third-party risk attention after DoorDash:** Coverage of the DoorDash breach reinforced the same vendor-oversight gap already flagged around AMCA, Freedom Mobile, and Wipro in Q2 2019 — that data handed to a processor is only as secure as that processor's own, often invisible, configuration.
