# 2019 Q3 — Cloud Credential Theft and Open Databases

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** `entry/public-database` — a third of this quarter's records are unsecured, unauthenticated databases reachable from the open internet (Suprema's BioStar 2 biometric platform, an unattributed cache of scraped Facebook phone numbers, Ecuador's Novaestrat leak). The single most consequential record is different in kind: `entry/ssrf`, Capital One's chain from a misconfigured WAF into AWS's instance metadata service.
> - **New technique this quarter:** none newly invented, but SSRF against cloud instance metadata went from a known technique to the industry's defining one — Capital One's breach is the case that made AWS ship IMDSv2 that November.
> - **Recurring failure:** organizations that already knew what had happened chose not to say so plainly. StockX called a forced password reset a "system update," Zynga never published its own player count, and Facebook dismissed a cache of 220–419 million of its users' phone numbers as merely "old."
> - **Records in this file:** 9

**What happened.** Los Angeles County's Department of Health Services disclosed on July 9, 2019 that employee email accounts had been compromised by a phishing attack, exposing patients' personal and medical information. Initial reporting described "thousands" of affected patients; the county's later public tally, reported that same month, put the number above 200,000.

**Entry.** `entry/phishing` — county employees responded to phishing emails that harvested their county email credentials, giving the attacker direct access to the mailboxes rather than to any backend clinical system.

**Escalation.** None required — patient names, diagnoses, medical record numbers, and other treatment details already sat in the compromised employees' inboxes and attachments, so reading the mailboxes directly yielded the exposure.

**Blast radius.** More than 200,000 patients' personal and health information — names, dates of birth, diagnoses, and medical record or Medi-Cal numbers among the data types described. No confirmation was published of a specific downstream fraud tied to the exposure.

**Human factor.** A public agency's frontline staff were targeted with ordinary phishing lures, and the emails reached inboxes protected by whatever authentication those accounts had — email compromise of this kind is a documented, generic-phishing failure mode, not a targeted or technically sophisticated one.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on every account, especially one holding patient correspondence (AUTH-01), staff training current on how a stolen mailbox is used to harvest the data sitting inside it (HUMAN-05), and centralized logging of authentication anomalies that could have flagged the compromised accounts sooner (OBSV-01).

sources:
  - https://www.capitalone.com/facts2019/
  - https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2020/nr-occ-2020-101.html
  - https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/pr/former-seattle-tech-worker-indicted-federal-charges-wire-fraud-and-computer-data-theft
```

**What happened.** Capital One disclosed on July 29, 2019 that a former Amazon Web Services engineer, Paige Thompson, had accessed and copied data on approximately 100 million people in the United States and 6 million in Canada — mostly applicants and customers of Capital One's credit card business from 2005 through early 2019. Thompson was arrested the same day after a tip that she had posted about the theft on GitHub and Slack; a federal grand jury later indicted her on wire fraud and computer fraud charges, and evidence showed she had used similar scanning techniques against more than 30 other organizations.

**Entry.** `entry/ssrf` — Thompson identified a misconfigured web application firewall in front of a Capital One web application and used it to issue a server-side request forgery request that reached AWS's EC2 instance metadata service (IMDSv1), which at the time answered any request from the instance itself with no additional authentication (`entry/misconfiguration` on the WAF that made the SSRF reachable in the first place).

**Escalation.** The metadata service returned temporary security credentials for the IAM role attached to the instance. That role was scoped far beyond the WAF's own needs — it could list and read a large number of Capital One's S3 buckets — so the single SSRF request yielded broad object-storage access without any further exploit (`escalation/overscoped-credential`).

**Blast radius.** Roughly 140,000 Social Security numbers, about 80,000 linked bank account numbers, approximately 1 million Canadian Social Insurance Numbers, and names, addresses, credit scores, credit limits, balances, and payment history for the ~106 million affected individuals, plus fragments of transaction data spanning 23 days between 2016 and 2018. Capital One stated no credit card numbers or account log-in credentials were compromised. The intrusion ran from March 22–23, 2019 until a tipster alerted Capital One around July 17, 2019 — roughly 117 days undetected internally. The OCC and Federal Reserve later fined Capital One a combined $80 million and imposed consent orders over the bank's cloud risk practices; Capital One separately agreed to a $190 million class-action settlement with affected customers.

**Human factor.** The OCC's order found Capital One had not established effective risk-assessment processes before migrating significant IT operations to the public cloud, and did not fix known deficiencies in a timely way. The intrusion was not found by any of Capital One's own controls — it surfaced only because an outside party saw the attacker's own public posts about it.

**What would have stopped it.** Instance metadata requiring a session token rather than answering any local request unauthenticated — the exact gap AWS closed with IMDSv2 (CLOUD-02), least-privilege IAM so a WAF's role cannot list buckets it has no reason to touch (CLOUD-03), and outbound requests from applications restricted to an allowlist that excludes the metadata address entirely (INPUT-04).

```

**What happened.** Sneaker and streetwear resale marketplace StockX had more than 6.8 million user records stolen in May 2019. Rather than disclose a breach, StockX emailed customers on August 1, 2019 citing a vague "system update" as the reason for a forced password reset; TechCrunch confirmed with a data broker that the records were for sale on the dark web and published on August 3, 2019, after which StockX admitted an intrusion had occurred.

**Entry.** `entry/unknown` — neither StockX nor independent reporting ever disclosed the specific technical vulnerability or credential that gave the attacker access to the user database, so this record does not guess at one.

**Escalation.** None described — the user database appears to have been reachable and exfiltrated as a single dataset, with no separate escalation step reported.

**Blast radius.** Names, email and shipping addresses, usernames, purchase history, and MD5-hashed (salted) passwords for more than 6.8 million accounts. StockX stated no payment card data was affected. The company never published its own confirmed count, leaving the 6.8 million figure sourced to the breach broker and TechCrunch's reporting.

**Human factor.** StockX chose language designed to avoid the word "breach" when it forced the password reset, denying its own users the ability to judge their exposure until outside reporting forced a fuller account — exactly the disclosure-failure pattern this corpus tracks separately from the technical entry vector. Passwords were also stored with MD5, an algorithm not designed to resist offline cracking at scale.

**What would have stopped it.** A written incident-communication plan that names who decides what customers are told and on what clock, rather than ad hoc language chosen to minimize alarm (OBSV-05), and password hashing with a modern, memory-hard algorithm instead of MD5 (DATA-06).

```

**What happened.** Researchers Noam Rotem and Ran Locar of vpnMentor found an unsecured, largely unencrypted Elasticsearch database belonging to Suprema's BioStar 2 biometric access-control platform, reachable by anyone who modified a URL parameter in a browser. They discovered it on August 5, 2019, notified Suprema on August 7, and the database was closed on August 13; vpnMentor published its findings on August 14.

**Entry.** `entry/public-database` — the Elasticsearch instance required no authentication and was directly browsable over the internet; researchers located it during routine scanning for exposed databases, the same technique used elsewhere this quarter.

**Escalation.** None required — fingerprints, facial recognition images, plaintext usernames and passwords, employee home addresses, security clearance levels, and physical access logs were all directly readable in the open index.

**Blast radius.** Over 1 million fingerprint records were confirmed exposed, drawn from a system used across more than 1.5 million installations in 83 countries; vpnMentor could not establish a precise count of unique individuals affected, and no confirmed downstream abuse of the data was reported (`impact/none-confirmed`). Unlike a password, an exposed fingerprint cannot be reset.

**Human factor.** A biometric security vendor ran a customer-facing production database with no authentication and largely without encryption, and only found out because outside researchers went looking — Suprema's German office initially would not engage with the researchers' disclosure attempt, and it was the French office that ultimately acted.

**What would have stopped it.** Databases never reachable from the public internet in the first place (DATA-04), biometric and credential fields encrypted at rest so an open index does not equal a readable one (DATA-07), and continuous enumeration of what the organization has exposed rather than waiting for a researcher's scan to find it (CLOUD-01).

```

**What happened.** Security researcher Sanyam Jain found a database, sitting on a server with no password, containing Facebook users' unique account IDs paired with their phone numbers and, for some records, names, gender, and location. Unable to identify the server's owner, Jain contacted TechCrunch, which confirmed and published the exposure on September 4, 2019.

**Entry.** `entry/public-database` — the server required no authentication to query. It was not Facebook's own infrastructure; the data appeared to have been scraped before Facebook restricted phone-number-based profile lookup the previous year, then stored by an unknown third party without any access control.

**Escalation.** None required — each record already paired a Facebook ID directly with a phone number, the complete unit of exposure.

**Blast radius.** TechCrunch and Jain counted more than 419 million records across regions (133 million US, 18 million UK, 50+ million Vietnam, among others); Facebook's own subsequent count, after de-duplication, was "about 220 million" (`confidence: medium` — the totals are disputed between the researcher's raw count and Facebook's own figure). Facebook stated no accounts were compromised and that the data was "old."

**Human factor.** Data that Facebook's own platform had made possible to collect years earlier persisted, unsecured, on infrastructure Facebook did not control and could not immediately identify the owner of — a reminder that data leaving an organization's boundary does not stop being that organization's problem when it is exposed.

**What would have stopped it.** Enforced data-minimization and retention limits so scraped or exported data cannot persist indefinitely on infrastructure nobody is accountable for (DATA-09), routine external scanning for exposed data bearing the organization's own identifiers (CLOUD-01), and a clear, published channel so a researcher who finds an orphaned dataset has someone specific to report it to (OBSV-06).

**What happened.** Toyota Boshoku Corporation, a Toyota Group auto-parts supplier, announced in early September 2019 that a European subsidiary had been defrauded of roughly 4 billion yen (about $37 million) through a business email compromise scheme. Fraudsters posing as a legitimate business partner sent fabricated wire-transfer instructions that finance staff acted on.

**Entry.** `entry/spear-phishing` — the scheme relied on messages crafted to look like they came from a real business counterparty, with enough context about an existing transaction to make a fraudulent bank-account change plausible to the employee who processed it, rather than any exploited software vulnerability.

**Escalation.** None required — the deception itself, not any technical foothold, produced the outcome. Once the finance employee accepted the fraudulent instructions, the transfer completed as an ordinary, authorized business transaction.

**Blast radius.** Approximately $37 million transferred to accounts controlled by the fraudsters, consistently reported across multiple outlets; Toyota Boshoku did not publicly detail how much, if any, was recovered.

**Human factor.** A business email compromise of this kind succeeds only when a wire-transfer change is acted on without an independent, out-of-band check with the counterparty — the pattern is the same one behind nearly every large BEC loss, not a Toyota-specific technical gap.

**What would have stopped it.** A second person required to approve any change to payment or bank-transfer instructions (HUMAN-02), separation of duties between whoever requests and whoever authorizes a wire transfer (HUMAN-08), and staff training that specifically covers how convincingly a compromised or spoofed partner account can request a routine-looking change (HUMAN-05).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- **AWS ships IMDSv2 (November 2019):** Directly in response to the technique behind Capital One's breach, AWS released a second version of the EC2 Instance Metadata Service that requires a session to be established with a PUT request and a secret token presented on every subsequent call, with the token's own network hop capped (TTL=1) so it cannot be relayed through an open proxy. AWS's own guidance frames this explicitly as closing the SSRF-to-credential-theft path.
- **UK ICO's GDPR intent-to-fine notices (July 8–9, 2019):** The Information Commissioner's Office announced its intention to fine British Airways £183 million and Marriott £99 million over their 2018 breaches — the largest proposed GDPR penalties to date at the time, and a signal that "notice of intent" fines calculated as a percentage of global turnover were no longer theoretical. Both final fines were substantially reduced in 2020 (BA to £20m, Marriott to £18.4m), a gap worth noting for anyone using the initial headline figures to model regulatory risk.
- **Cloud risk-assessment scrutiny of financial institutions:** Capital One's breach immediately drew Congressional and bank-regulator attention to how large financial institutions govern a migration to public cloud infrastructure, foreshadowing the formal OCC and Federal Reserve consent orders that followed in 2020.
- **Independent researcher scanning as a detection layer:** vpnMentor's sweeps this quarter (BioStar 2, the Ecuador leak) continue a pattern already visible in Q2 2019 — routine, unsolicited scanning for exposed Elasticsearch and similar databases by outside researchers found what the affected organizations' own monitoring did not.
- **Pushback on vague breach notification:** StockX's "system update" language and Zynga's uncounted disclosure drew specific criticism from identity-theft and security commentators this quarter for denying affected users the information needed to judge their own exposure, adding to the case for notification standards that require a stated scope rather than euphemism.
- **Renewed third-party risk attention after DoorDash:** Coverage of the DoorDash breach reinforced the same vendor-oversight gap already flagged around AMCA, Freedom Mobile, and Wipro in Q2 2019 — that data handed to a processor is only as secure as that processor's own, often invisible, configuration.
