# 2024 Q4 — Unrotated Secrets, Phished Publishers

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/phishing — the only mechanism that repeated this quarter (Casio, Cyberhaven), each time against a human inbox rather than a technical control
> - **New technique this quarter:** a single phished Chrome Web Store publisher account cascaded into a supply-chain campaign across at least 35 unrelated extensions and roughly 2.6 million installs, several of them ChatGPT- and Bard-branded AI assistant tools — publisher-account phishing as a browser-extension supply-chain vector at scale
> - **Recurring failure:** a third of this quarter's records (Finastra, Blue Yonder, Andrew Tate's platform) never had a specific technical entry vector disclosed, and Internet Archive's second wave happened only because tokens it had already told the public were exposed were never rotated

**What happened.** Beginning at some point before mid-2024 and surfacing in press reporting through September and October, a Chinese state-linked group tracked as Salt Typhoon was found to have gained and held access inside the networks of multiple major US telecommunications carriers, named at various points as including AT&T, Verizon, T-Mobile, Lumen Technologies, Charter/Spectrum, Consolidated Communications, and Windstream. Beyond general network access, investigators said the group reached the systems carriers use to fulfill law-enforcement wiretap requests under the Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act (CALEA), along with call and text metadata for more than a million people and, reportedly, audio involving a small number of high-value political figures.

**Entry.** Public reporting attributes initial footholds to exploitation of known, unpatched vulnerabilities in internet-facing network edge devices — Cisco IOS XE, Ivanti Connect Secure/Policy Secure, and Palo Alto Networks GlobalProtect have all been named in connection with the broader Salt Typhoon campaign — rather than a single confirmed zero-day against any one named carrier. No carrier has published its own root-cause account, so the exact device and vulnerability used against each victim is not independently confirmed.

**Escalation.** Once inside carrier infrastructure, the group is reported to have moved laterally from general network access into the specific systems that process CALEA intercept requests — a far more sensitive foothold than ordinary subscriber data, since it offers visibility into who US law enforcement was already surveilling. How that lateral movement was accomplished has not been made public.

**Blast radius.** US officials have avoided giving a single victim count or duration, and both remain under investigation; independent reporting describes access lasting many months across at least seven carriers, metadata for over a million people, and audio tied to a small number of targets. This record carries medium confidence and no scale figure because the scope is genuinely still unresolved or classified in the public record, not merely unreported.

**Human factor.** Every named carrier was running internet-facing network equipment with vulnerabilities that had available patches, on infrastructure privileged enough to reach lawful-intercept systems without additional segmentation — a decision, repeated across at least seven separate companies, to treat perimeter network gear as a lower patching priority than core services.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing and laterally reachable network equipment, since every named exploit target had a fix available), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between general network access and the specific systems that process lawful-intercept requests), OBSV-01 (centralized logging that could show when CALEA intercept systems are reached from unexpected internal hosts).

```

**What happened.** Archive.org, the nonprofit that runs the Wayback Machine, was breached twice within the same month. Attackers first pulled a full user-authentication database — a file named ia_users.sql, dated September 28, 2024, containing roughly 31 million unique email addresses and bcrypt-hashed passwords — and used their access to deface the site on October 9, coinciding with a separate DDoS campaign against the same organization. The Wayback Machine was restored to read-only service within days, but on October 20 attackers returned through the organization's Zendesk help-desk platform, using API tokens that had already been exposed in the first incident and were never rotated.

**Entry.** The initial foothold traced back to a configuration file for Internet Archive's GitLab instance that had been reachable from the public internet since 2022, containing a live authentication token. That token opened access to source-code repositories, which in turn held further credentials attackers used to reach the user database and, eventually, Zendesk support-platform API keys.

**Escalation.** No sophisticated privilege escalation was required: the pattern was one exposed secret leading directly to the next. The GitLab token opened the source tree; secrets sitting in that source tree opened the user database and support systems. When Internet Archive disclosed the breach, it necessarily disclosed which tokens had been exposed — and the Zendesk tokens among them were left valid, so attackers simply reused them three weeks later for a second wave into support tickets.

**Blast radius.** Have I Been Pwned recorded roughly 31.1 million affected accounts (email addresses and bcrypt password hashes) from the first wave; the Zendesk wave exposed support-ticket contents rather than a second bulk database. Public-facing services were degraded or offline, in stages, from October 9 through October 25.

**Human factor.** The rotation failure, not the original exposure, is the lesson: once Internet Archive knew — because it had just told the public — which credentials an attacker had seen, it did not revoke and reissue the Zendesk tokens before the same attacker came back to use them. A small, resource-constrained nonprofit running its own developer and support tooling had no rotation runbook that fired automatically the moment a secret was known to be compromised.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no live secret sits in a reachable configuration file, whether or not the surrounding service is meant to be public), CRED-03 (rotate anything that has been exposed — the single control that would have stopped the second wave entirely, since those tokens were already known-bad), CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook with a named owner and a maximum time-to-revoke, so disclosing an exposure three weeks ago cannot also mean it still works).

**What happened.** Casio, the Japanese electronics maker best known for G-Shock watches and calculators, detected unauthorized access to its network on October 5, 2024. The company took systems offline, postponed its second-quarter earnings release, and disrupted product deliveries while it investigated; a ransomware attack encrypted servers and data was stolen, though Casio's public incident report declined to name the group responsible. A subsequent wave of phishing emails, apparently drawing on information taken in the breach, targeted Casio employees and business contacts.

**Entry.** Casio's own investigation, published January 7, 2025, attributed the intrusion to "deficiencies in the company's measures against phishing emails and its global network security system" — a phishing email provided the foothold, and inadequate network-wide controls let it spread from there. Casio did not identify which employee or system was compromised first.

**Escalation.** From the phishing-derived foothold, the attacker reached servers broadly enough across Casio's network to encrypt them and extract employee, business-partner, and a small number of customer records — consistent with the under-segmented network Casio's own report points to, though the company did not detail the specific lateral-movement steps.

**Blast radius.** Casio's investigation identified approximately 8,478 affected individuals: 6,456 current and former employees (names, employee numbers, email addresses, and for some, birthdates and family information), 1,931 business partners (contact and company details), and 91 customers (delivery addresses and purchase records), plus internal invoices and contracts. Casio said its separate CASIO ID and ClassPad.net customer platforms were unaffected and that no payment card data was exposed.

**Human factor.** Casio's own report frames this as an institutional gap rather than one employee's mistake: its anti-phishing controls and network security architecture were, in the company's own words, deficient, on a corporate network trusted enough to hold HR records, partner data, and customer order data all within reach of the same intrusion.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training reflecting current technique), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA so a phished credential alone cannot be reused), CLOUD-05 (network segmentation so a single phishing-derived foothold cannot reach HR, partner, and customer data alike).

**What happened.** Threat-intelligence vendor Hudson Rock and a hacking-forum seller using the handle "Satanic" reported that a device belonging to an employee of Robling, a third-party data-analytics contractor serving Hot Topic, Box Lunch, and Torrid, had been infected with commodity infostealer malware on September 12, 2024. Hudson Rock said the infected machine held over 240 corporate credentials, including several tied to Hot Topic's and Torrid's Snowflake and Google Cloud/Looker environments, and that the seller was offering records they described as covering 350 million customers. Neither Hot Topic nor Robling had publicly confirmed or denied the claim as of this record, and Hudson Rock itself said it could not conclusively verify the seller was telling the truth.

**Entry.** An infostealer infection on a third-party contractor's device, not a flaw in Hot Topic's own systems, is the reported starting point — commodity credential-harvesting malware that silently exfiltrates saved browser and application logins from an infected machine to a criminal marketplace.

**Escalation.** The reported harm is one of scope rather than technical privilege escalation: a single contractor laptop is described as holding credentials into multiple unrelated corporate cloud environments (Hot Topic's and Torrid's separately), meaning one infostealer infection at Robling had a blast radius spanning several retail clients' data platforms at once, if the claim holds.

**Blast radius.** The claimed scale — 350 million customer records, framed by Hudson Rock as "the largest retail data breach in history" — is disproportionate to Hot Topic's actual customer base and has not been independently corroborated by either company or a regulator; this record therefore uses low confidence and an undisclosed scale rather than repeating the seller's number as fact. What is more directly attested is the infostealer infection itself and the presence of Hot Topic/Torrid-linked corporate credentials on the infected machine.

**Human factor.** If the claim holds up, the underlying failure is vendor access scope: a data-analytics contractor's employee held working credentials into client production data platforms on a device with no evident restriction on what a single infostealer infection could reach.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding a credential into your systems, so Hot Topic would know exactly what Robling could reach), VENDOR-08 (vendor access that is time-boxed and separately monitored rather than a standing, broad credential), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job and one resource, so a single contractor's infected laptop cannot hold keys to more than one client's data warehouse).

**What happened.** In November 2024 a cybercriminal began advertising on a hacking forum what they claimed was more than 400 gigabytes of data stolen from Finastra, a financial-technology vendor whose core-banking and lending software is used by roughly 45 of the world's top 50 banks. Finastra confirmed to KrebsOnSecurity on November 19 that it was investigating "the alleged large-scale theft of information" from its internal Secure File Transfer Platform, without confirming the seller's specific figures. **Escalation.** No escalation beyond the initial access has been described: the file-transfer platform was reportedly itself the source of the data being sold, meaning whatever got the attacker onto that system may have been sufficient on its own, without a separate lateral-movement step being necessary or at least disclosed.

**Human factor.** Finastra's investigation followed a criminal forum listing becoming public, rather than the company's own monitoring catching the activity first — consistent with a detection gap on a platform whose entire purpose is moving sensitive files, which should be among the most closely watched systems a fintech vendor operates.

**What happened.** In November 2024, self-described hacktivists breached "The Real World" (formerly Hustlers University), the paid online business-education platform run by Andrew Tate, for the second time in the same year. The attackers used their access to flood chat channels with LGBTQ+-themed imagery, then exfiltrated the usernames of 794,000 current and former members, 324,382 registered email addresses, and the contents of 221 public and 395 private chat servers, before publishing the data to Have I Been Pwned and the leak site DDoSecrets.

**Entry.** The Register reported the group exploited "an unpatched flaw" in the platform's custom chat client — one that let them upload arbitrary content, delete other users' attachments, crash other members' clients, and issue temporary bans, functionality that would ordinarily require administrator or moderator privileges. Neither the attackers nor the platform publicly identified the specific vulnerability, so this record tags the entry vector unknown rather than name a mechanism that was never technically disclosed.

**Escalation.** No separate escalation step was described: the flaw itself reportedly granted moderator-equivalent actions directly, meaning whatever function was being abused was not distinguishing an ordinary member's session from an administrator's in the first place.

**Blast radius.** 794,000 usernames, 324,382 email addresses, and the contents of 616 chat servers (public and private combined) were taken and published externally, alongside disruptive in-platform vandalism. This followed a separate May 2024 incident in which an unsecured, unauthenticated database had already exposed 968,447 accounts and roughly 22 million messages — two large-scale exposures at the same platform inside one year.

**Human factor.** A platform that had already been through one large, avoidable exposure in May had not fixed whatever allowed attacker-controlled sessions to invoke moderator-level actions by November — the repetition is itself the finding, independent of any single person's mistake.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-02 (authorization checked server-side, per action, so a regular member's session cannot invoke administrator-only chat functions), CICD-02 (required review on anything reaching users, which a second major incident in six months suggests was not consistently applied), OBSV-06 (a published, monitored way for outside researchers to report flaws like this one before hacktivists find and use it instead).

```

**What happened.** Over the 2024 holiday period, an attacker compromised the Google Chrome Web Store publisher account of Cyberhaven, a data-security vendor, and pushed a malicious update of its legitimate, already-installed browser extension to Cyberhaven's existing user base. The malicious version was built to exfiltrate authenticated session cookies and tokens, including for Facebook Ads business accounts. Cyberhaven disclosed the incident on December 27, 2024. Independent researchers subsequently found the same publisher-phishing technique had been used against at least 35 other Chrome extensions, several of them ChatGPT- and Bard-branded AI assistant tools and VPN utilities, in a broader campaign estimated to have reached roughly 2.6 million browser installations.

**Entry.** A phishing email targeted an individual with publish access to Cyberhaven's Chrome Web Store developer account, leading them to grant the attacker permission to push updates under Cyberhaven's existing, already-trusted extension listing — no separate exploit of Cyberhaven's product or infrastructure was required.

**Escalation.** Because the malicious code shipped as a routine auto-update to an extension already installed and trusted by Cyberhaven's users, it inherited every permission those users had already granted the legitimate extension — a signed, previously-vetted artifact carried the payload straight past whatever review users or their organizations had done at install time. The same publisher-account-phishing technique then cascaded across dozens of unrelated extension developers, each individually phished the same way, turning what looked like a single-vendor incident into a supply-chain campaign touching millions of installs.

**Blast radius.** Cyberhaven's own user base was affected first; the wider campaign, once researchers connected the pattern across extensions, is estimated at roughly 2.6 million affected browser installations across at least 35 Chrome extensions, including several marketed as ChatGPT and AI-chatbot companion tools. The malware specifically targeted Facebook advertising account sessions, suggesting the operators were after ad-account takeover and fraud rather than broad espionage.

**Human factor.** A single successful phishing email against one person with publish rights to a widely-installed browser extension was enough to reach every organization that had approved that extension for its employees — the trust boundary that failed was not technical review of the extension's code, but the assumption that a previously-vetted publisher account would stay in the hands of the person it was vetted for.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on Chrome Web Store publisher and developer accounts specifically, not just general employee accounts), CICD-04 (extension updates pinned, reviewed, and version-diffed before going live, so an unreviewed auto-update cannot silently reach an entire install base), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed response for "a browser extension or package we depend on was compromised upstream," since the campaign's true scale only became clear once independent researchers, not the affected vendors collectively, connected dozens of separate incidents).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA, the FBI, and NSA issued joint hardening guidance for communications infrastructure in response to Salt Typhoon, urging carriers to encrypt traffic end-to-end and treat patching of internet-facing network gear as a baseline rather than an aspiration; a related joint advisory (AA25-239A) later named Salt Typhoon explicitly as part of a wider pattern of global telecom targeting.
- Internet Archive's own postmortem is a live argument for automatic secret rotation on disclosure: its Zendesk tokens were only usable in the second wave because they were never revoked after the first wave made them public.
- Several Chrome extensions implicated in the December publisher-phishing campaign were pulled from the Chrome Web Store once Extension Total, Secure Annex, and Cyberhaven's own investigation connected the pattern across more than 35 listings — though reporting noted some compromised extensions remained live and under attacker control after disclosure.
- Casio's public incident report — naming its own phishing defenses and network security as deficient — is a rare instance of a large manufacturer publishing a specific, self-critical root-cause account rather than only a generic notification letter.
- Hot Topic and Robling's silence in the face of Hudson Rock's claims is a case study in why claimed figures from threat-intelligence vendors and forum sellers should not be repeated as confirmed scale until a named victim responds.
- Retailers' and grocers' operational dependence on a single SaaS supply-chain vendor (Blue Yonder), with no disclosed manual-continuity plan, became visible only once that vendor was hit — prompting renewed attention to vendor-concentration risk as a security question, not only a procurement one.
