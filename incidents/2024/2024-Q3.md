# 2024 Q3 — Trusted Channels Become the Attack Surface

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — commodity infostealer malware on personal or third-party devices (AT&T's Snowflake warehouse credential, Disney's Slack session) walked straight past authentication with no exploit required
> - **New technique this quarter:** indirect prompt injection matured from lab demonstration to a repeatable data-exfiltration primitive against shipped production AI features (Slack AI's private-channel retrieval, ChatGPT's persistent memory)
> - **Recurring failure:** several of the quarter's most consequential incidents never named an entry vector at all — Halliburton's own SEC filing, Star Health, the City of Columbus, and Germany's BKG attribution all stop at "unauthorized access"
> - **Records in this file:** 11

**What happened.** In July 2024, the German government formally attributed a "serious" cyberattack against the Bundesamt für Kartographie und Geodäsie (BKG) — the federal agency responsible for precision mapping and geodesy — to Chinese state-sponsored actors. The intrusion itself dated to 2021; the public attribution followed a three-year investigation.

**Escalation.** Not detailed. **Blast radius.** Not quantified publicly. BKG's output feeds government and infrastructure planning; espionage-level access to such an agency for an unknown period before a three-year-old intrusion was even attributed represents a real, if unmeasured, intelligence exposure.

**Human factor.** None identified with confidence — Germany's public disclosure centered on diplomatic attribution, not on any internal process or technical failure at BKG. That is itself notable: the "who" was settled publicly years before any "how" was.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and admin activity, supporting faster detection of long-dwell intrusions), OBSV-08 (log retention that exceeds realistic dwell time — a years-old intrusion cannot be reconstructed without years of retained logs), CLOUD-05 (segmentation so a foothold in one government network cannot freely reach a specialized agency's most sensitive systems).

```yaml
  - https://securityboulevard.com/2024/12/att-snowflake-breach-richixbw-2/
```

**What happened.** AT&T disclosed on July 12, 2024 that an attacker had accessed and exfiltrated nearly six months of customer call and text metadata — which phone numbers AT&T wireless customers interacted with, how often, and for how long, plus some location data — for close to 110 million customers, from an AT&T workspace hosted on the Snowflake cloud data platform. Message content, names, and Social Security numbers were not exposed. AT&T said it discovered the intrusion April 19, 2024, and delayed public disclosure at the request of the FBI and Department of Justice, which cited national security concerns in letters dated May 9 and June 5.

**Entry.** The attacker used credentials stolen via infostealer malware planted on systems that had nothing to do with Snowflake itself — likely a contractor's or employee's device — to log into AT&T's Snowflake account. That account was not configured with multi-factor authentication, which Snowflake did not enforce by default at the time.

**Escalation.** None required. A single warehouse-level credential granted direct query access to the full call-detail-record table; no further privilege escalation was needed once the attacker was authenticated.

**Blast radius.** Unauthorized access to AT&T's Snowflake environment ran from April 14 to April 25, 2024, and swept up metadata for nearly all of AT&T's wireless customer base plus some landline customers who interacted with them. AT&T said "at least one person" had been apprehended in connection with the intrusion by the time of its filing, without naming an actor or group.

**Human factor.** AT&T's own application code was not the point of failure — a third-party analytics platform holding a full export of AT&T's call records was reachable with a single-factor credential that a completely unrelated malware infection was enough to steal.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA required on every account with production data reach, including SaaS data-warehouse accounts), DATA-11 (bulk export of a table this size treated as a privileged, logged, alerting action), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job and one resource rather than a single login unlocking the entire call-detail-record warehouse).

```yaml
  - https://www.securityweek.com/man-admits-hacking-disney-and-leaking-data-disguised-as-hacktivist/
```

**What happened.** A Disney employee, Matthew Van Andel, downloaded a tool from GitHub in February 2024 that was advertised as an AI image-generation utility. It was in fact malware, built by a 25-year-old later identified as Ryan Kramer, designed to steal credentials from whatever device it ran on. On July 11–12, 2024, a persona calling itself "NullBulge" released a roughly 1.2 TB archive containing an estimated 44 million internal Disney Slack messages, framing the leak as hacktivism in protest of AI use in the entertainment industry. Kramer later pleaded guilty to the intrusion; reporting on his plea made clear "NullBulge" was one person, not a collective.

**Entry.** The malicious tool ran on Van Andel's personal computer and harvested credentials stored on that device, which included the login for the Slack account he used for Disney work. No exploit against Disney's own systems was involved.

**Escalation.** None required. The stolen personal-device credential was itself valid corporate Slack access — once authenticated, the attacker could read and export whatever channels that account could reach.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 1.2 TB of internal Slack communications and files, spanning thousands of channels, was exfiltrated and published. The leak also exposed Van Andel's own personal and financial information, which the attacker released separately; Van Andel was later terminated by Disney and has described the episode as having severely damaged his life. Disney subsequently moved away from using Slack for internal communication.

**Human factor.** An employee downloaded unverified software from a public code-sharing site onto a personal device that also held credentials for internal company systems — a boundary that should not have existed, since a personal, unmanaged machine had no business being a valid path into a corporate Slack workspace with no additional check in between.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-07 (managed devices: corporate access should require an enrolled, monitored device, not a personal machine running unvetted downloads), AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions that limit how long a stolen token remains useful), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal read or export volume, which a bulk 1.2 TB Slack export should have triggered).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_CrowdStrike-related_IT_outages
```

**What happened.** On July 19, 2024, CrowdStrike pushed a routine content update ("Channel File 291") to its Falcon sensor, which runs with kernel-level access on protected Windows machines. A defect in the update — an out-of-bounds memory read the Content Interpreter's runtime bounds check should have caught but did not — crashed the sensor and, with it, the Windows kernel, sending affected machines into boot loops. There was no attacker. This is a defect record, not an intrusion, and it is included because the mechanism is identical to one: a trusted software channel with kernel-level reach pushed a payload to millions of machines at once, and nothing in the deployment pipeline caught it before it went everywhere.

**Entry.** CrowdStrike's own root-cause analysis identified three compounding validation gaps: the IPC Template Type's field count was never validated at sensor compile time, a runtime array-bounds check was missing specifically for Channel File 291's Content Interpreter inputs, and CrowdStrike's own Content Validator had a logic flaw that let the malformed template instance through the deployment pipeline undetected.

**Escalation.** None required. The Falcon sensor already runs with kernel-level privileges on every protected endpoint by design, so a defective update needed no further escalation to bring the machine down — the trusted agent already was the highest privilege available.

**Blast radius.** Approximately 8.5 million Windows devices worldwide crashed — CrowdStrike's own estimate, describing it as under 1% of all Windows devices, though those 8.5 million skewed heavily toward the enterprise, healthcare, and aviation systems Falcon is deployed to protect. Airlines cancelled over 5,000 flights globally in a single day; Delta alone cancelled more than 7,000 flights across five days, affecting roughly 1.3 million passengers. Hospitals postponed non-urgent procedures and the London Ambulance Service logged thousands of emergency calls during the disruption. Estimated financial damages are disputed and unaudited: reporting has put UK economic impact at roughly £1.7–2.3 billion and losses across the top 500 US companies at approximately $5.4 billion, of which only a fraction was insured. CrowdStrike reverted the bad update within roughly 79 minutes of it going out and published a fix by mid-morning UTC the same day, but remediation on already-crashed machines required manual intervention at scale.

**Human factor.** CrowdStrike shipped a change with kernel-level blast radius to its entire fleet at once, without the staged, ring-based rollout that would have caught a fatal defect on a small subset of machines before it reached millions — a deployment-process choice, not a one-off coding mistake. The broader lesson for defenders is that a vendor's trusted update channel into a system with kernel access is exactly the access an attacker would want, whether or not this particular failure was malicious.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-09 (release approval requiring a second human and a staged rollout rather than a single global push, which CrowdStrike itself committed to after this incident), CICD-06 (a rehearsed rollback path that does not depend on manually touching every crashed machine), DEPS-03 (customers running a cooldown or deployment ring for content/definition updates rather than trusting every vendor push to go live everywhere immediately, the same discipline recommended for any third-party dependency).

**What happened.** National Public Data, a little-known background-check data broker, confirmed on August 12, 2024 that Social Security numbers and other personal records it held had been stolen, after a hacker using the name "USDoD" began offering the data for sale in April 2024. Separately, security researchers found that a related site sharing the same records infrastructure, RecordsCheck.net, had a file named "members.zip" sitting on its own homepage containing plaintext usernames and passwords for its own site components. The parent company, Jerico Pictures, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 2, 2024, citing the breach-related lawsuits, and shut the business down entirely by December.

The separate RecordsCheck.net exposure had a distinct and fully understood mechanism: a zip archive containing source code and plaintext credentials for the site's own components was reachable directly from the public homepage, no attacker required.

Security researchers and reporters who examined the data found it riddled with duplicates, and the company itself said it was still trying to determine how many actual people were affected — no single, confirmed figure exists, which is why this record uses a disputed range at low confidence rather than either raw claim. The RecordsCheck.net password file's founder said the exposed file held old, "non-working" code and credentials, but it was live and publicly downloadable until removed on August 19, 2024.

**Human factor.** A company whose entire business model was aggregating other people's Social Security numbers and addresses at population scale did not adequately protect the systems the data lived on, and a related site went on to store its own operational credentials in plaintext in a location anyone could download without asking.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits — a broker with no direct relationship to the individuals in its database is a poor candidate for holding identifiers indefinitely), DATA-06 (passwords hashed with a modern algorithm rather than stored in plaintext, which would have made the RecordsCheck.net exposure a non-event even after the file leaked), CRED-01 (no live secret or credential file sitting in a publicly reachable location, on the main site or on any affiliated one).

```yaml
controls: [AGENT-01, AGENT-03, DATA-01]
sources:
  - https://promptarmor.substack.com/p/data-exfiltration-from-slack-ai-via
```

**What happened.** Security firm PromptArmor published research on August 20, 2024 showing that Slack AI could be manipulated into leaking data from private channels a user had never joined. An attacker needed only to create a public channel — visible to Slack AI's retrieval even if not to the target user — and post a message containing instructions disguised as ordinary text. PromptArmor privately notified Slack on August 14; Slack's security team reviewed it and concluded the behavior was working as intended, so PromptArmor published publicly, noting that a Slack AI update that same week to let the assistant ingest uploaded files had just made the same technique meaningfully more dangerous. No compromise of a real Slack customer was confirmed; this is a responsibly disclosed research finding, not an in-the-wild exploit.

**Entry.** Indirect prompt injection: the malicious instructions never came from the user operating Slack AI. They arrived embedded in a message in a channel the underlying retrieval system read as context, and Slack AI's model could not distinguish that planted instruction text from data it was supposed to merely summarize.

**Escalation.** None required. Once the attacker's instructions and the target's private-channel data were pulled into the same model context together, the model itself did the exfiltration — rendering a link that embedded the confidential data as a URL parameter, which the victim's own browser would then request from an attacker-controlled server.

**Blast radius.** The technique, as demonstrated, could reach any information available to Slack AI's retrieval regardless of the querying user's own channel membership, including API keys and credentials PromptArmor found had been pasted into Slack messages — a common practice the technique turned into a direct exposure path. No customer-data theft via this specific technique was confirmed to have occurred outside PromptArmor's own testing.

**Human factor.** Slack shipped a feature that pools content across permission boundaries into one model context without a way to keep untrusted instructions and privileged data apart, then treated a researcher's first report as expected behavior rather than a defect — this corpus's tag vocabulary does not yet have a precise entry for "an AI feature mixed data across authorization boundaries by design," which is itself worth flagging as a vocabulary gap.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-01 (treat everything an agent reads as untrusted data, never as instruction, including messages from channels the user does not control), AGENT-03 (never let private data, untrusted content, and a path to an external destination combine without a human in the loop), DATA-01 (default-deny, per-request authorization so content the querying user could not otherwise read never enters their answer, regardless of what the retrieval layer can see).

```yaml
sources:

```

**What happened.** Researcher Johann Rehberger (embracethered.com) demonstrated a technique dubbed "SpAIware": a website or document containing hidden instructions could, when a user asked ChatGPT to read or summarize it, trick ChatGPT into writing spyware-like instructions into its own persistent memory feature. Once planted, that memory silently exfiltrated the content of every subsequent conversation by rendering an invisible image whose URL embedded the conversation's contents, fetched from an attacker-controlled server. Rehberger reported an initial version of the underlying image-rendering exfiltration technique in April 2023 and a full end-to-end exploit chain using the memory feature in June 2024; OpenAI shipped a fix in ChatGPT version 1.2024.247 in September 2024, and the research was presented publicly at BSides Vancouver Island around the same time.

**Entry.** Indirect prompt injection: the instructions that wrote the malicious memory never came from the user. They arrived embedded in a web page or document the user had ChatGPT process, and the model treated that embedded text as something to act on rather than inert content to summarize.

**Escalation.** None required. Writing one malicious memory entry was sufficient — from that point forward, every new conversation the user had was compromised automatically, with no further injection needed.

**Blast radius.** As demonstrated, the technique could exfiltrate the full content of a user's ongoing ChatGPT conversations indefinitely, until the planted memory was found and removed. No real-world victim was confirmed; this was responsibly disclosed research, and OpenAI's fix specifically blocked the automatic outbound rendering that carried data to an attacker's server. The underlying ability for injected content to write a false memory at all was reported as not fully resolved even after the fix.

**Human factor.** OpenAI shipped a persistent-memory feature and largely unrestricted outbound image rendering as two independently reasonable features that combined into a silent, self-perpetuating exfiltration channel — a class of interaction effect that is hard to catch without specifically testing how new features compose with existing ones.

**What would have stopped it.** AGENT-01 (content a model reads from an untrusted document or page is data, never an instruction to act on, including an instruction to write to memory), AGENT-03 (a feature that can both read untrusted content and reach an external destination needs a human in the loop between the two, not automatic execution), INPUT-04 (outbound requests to attacker-suppliable URLs — including markdown image rendering — allowlisted or blocked by default rather than rendered automatically).

**What happened.** Transport for London detected unauthorized activity on its systems on September 10, 2024 and disclosed a cyberattack publicly on September 12. The attack, attributed to the Scattered Spider collective, began around August 31, 2024 and exposed personal details — names, contact information, and addresses — for roughly 10 million customers, plus bank account numbers and sort codes for about 5,000 customers who had received Oyster card refunds. Card payment data was not affected, since TfL's payment-card processing runs on a separate system. Two British teenagers were subsequently charged in connection with the intrusion.

**Entry.** Consistent with Scattered Spider's documented playbook elsewhere, the group is reported to have used phone-based social engineering — a vishing call to TfL's IT help desk — to get credentials or MFA reset for an account the caller did not own, rather than exploiting a software vulnerability.

**Escalation.** A help-desk credential or MFA reset performed for an impersonated caller is, functionally, internal support tooling doing the attacker's escalation work for them: once the reset succeeded, the attacker held valid access that needed no further technical exploit to reach the customer systems holding Oyster and contactless payment data.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 10 million people had personal data exposed; approximately 5,000 had bank details specifically accessed. The intrusion disrupted several online services and took station information boards offline in parts of the network. TfL has estimated the incident's cost at roughly £39 million, with disruption to some services lasting around three months.

**Human factor.** A phone call to a help desk was enough to obtain a credential or MFA reset for an account the caller did not control — the identity-verification step that should stand between "someone called claiming to be an employee" and "that employee's access was reset" was not strong enough to withstand a confident impersonation.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential or MFA reset that is independent of the caller's own claims about who they are), AUTH-05 (account recovery and help-desk paths held to the same strength as the primary login path, since they are otherwise the weakest way in), AUTH-07 (admin and support-reset tooling sitting behind its own separate authentication and full audit trail, so a single phone call cannot directly trigger a live access change).

**What happened.** A threat actor using the name "xenZen" set up Telegram chatbots, active from around August 6, 2024, that let anyone download samples of what was claimed to be Star Health Insurance customer data for free, with bulk downloads sold for a fee. The actor claimed roughly 31 million customers' data was included — names, addresses, phone numbers, policy details, government-issued ID numbers, and health information including diagnoses and test results — and separately demanded a $68,000 ransom from Star Health, India's largest health insurer. Media scrutiny initially fell on the company's own security leadership; Star Health subsequently stated its security chief was not involved. In October 2024, India's Madras High Court ordered Telegram to remove the chatbots and related posts.

**Entry.** Not established. Star Health has not confirmed how xenZen obtained the data, and this record does not tag an insider despite the early media speculation, since the company's own statement disputes that account and no independent confirmation of a different mechanism is available.

**Escalation.** None required as best can be inferred: the scale and specificity of the leaked data — spanning policy, identity, and medical detail together — is more consistent with a single bulk database export than with piecemeal access requiring further escalation, though the entry mechanism itself remains unconfirmed.

**Blast radius.** The attacker's own claim put the affected population at roughly 31 million customers with several terabytes of data; Star Health has not published a confirmed figure, so this record treats the number as an attacker claim rather than a confirmed scale, per this corpus's standard practice for unverified attacker assertions.

**Human factor.** Not established with confidence — no specific technical or process failure at Star Health has been publicly confirmed, and the one specific human-factor allegation that surfaced in reporting (involvement by the company's security chief) was subsequently disputed by the company itself.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-07 (sensitive fields — medical diagnoses, government ID numbers — encrypted at rest, which limits what a bulk export actually yields even if access occurs), DATA-11 (bulk export of a customer database this size treated as a privileged, logged, alerting action), OBSV-06 (a published, monitored channel for reporting exposures, which might have surfaced the chatbot distribution channel faster).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CrowdStrike published a detailed public root-cause analysis within weeks of the outage and committed to staged, ring-based content deployment, expanded automated testing, and independent third-party review of its update pipeline — treating its own content-delivery channel with the rigor normally reserved for code, not configuration.
- Halliburton's Item 1.05 filing is a concrete instance of the SEC's 2023 cybersecurity-disclosure rule in practice: a large public company filing a dedicated "material cybersecurity incident" 8-K within about two weeks of discovery, while the investigation was still open.
- Slack's security team initially reviewed PromptArmor's private report and judged the behavior intended; only after public disclosure did the company restrict how Slack AI ingests uploaded files, illustrating how a vendor's first response to a researcher can differ from its eventual one.
- OpenAI's fix for the ChatGPT memory-exfiltration technique blocked the specific outbound rendering channel within roughly three months of the full exploit chain being demonstrated, though researchers noted the underlying ability for injected content to write a false memory was not itself eliminated.
- Star Health pursued Telegram directly through India's Madras High Court to remove the leak-distribution chatbots, an example of targeting the distribution channel through the courts rather than only pursuing the original intruder.
- Transport for London's and other 2024 Scattered-Spider-linked cases (two UK teenagers were charged over the TfL intrusion) continued a pattern of help-desk-targeted social engineering that phishing-resistant MFA alone does not stop, since the attack targets the reset process rather than the credential itself.
