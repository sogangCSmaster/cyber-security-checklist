# 2024 Q2 — Single-Factor Accounts, Borrowed Trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — infostealer-harvested Snowflake account credentials, protected by a password alone, drove the quarter's defining campaign (Santander, Ticketmaster, and AT&T's 110-million-record breach, which discloses in Q3 and is referenced here only as context)
> - **New technique this quarter:** not new tradecraft so much as a new target for old tradecraft — buying a trusted domain and GitHub account outright (Polyfill.io) to poison a CDN hostname that 100,000+ sites already trusted without pinning
> - **Recurring failure:** the control that would have stopped the quarter's biggest breaches (MFA, a rate limit, a version pin) had been available and unused for years; where ransomware crews got in cleanly instead (Ascension, Synnovis), even the victims' own post-incident investigations couldn't say how
> - **Records in this file:** 8

**What happened.** Ascension, a Catholic health system operating 142 hospitals across the United States, detected a ransomware attack on May 8, 2024 that forced clinical staff to revert to paper charting, diverted ambulances, and took electronic health records offline for weeks. Press reporting attributed the attack to Black Basta, though Ascension itself never officially named the group; a joint FBI/CISA/HHS/MS-ISAC advisory on Black Basta was issued two days later. Ascension confirmed in December 2024 — seven months after detection — that the intrusion had exposed protected health information for 5,599,699 patients.

**Entry.** Ascension's own account says an employee "inadvertently downloaded a file they believed to be legitimate" onto a company device, which the company has called "an honest mistake." Ascension has not disclosed the delivery channel — whether an email attachment, a malicious search result, or a compromised download page — only that the employee was deceived into trusting the file.

**Escalation.** From that single compromised device, the attackers moved laterally to 7 of Ascension's roughly 25,000 servers — machines described as handling "daily and routine tasks" rather than clinical systems directly — before deploying ransomware broadly enough to take the health system's EHR access down system-wide.

**Blast radius.** Multiple hospitals placed emergency rooms on divert, postponed elective procedures, and ran pharmacy and prescription systems on paper for roughly six weeks while EHR access was rebuilt. The confirmed patient count grew sharply over time: an initial HHS filing in July 2024 listed about 500 affected individuals; the figure finalized in December 2024 was 5,599,699 patients, making this one of the largest healthcare breaches disclosed that year.

**Human factor.** One employee's mistaken trust in a downloaded file was enough to shut down patient-facing systems across a 142-hospital system for weeks — a mismatch between the blast radius a single workstation should be able to cause and the blast radius one actually caused.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (training reflecting current file-based social-engineering technique, not only email phishing), CLOUD-05 (segmentation that keeps a compromised end-user device from reaching servers capable of propagating ransomware system-wide), OBSV-01 (centralized logging that should have surfaced lateral movement from one device to seven servers before full deployment).

```yaml
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/dell-api-abused-to-steal-49-million-customer-records-in-data-breach/
```

**What happened.** Dell notified customers on May 9–10, 2024 that a threat actor had scraped approximately 49 million customer records — names, physical addresses, and order/warranty details for purchases going back to 2017 — from a portal Dell operates for resellers and channel partners. The attacker, using the handle "Menelik," emailed Dell on April 12 and 14 to report the underlying flaw, then posted the harvested data for sale on a hacking forum on April 28, days before Dell's own customer notification went out.

**Entry.** Menelik registered several accounts under fake company names on Dell's partner portal, which granted working API access within about two days with no verification that the "companies" were real. A script then generated sequential seven-digit Dell service tags and submitted them to the portal's order-lookup endpoint at up to 5,000 requests per minute.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial registration — the partner API returned full order and customer records for any service tag submitted, with no rate limit or per-account volume cap to slow bulk enumeration.

**Blast radius.** Over roughly three weeks the script pulled records for approximately 49 million customers: names, addresses, order numbers, and warranty details. Dell said the data did not include financial information, email addresses, phone numbers, or other highly sensitive fields, though the attacker separately claimed to also hold email and phone data for "several thousand" additional customers — a claim Dell has not confirmed. Dell warned customers of likely follow-on tech-support scam calls using the leaked order details as social-engineering material.

**Human factor.** A partner-onboarding process that granted working API credentials to unverified, freshly registered "companies" within 48 hours, feeding an API with no rate limiting, turned a bug worth a quiet report into a 49-million-record breach before Dell ever heard from the person who found it.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-09 (the service tags were sequential and guessable, and nothing else gated the record behind them), AUTH-06 (rate limiting on the partner API would have capped enumeration long before 49 million records), DATA-11 (a bulk-export volume alert would have flagged 5,000 requests per minute well before three weeks passed).

```yaml
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowflake_data_breach
```

**What happened.** Banco Santander confirmed in May 2024 that an unauthorized party had accessed a database hosted by a third-party provider, containing information on Santander customers in Spain, Chile, and Uruguay as well as current and some former Santander employees. Santander was one of roughly 165 organizations Mandiant later tied to the same wave of Snowflake-account compromises, tracked as UNC5537 — the same campaign behind this quarter's Ticketmaster breach and AT&T's later-disclosed 110-million-record breach.

**Entry.** Consistent with the wider campaign, access followed from a Snowflake account credential previously harvested by infostealer malware. The account was protected by a username and password alone, with no multi-factor authentication and no network allow-list restricting which IP addresses could connect.

**Escalation.** None required. A valid, unrotated credential — some in this campaign were four or more years old — was itself sufficient to query the full contents of the linked database and export the results directly.

**Blast radius.** Santander confirmed that customer records for Spain, Chile, and Uruguay, plus employee records, were accessed, and said the breach did not affect transactional capability or online banking credentials. The widely reported "30 million records" figure traces to the data as advertised for sale on a criminal forum, not to a count Santander itself has confirmed — consistent with this quarter's pattern of attacker-claimed scale outrunning victim confirmation, this record treats it as reported rather than established and is scored accordingly.

**Human factor.** Santander extended a third-party data platform the same account-security assumptions it would apply to a purely internal system, without requiring the MFA or IP restrictions Snowflake itself offered as configurable options.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA would have made the stolen password alone insufficient), CRED-07 (credentials should expire rather than remain valid for years), OBSV-02 (an alert on a bulk read of the full customer table).

```yaml
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/medisecure-ransomware-attack/
```

**What happened.** MediSecure, an Australian company that delivered electronic prescriptions as part of the national digital-health program, identified a ransomware-linked data breach in April 2024 and confirmed publicly in May that personal and health information belonging to up to 12.9 million Australians — roughly half the country's population — had been stolen from a compromised backup data store spanning transactions from 2019 to November 2023. MediSecure entered voluntary administration weeks later after losing its primary government contract; Australia's privacy regulator, the OAIC, closed its inquiry in September 2024 without pursuing a full investigation.

**Entry.** MediSecure said "early indicators" pointed to the incident originating at one of its third-party vendors rather than in MediSecure's own primary systems. The company has not published further technical detail on which vendor, or what access that vendor held.

**Escalation.** Not detailed publicly, but the exposed data was drawn from a backup store rather than MediSecure's live prescription-delivery database — the attacker reached a secondary store holding years of retained records rather than only current transactions, implying movement beyond the initial third-party access point.

**Blast radius.** Up to 12.9 million Australians had names, contact details, Medicare numbers, concession card details, and prescription records exposed, in a stolen 6.5TB data set — one of the five largest breaches in Australian history. The stolen data was listed for sale on a criminal forum for $50,000, then resold for $25,000, after the Australian government declined to fund a rescue of the company.

**Human factor.** A single subprocessor's compromise reached years of retained backup records for nearly half a country's population, because a vendor relationship for a service that, by MediSecure's own account, "did not have any connections to the prescribing and dispensing of medications" still carried access to years of historical health data.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding sensitive data, including what backups a vendor's own subprocessors retain), DATA-09 (retention limits — over four years of prescription backup data sat exposed to a single compromise), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification and monitoring obligations on vendors handling national health data).

**What happened.** Live Nation Entertainment disclosed in a May 2024 SEC filing that it had identified unauthorized activity in a third-party cloud database used by its Ticketmaster subsidiary. Within days, the group ShinyHunters began advertising a database it described as 560 million Ticketmaster customer records — names, addresses, and partial payment data — for sale on a criminal forum, threatening to leak it without payment. Live Nation's own filing confirmed the intrusion but did not corroborate the 560-million figure or the payment-data claim.

**Entry.** Live Nation attributed the access to the same Snowflake-linked campaign affecting dozens of other companies this quarter: a valid Snowflake account credential, obtained via infostealer malware rather than any vulnerability in Ticketmaster's own applications, protected by a password alone.

**Blast radius.** Live Nation confirmed unauthorized access to Snowflake-hosted data but has not corroborated ShinyHunters' 560-million-record claim or the assertion that partial payment card data was included. Consistent with the instruction to separate an extortion actor's claim from a victim's confirmation, this record scores the scale at low confidence: it is what ShinyHunters advertised, not what Live Nation has verified.

**Human factor.** The same single-factor, unrotated Snowflake credential pattern behind the rest of this quarter's defining campaign — a configuration decision made, or left unmade, well before any attacker was involved.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (MFA would have blocked reuse of an infostealer-harvested password), CRED-07 (credential rotation limits how long a stolen password stays useful), OBSV-02 (export-volume alerting on the scale ShinyHunters claims would have been very hard to miss).

**What happened.** On June 3, 2024, the Qilin ransomware group encrypted systems at Synnovis, a pathology-services partnership (SYNLAB with Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital NHS trusts) that processes blood and specimen tests for major London hospitals. The group exfiltrated roughly 400GB of data beforehand and demanded a $50 million ransom; Synnovis refused to pay, citing the risk of funding further attacks on critical infrastructure, and Qilin published the stolen data after its deadline passed.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. Synnovis's investigation could not determine how the attackers first gained access despite what it described as an extensive forensic effort — one of two ransomware cases this quarter (with Ascension) where the initial foothold was never identified even after the fact.

**Escalation.** Not detailed publicly, though the scale of disruption — full recovery required rebuilding more than 75 applications and over 65 scientific analyzers — points to the ransomware reaching broadly across Synnovis's network rather than a single isolated system.

**Blast radius.** More than 10,000 hospital appointments were cancelled, over 1,500 operations and outpatient appointments were postponed across the two main trusts, and dozens of cancer treatments and organ transplants were delayed. The disruption also caused a months-long nationwide shortage of O-negative blood as hospitals fell back on manual blood-typing; full technical recovery took roughly five months. The stolen data included results for HIV, STI, and cancer tests, described as covering some 300 million historical patient interactions, though forensic analysis found the exfiltrated set was not drawn from Synnovis's primary lab databases and was assessed as not readily exploitable in the form taken.

**Human factor.** None identified with certainty — the entry vector itself was never established, which is a finding in its own right: a pathology provider handling results for two major hospital trusts could not say, even after extensive investigation, how a ransomware group got in.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation that would have kept ransomware from reaching enough of the estate to require rebuilding 75+ applications), DATA-08 (restore-tested backups isolated from production credentials, to shorten a five-month recovery), OBSV-05 (a rehearsed incident and business-continuity plan for a service whose downtime cancels surgeries and delays cancer treatment, not only one that protects data).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://thehackernews.com/2024/06/over-110000-websites-affected-by.html
  - https://sansec.io/research/polyfill-supply-chain-attack
```

**What happened.** In February 2024, the domain and GitHub account behind polyfill.io — a widely trusted JavaScript CDN originally built by developer Andrew Betts to patch older browsers' missing features — were sold to Funnull, a China-based CDN operator. Security firm Sansec published research on June 25, 2024 showing the new owners had modified the script served from cdn.polyfill.io to redirect mobile visitors to sports-betting and other scam sites, affecting more than 100,000 websites that linked to the script by hostname, including JSTOR, Intuit, and the World Economic Forum.

**Entry.** No intrusion was needed. The attack path was ownership of a domain name: sites that wrote a `<script src="cdn.polyfill.io/...">` tag were trusting whoever currently controlled that hostname, and when control changed hands, every one of those sites began serving the new owner's code automatically, with no code change on the sites' own end.

**Escalation.** A textbook supply-chain cascade — compromising one shared, widely included resource reached the visitors of every downstream site that referenced it, with no further access needed to any individual site's own infrastructure.

**Blast radius.** More than 100,000 sites served the malicious script to their visitors. The payload used deliberate evasion logic: it activated mainly for mobile visitors, varied by time of day, and avoided triggering for detected site administrators or when analytics tools were present, to delay discovery. Namecheap suspended the original domain on June 27, 2024; Cloudflare, Google, and Fastly stood up safe mirrored copies of the legitimate pre-acquisition script so affected sites could redirect away without code changes, and Google blocked ads on e-commerce sites still loading it.

**Human factor.** Thousands of engineering teams made the same reasonable-looking choice years earlier — link to a free, convenient CDN by its hostname — and none were still checking, years later, whether that hostname was still owned by whoever they had trusted when they added it. The lesson this quarter's framing draws out directly: these sites had pinned a hostname, not a version.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-06 (no script loaded from a URL you do not control — pinned or self-hosted is the direct fix), VENDOR-05 (third-party scripts on any page minimized, pinned to a specific version, or isolated), VENDOR-09 (periodically verifying what a bundled third-party component actually does at runtime, not just what it did when it was added).

**What happened.** On May 16, 2024, a group calling itself Rabbitude said it gained access to source code for the Rabbit R1, a standalone AI voice-assistant hardware device, and found hardcoded API keys for several backend services — including ElevenLabs' text-to-speech API and, disclosed only later, a SendGrid email key — built directly into code shipping on the device. Rabbit did not act while the report stayed private; Rabbitude published its findings publicly on June 25, 2024, roughly six weeks after first finding the keys, at which point Rabbit began rotating them.

**Entry.** The keys were exposed the way any hardcoded client-side secret is: shipped inside the software running on a device Rabbitude was able to examine, rather than held behind a server-side call the device made on request.

**Escalation.** None required technically — a hardcoded key found in shipped client code is immediately usable with the full permissions it was given. Rabbitude claimed the exposed keys could read every response any R1 had ever given, alter live responses, or disable devices entirely; Rabbit's own account disputes the severity of some of these claims, saying the ElevenLabs key could disrupt voice output but not permanently disable a device, and that the SendGrid key was scoped only to send mail from one address.

**Blast radius.** Rabbit's own investigation concluded that no customer data was exposed or accessed, and that the only confirmed misuse of the keys was sending defamatory emails to Rabbit employees, journalists, and other members of the group — Rabbitude's broader claims about read access to historical device responses were not independently confirmed, so this record treats the confirmed impact as none beyond that misuse. Rabbit rotated the exposed secrets, migrated them into AWS Secrets Manager, added automated checks for hardcoded secrets in code review, and shortened its vulnerability-disclosure response window from 180 to 90 days.

**Human factor.** An AI hardware product shipped with live third-party API credentials embedded in client code that any sufficiently curious owner of the device could extract — a basic secrets-handling mistake in a product built around exactly the kind of always-listening, cloud-connected AI functionality that makes those credentials valuable to misuse.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no secret should sit in a reachable client bundle in the first place), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime from a manager rather than baked into shipped code — precisely the fix Rabbit applied after the fact), CRED-03 (rotate anything a researcher, or anyone else, could have extracted, immediately rather than six weeks later).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Snowflake, Mandiant, and CISA published coordinated guidance urging all customers to enable multi-factor authentication and network allow-lists on data-platform accounts, and to treat any credential ever seen in an infostealer log as compromised regardless of age — the direct response to the campaign behind three of this quarter's records (Santander, Ticketmaster, and AT&T's later-disclosed breach).
- CISA, the FBI, HHS, and MS-ISAC issued a dedicated #StopRansomware advisory on Black Basta (AA24-131A) days after the Ascension attack began, detailing the group's phishing, vishing/help-desk impersonation, and ConnectWise (CVE-2024-1709) techniques for defenders across critical infrastructure sectors.
- Cloudflare, Google, and Fastly responded to the Polyfill.io hijack by standing up safe mirror endpoints within days, and Google blocked ads on affected e-commerce sites — an infrastructure-level response that let many downstream sites recover without individually rewriting code.
- Rabbit's post-incident remediation — migrating secrets into AWS Secrets Manager, adding automated secret-scanning to code review, and shortening its vulnerability-disclosure window from 180 to 90 days — is a workable template for any hardware or AI product shipping client-side code with embedded backend credentials.
- Australia's privacy regulator (OAIC) closed its MediSecure inquiry without a full investigation but publicly used the case to back the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Bill 2024, aimed at strengthening enforcement powers under Australian Privacy Principle 11.
- Synnovis and NHS England sustained a national call for O-negative blood donations for months after the attack — a reminder that ransomware against a single mid-sized lab-services vendor can produce physical, not only data, consequences.
- Across this quarter's largest breaches, the pattern was rarely a novel exploit: it was a missing MFA requirement, an unpinned CDN hostname, an unrate-limited API, or a hardcoded key — controls that were available, well understood, and simply not applied.
