# 2024 Q2 — Single-Factor Accounts, Borrowed Trust

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — infostealer-harvested Snowflake account credentials, protected by a password alone, drove the quarter's defining campaign (Santander, Ticketmaster, and AT&T's 110-million-record breach, which discloses in Q3 and is referenced here only as context)
> - **New technique this quarter:** not new tradecraft so much as a new target for old tradecraft — buying a trusted domain and GitHub account outright (Polyfill.io) to poison a CDN hostname that 100,000+ sites already trusted without pinning
> - **Recurring failure:** the control that would have stopped the quarter's biggest breaches (MFA, a rate limit, a version pin) had been available and unused for years; where ransomware crews got in cleanly instead (Ascension, Synnovis), even the victims' own post-incident investigations couldn't say how
> - **Records in this file:** 18

  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/biggest-healthcare-data-breaches-2024/
```

**What happened.** Kaiser Foundation Health Plan disclosed in April 2024 that an internal investigation had found tracking code embedded on its member websites and mobile apps — including its member portals, health-encyclopedia pages, and the My Doctor Online and KP Health Ally apps — had been transmitting user data to Google, Microsoft, Adobe, and X. Up to 13.4 million current and former members were notified, one of the largest healthcare breaches disclosed that year, trailing only a handful of larger incidents such as the Change Healthcare ransomware attack.

**Entry.** No intrusion occurred. Kaiser's own engineering and marketing teams had added standard third-party analytics and advertising tracking pixels to authenticated, health-related pages, which is what made this an inadvertent disclosure rather than a hack: the data left Kaiser's control because Kaiser's own code sent it out, not because anyone broke in.

**Escalation.** None required — the trackers ran on every page load and transmitted their payload directly to the third parties receiving it, with no further access needed on any attacker's part, because there was no attacker.

**Blast radius.** The transmitted data included names, IP addresses, sign-on status, site navigation, and — because the trackers sat on Kaiser's health-encyclopedia search function — the search terms members used to look up symptoms, drugs, injuries, and exercises, tied to an identifiable individual. Litigation over the incident settled in late 2025 for up to $47.5 million.

**Human factor.** Standard marketing and analytics tooling was deployed onto pages that only authenticated patients could reach, by people who evidently were not asking whether a page showing what health condition a specific logged-in user had just searched for was an appropriate place for a third-party ad pixel — a mismatch between a routine web-development decision and the sensitivity of the page it ran on.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-05 (third-party scripts on sensitive, authenticated pages minimized, pinned, or isolated from analytics entirely), HUMAN-07 (a checklist for adding any third-party script to a patient-facing page, not just a competent developer's judgment), DATA-09 (data minimization — health-encyclopedia search terms did not need to be transmitted anywhere at all).

  - https://thehackernews.com/2024/04/ai-as-service-providers-vulnerable-to.html
```

**What happened.** Wiz Research published findings on April 4, 2024 showing two ways a Hugging Face account — the most widely used hub for sharing AI models — could be used to reach other customers' private models and infrastructure: uploading a malicious model in Python's "pickle" serialization format, and uploading a malicious Dockerfile through Hugging Face's Spaces feature. Hugging Face fixed the issues before publication and, in the same response, deployed additional scanning tooling across the platform.

**Entry.** Both paths used Hugging Face's own core functionality — uploading a model or a container — as the delivery mechanism. A pickle-format PyTorch model executes arbitrary code the moment it is loaded for inference, which Hugging Face's own infrastructure would do automatically even for a model already flagged as dangerous; a crafted Dockerfile achieved code execution during the image build step instead, when a Space was deployed.

**Escalation.** From code execution inside one container, Wiz's researchers found a path to escape into the shared Kubernetes cluster running customer workloads, reach cloud instance metadata, and obtain credentials with write access to the platform's internal container registry — a foothold in one customer's model translating into the ability to tamper with images served to others, and, separately, into the "millions of private AI models and apps" hosted across the platform.

**Blast radius.** No customer's data was confirmed compromised; this was a proof-of-concept chain Wiz reported before anyone exploited it maliciously. The researchers' own framing is that, used maliciously, it could have reached the private models of any of Hugging Face's customers, not just the account that uploaded the malicious model.

**Human factor.** None identified — the exposure was architectural: a platform whose entire product is running untrusted, user-supplied code and models did not yet isolate that execution as strictly as running arbitrary code demands.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-06 (no deserialization or execution of untrusted data — the pickle format itself is the root cause Hugging Face has since pushed customers away from), CLOUD-05 (network and cluster segmentation that keeps one tenant's container from reaching another's or the registry), DATA-01 (default-deny isolation on the shared infrastructure so one compromised model cannot reach every other customer's).

**What happened.** On the morning of April 18, 2024, the Black Basta ransomware group hit Synlab Italia, the Italian arm of the SYNLAB diagnostics group — a distinct national subsidiary from Synnovis, the UK SYNLAB partnership hit separately by a different gang later this same quarter. Synlab Italia's IT department excluded its entire corporate network and shut down all machines on discovery, suspending laboratory analysis, sample collection, and email across the roughly 380 labs and medical centres it operates nationwide. Black Basta claimed the attack on its leak site on May 4, 2024, publishing identity documents and medical test results as proof, and threatened to release roughly 1.5TB of stolen data if a ransom was not paid by May 11.

**Human factor.** None identified with certainty, beyond the same structural question this quarter's other unattributed ransomware cases raise: a single foothold, however it was obtained, was able to reach far enough across Synlab Italia's estate to force a full network shutdown rather than a contained response.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation that would have kept a single foothold from reaching all 380 labs and the corporate email system alike), DATA-08 (restore-tested backups isolated from production credentials, to shorten recovery without relying on the attacker), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan for a diagnostics provider whose downtime stops medical test results from reaching patients, not only a data-protection plan).

**What happened.** Banco Santander confirmed in May 2024 that an unauthorized party had accessed a database hosted by a third-party provider, containing information on Santander customers in Spain, Chile, and Uruguay as well as current and some former Santander employees. Santander was one of roughly 165 organizations Mandiant later tied to the same wave of Snowflake-account compromises, tracked as UNC5537 — the same campaign behind this quarter's Ticketmaster breach and AT&T's later-disclosed 110-million-record breach.

**Entry.** Consistent with the wider campaign, access followed from a Snowflake account credential previously harvested by infostealer malware. The account was protected by a username and password alone, with no multi-factor authentication and no network allow-list restricting which IP addresses could connect.

The widely reported "30 million records" figure traces to the data as advertised for sale on a criminal forum, not to a count Santander itself has confirmed — consistent with this quarter's pattern of attacker-claimed scale outrunning victim confirmation, this record treats it as reported rather than established and is scored accordingly.

**Human factor.** Santander extended a third-party data platform the same account-security assumptions it would apply to a purely internal system, without requiring the MFA or IP restrictions Snowflake itself offered as configurable options.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA would have made the stolen password alone insufficient), CRED-07 (credentials should expire rather than remain valid for years), OBSV-02 (an alert on a bulk read of the full customer table).

**What happened.** MediSecure, an Australian company that delivered electronic prescriptions as part of the national digital-health program, identified a ransomware-linked data breach in April 2024 and confirmed publicly in May that personal and health information belonging to up to 12.9 million Australians — roughly half the country's population — had been stolen from a compromised backup data store spanning transactions from 2019 to November 2023. MediSecure entered voluntary administration weeks later after losing its primary government contract; Australia's privacy regulator, the OAIC, closed its inquiry in September 2024 without pursuing a full investigation.

**What happened.** Ascension, a Catholic health system operating 142 hospitals across the United States, detected a ransomware attack on May 8, 2024 that forced clinical staff to revert to paper charting, diverted ambulances, and took electronic health records offline for weeks. Press reporting attributed the attack to Black Basta, though Ascension itself never officially named the group; a joint FBI/CISA/HHS/MS-ISAC advisory on Black Basta was issued two days later. **Human factor.** One employee's mistaken trust in a downloaded file was enough to shut down patient-facing systems across a 142-hospital system for weeks — a mismatch between the blast radius a single workstation should be able to cause and the blast radius one actually caused.

```

**What happened.** Dell notified customers on May 9–10, 2024 that a threat actor had scraped approximately 49 million customer records — names, physical addresses, and order/warranty details for purchases going back to 2017 — from a portal Dell operates for resellers and channel partners. The attacker, using the handle "Menelik," emailed Dell on April 12 and 14 to report the underlying flaw, then posted the harvested data for sale on a hacking forum on April 28, days before Dell's own customer notification went out.

**Entry.** Menelik registered several accounts under fake company names on Dell's partner portal, which granted working API access within about two days with no verification that the "companies" were real. A script then generated sequential seven-digit Dell service tags and submitted them to the portal's order-lookup endpoint at up to 5,000 requests per minute.

Dell warned customers of likely follow-on tech-support scam calls using the leaked order details as social-engineering material.

**Human factor.** A partner-onboarding process that granted working API credentials to unverified, freshly registered "companies" within 48 hours, feeding an API with no rate limiting, turned a bug worth a quiet report into a 49-million-record breach before Dell ever heard from the person who found it.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-09 (the service tags were sequential and guessable, and nothing else gated the record behind them), AUTH-06 (rate limiting on the partner API would have capped enumeration long before 49 million records), DATA-11 (a bulk-export volume alert would have flagged 5,000 requests per minute well before three weeks passed).

```

**What happened.** Wiz Research disclosed on May 23, 2024 that it had found a critical vulnerability in Replicate, a hosted platform that runs AI models on customers' behalf, that could have let one customer read and manipulate another customer's private AI prompts, model outputs, and proprietary models. Wiz reported the flaw to Replicate in January 2024; Replicate shipped an initial mitigation within 24 hours and completed a full fix by February 20, 2024, three months before the research was made public.

**Entry.** Wiz's researchers used Replicate's own core feature against it: uploading a custom AI model packaged as a "Cog" container, the format Replicate uses to run customer-supplied models. A specially constructed container achieved code execution on Replicate's infrastructure the moment it was run — a model artifact behaving as designed on the surface, malicious underneath.

**Escalation.** From code execution inside their own container, the researchers found their workload shared a Kubernetes network namespace with neighboring customers' containers, letting them observe an unencrypted, already-authenticated connection to Replicate's shared Redis queue. By injecting traffic into that live connection, they could redirect other customers' prediction webhooks and intercept or alter other tenants' prompts and outputs — reaching across the tenant boundary from a single account signed up like any other customer.

**Blast radius.** Exploited at scale, the flaw could have exposed the AI prompts, outputs, and private models of every customer on Replicate's shared infrastructure. Replicate states it found no evidence the vulnerability was exploited by anyone other than the Wiz researchers who reported it, and that no customer action was required.

**Human factor.** None identified — this was an architecture decision (containers sharing a network namespace, an internal queue authenticated but not encrypted) rather than an individual mistake, found and fixed before it reached a customer.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between each customer's container, rather than a shared namespace), DATA-01 (default-deny isolation on the shared queue so one tenant's traffic cannot reach another's), DATA-12 (capping what a single compromised container could reach — here, every other customer's data on the platform).

**What happened.** In February 2024, the domain and GitHub account behind polyfill.io — a widely trusted JavaScript CDN originally built by developer Andrew Betts to patch older browsers' missing features — were sold to Funnull, a China-based CDN operator. Security firm Sansec published research on June 25, 2024 showing the new owners had modified the script served from cdn.polyfill.io to redirect mobile visitors to sports-betting and other scam sites, affecting more than 100,000 websites that linked to the script by hostname, including JSTOR, Intuit, and the World Economic Forum.

**Entry.** No intrusion was needed. The attack path was ownership of a domain name: sites that wrote a `<script src="cdn.polyfill.io/...">` tag were trusting whoever currently controlled that hostname, and when control changed hands, every one of those sites began serving the new owner's code automatically, with no code change on the sites' own end.

**Blast radius.** More than 100,000 sites served the malicious script to their visitors. The payload used deliberate evasion logic: it activated mainly for mobile visitors, varied by time of day, and avoided triggering for detected site administrators or when analytics tools were present, to delay discovery. Namecheap suspended the original domain on June 27, 2024; Cloudflare, Google, and Fastly stood up safe mirrored copies of the legitimate pre-acquisition script so affected sites could redirect away without code changes, and Google blocked ads on e-commerce sites still loading it.

**Human factor.** Thousands of engineering teams made the same reasonable-looking choice years earlier — link to a free, convenient CDN by its hostname — and none were still checking, years later, whether that hostname was still owned by whoever they had trusted when they added it. The lesson this quarter's framing draws out directly: these sites had pinned a hostname, not a version.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-06 (no script loaded from a URL you do not control — pinned or self-hosted is the direct fix), VENDOR-05 (third-party scripts on any page minimized, pinned to a specific version, or isolated), VENDOR-09 (periodically verifying what a bundled third-party component actually does at runtime, not just what it did when it was added).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- Snowflake, Mandiant, and CISA published coordinated guidance urging all customers to enable multi-factor authentication and network allow-lists on data-platform accounts, and to treat any credential ever seen in an infostealer log as compromised regardless of age — the direct response to the campaign behind three of this quarter's records (Santander, Ticketmaster, and AT&T's later-disclosed breach).
- CISA, the FBI, HHS, and MS-ISAC issued a dedicated #StopRansomware advisory on Black Basta (AA24-131A) days after the Ascension attack began, detailing the group's phishing, vishing/help-desk impersonation, and ConnectWise (CVE-2024-1709) techniques for defenders across critical infrastructure sectors.
- Cloudflare, Google, and Fastly responded to the Polyfill.io hijack by standing up safe mirror endpoints within days, and Google blocked ads on affected e-commerce sites — an infrastructure-level response that let many downstream sites recover without individually rewriting code.
- Rabbit's post-incident remediation — migrating secrets into AWS Secrets Manager, adding automated secret-scanning to code review, and shortening its vulnerability-disclosure window from 180 to 90 days — is a workable template for any hardware or AI product shipping client-side code with embedded backend credentials.
- Australia's privacy regulator (OAIC) closed its MediSecure inquiry without a full investigation but publicly used the case to back the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Bill 2024, aimed at strengthening enforcement powers under Australian Privacy Principle 11.
- Synnovis and NHS England sustained a national call for O-negative blood donations for months after the attack — a reminder that ransomware against a single mid-sized lab-services vendor can produce physical, not only data, consequences.
- Across this quarter's largest breaches, the pattern was rarely a novel exploit: it was a missing MFA requirement, an unpinned CDN hostname, an unrate-limited API, or a hardcoded key — controls that were available, well understood, and simply not applied.
