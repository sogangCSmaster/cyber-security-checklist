# 2024 Q1 — Stolen Credentials, Patient Supply-Chain Attacks

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/stolen-credentials — infostealer- and contractor-harvested logins, not novel exploits, opened this quarter's two largest breaches (Change Healthcare, Tangerine Telecom), while password-spraying and help-desk impersonation opened two more (Microsoft, France Travail)
> - **New technique this quarter:** a roughly two-year, patient social-engineering campaign against a single burned-out open-source maintainer (XZ Utils) — a supply-chain attack with a multi-year investment horizon rather than a single smash-and-grab exploit
> - **Recurring failure:** entry/unknown — AnyDesk, Fujitsu, the Almerys side of the French insurance breach, and Infosys McCamish Systems all disclosed impact without ever disclosing how the attacker first got in, even in their own statements
> - **Records in this file:** 13

```yaml
  - https://www.techtarget.com/searchsecurity/news/366573095/CISA-confirms-compromise-of-internal-Ivanti-systems
```

**What happened.** Ivanti disclosed on January 10, 2024 that two vulnerabilities in its Connect Secure and Policy Secure remote-access gateways — CVE-2023-46805 (authentication bypass) and CVE-2024-21887 (command injection) — were being actively exploited as a zero-day chain. A suspected China-nexus espionage actor was exploiting it narrowly as early as December 2023, according to Volexity's research; once technical details spread, a much broader set of opportunistic attackers joined in. CISA issued its first-ever Emergency Directive ordering every federal civilian agency to disconnect affected appliances, and in February confirmed two of its own internal systems had been compromised through the same flaws.

**Entry.** Chaining the two vulnerabilities let an unauthenticated attacker send a single crafted request to the appliance's internet-facing web interface and execute arbitrary commands — no credentials or prior access of any kind were required.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial chain. The command injection ran with enough privilege to plant a webshell and establish persistence directly on the appliance in one step.

**Blast radius.** Neither CISA nor Ivanti disclosed a total count of compromised devices or organizations. Confirmed victims include CISA itself: its Infrastructure Protection Gateway (interdependency data for US critical infrastructure) and Chemical Security Assessment Tool (private-sector chemical security plans) were both taken offline after compromise.

**Human factor.** Ivanti's own Integrity Checker Tool — the mechanism customers were told to use to confirm they had not been compromised — could itself be deceived by attackers who overwrote and time-stomped files to hide their tracks. CISA validated that the tool "is not sufficient to detect compromise" and that root-level persistence could survive even a factory reset, meaning organizations that checked and believed themselves clear were not.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems, and a rehearsed disconnect-if-unpatchable fallback — which is what CISA's Emergency Directive ultimately forced), OBSV-03 (a detection tool's own reliability has to be verified, not assumed — the Integrity Checker Tool's failure was itself the story), VENDOR-06 (actively monitoring vendor advisories and emergency directives rather than waiting for a standard patch cycle).

```yaml
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/ed-24-02-mitigating-significant-risk-nation-state-compromise-microsoft-corporate-email-system-closed
```

**What happened.** Microsoft disclosed on January 19, 2024 that the Russian state-sponsored group it tracks as Midnight Blizzard (also known publicly as APT29 / Cozy Bear / Nobelium) had accessed a small number of Microsoft corporate email accounts, including members of its senior leadership team and staff in its cybersecurity and legal functions, and exfiltrated emails and attachments — some of it Microsoft's own internal correspondence about Midnight Blizzard itself. Microsoft's security team detected the intrusion on January 12, roughly seven weeks after the attacker's first foothold.

**Entry.** Beginning in late November 2023, the attacker ran a password-spray attack — a small number of common passwords tried across many accounts — against a legacy, non-production test tenant account that had no MFA enabled.

**Escalation.** That forgotten test tenant held a legacy OAuth application that had been granted standing, elevated access into Microsoft's production corporate environment. The attacker used it to grant itself the `full_access_as_app` Exchange Online role — mailbox access across the tenant — and created additional malicious OAuth applications to preserve that access, turning one abandoned test account into a direct path into production email.

**Blast radius.** Microsoft said only "a very small percentage" of its corporate email accounts were accessed, and stated there was no evidence of access to customer environments, source code, or AI systems. CISA still issued Emergency Directive 24-02, requiring federal civilian agencies that had exchanged email with the compromised accounts or used the same class of OAuth applications to reset credentials and hunt for further activity — a single test-tenant compromise reaching well beyond Microsoft's own walls.

**Human factor.** A non-production tenant was allowed to keep both a live OAuth application with production-grade permissions and no MFA requirement, years after it should have been decommissioned or at minimum isolated from anything that could reach the real corporate environment.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (MFA on every account, including test and legacy ones, with no exceptions), CLOUD-06 (non-production environments carry production-grade auth or have no path into production at all), CRED-06 (an OAuth grant scoped to a test app's actual purpose, not tenant-wide mailbox access), AUTH-08 (an access inventory that would have found and killed this forgotten tenant and its elevated app long before November 2023).

```yaml
  - https://community.atlassian.com/forums/Trello-articles/Setting-the-record-straight-about-Trello-user-profile-data/ba-p/2587253
  - https://haveibeenpwned.com/Breach/Trello
```

**What happened.** A threat actor using the handle "emo" queried a public, unauthenticated Trello REST API endpoint with a list of roughly 500 million email addresses drawn from earlier, unrelated breach dumps. For every address that matched a real Trello account, the endpoint returned that account's public profile. The actor compiled 15,115,516 matched records and offered them for sale on a hacking forum; the dataset was later published in full and added to Have I Been Pwned on January 22, 2024.

**Entry.** The API endpoint let anyone submit an email address and get back whatever public profile Trello had on file for a matching account — full name, username, and account ID — with no authentication and no rate limit to stop hundreds of millions of sequential lookups.

**Escalation.** None required. The endpoint was designed to return exactly this data to any caller; large-scale collection was a function of the endpoint's own design, not a separate compromise.

**Blast radius.** 15,115,516 Trello accounts had their email address, username, and full name exposed and later published as a 21GB dataset. Atlassian said passwords and payment information were not involved, but correlating real names to email addresses at this scale fed subsequent phishing campaigns against the identified users.

**Human factor.** An API endpoint built for a legitimate single-user lookup was shipped without the authentication or rate-limiting that would ordinarily stop it being turned into a bulk-enumeration tool — nobody had asked "how many of these can be called in a row" before launch.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint should return user data, public profile or not), AUTH-06 (rate limiting and anomaly detection on lookup-style endpoints would have flagged hundreds of millions of sequential queries), DATA-11 (treating large-scale reads, not only writes, as an event worth alerting on).

```yaml
  - https://www.teiss.co.uk/news/massive-data-breach-affects-33-million-french-citizens-viamedis-and-almerys-targeted-13451
```

**What happened.** Viamedis and Almerys, two separate companies that process third-party payments (*tiers payant*) between supplementary health insurers and healthcare providers on behalf of dozens of French insurance brands, each disclosed a data breach at the start of February 2024. Because the two companies together handle claims data for most of France's health-insurance market, the combined exposure reached an estimated 33 million people — close to half the country's population — the largest breach disclosed in French history at that point.

**Entry.** Viamedis said the attacker used a phishing email to gain the access needed to reach its systems. Almerys said its core network was not breached directly; instead the attacker reached data through a portal Almerys operates for healthcare professionals to submit reimbursement claims. The specific technical mechanism used against that portal was never disclosed.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial access in either case. Both companies' core function is processing claims against the full insured-member database, so whichever foothold the attacker used to reach the claims-processing systems put them directly in front of the data at scale.

**Blast radius.** Names, dates of birth, French social security numbers, marital status, and each person's insurer and third-party-payment entitlements were exposed for approximately 33 million people combined. Both companies said banking details, medical information, and login credentials were not affected. France's data protection authority, CNIL, opened an investigation into both companies' security measures and GDPR compliance and required direct notification to every affected individual.

**Human factor.** Two subcontractors sitting deep in the insurance-claims supply chain each held a copy of a huge share of the national insured population's identity data — a concentration of risk that neither the insurers who contracted them nor regulators had forced into the open, until phishing at one and an undisclosed access issue at the other did it for them.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training and technical controls that reflect current targeted-phishing technique), AUTH-07 (professional-facing claim portals held behind their own strong authentication and full audit logging, separate from general account login), VENDOR-01 (an inventory, on the insurers' side, of every subprocessor holding this much concentrated identity data, with security requirements that scale to the concentration).

```yaml
  - https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/cloudflare-breach-stolen-okta/
```

**What happened.** Cloudflare disclosed on February 1, 2024 that, beginning November 14, 2023 and detected November 23 (Thanksgiving Day in the US), a threat actor it assessed as likely nation-state affiliated used credentials stolen during Okta's October 2023 support-system breach to access Cloudflare's internal Atlassian environment (Jira and Confluence), its Bitbucket source-code repositories, and part of its AWS environment, before being fully removed by November 24.

**Entry.** After Okta disclosed its October 2023 breach, Cloudflare rotated what it believed were all potentially exposed credentials — but missed one Moveworks service token and three service accounts (a Smartsheet credential providing Atlassian admin access, a Bitbucket credential, and an AWS credential) out of thousands rotated, because these specific ones were mistakenly believed to be unused. The attacker used the still-valid Moveworks token to gain initial access to Cloudflare's Atlassian systems on November 15, then used the Smartsheet credential to establish persistence.

**Escalation.** From the initial Atlassian foothold, the threat actor moved laterally to view 120 Bitbucket source-code repositories (downloading 76 of them), accessed a subset of Jira tickets (36 of just over 2 million) and Confluence pages (202 of about 194,000), reached Cloudflare's AWS Apps Marketplace environment, and unsuccessfully attempted to reach a console server in Cloudflare's São Paulo data center.

**Blast radius.** Cloudflare said no customer data, customer systems, the global network, data-center SSL keys, or customer databases were affected. Cloudflare's response, internally named "Code Red," rotated more than 5,000 production credentials, physically reimaged and rebooted every machine on its global network, forensically triaged 4,893 systems, and returned hardware from the São Paulo data center to its manufacturer for inspection — an effort that ran through January 5, 2024.

**Human factor.** Cloudflare's own account is explicit that this was not a new vulnerability in Cloudflare's own code — it was a rotation effort, undertaken in direct response to a known third-party breach, that missed four credentials out of thousands because they were assumed to be unused. The assumption, not the scale of the rotation effort, is what left a door open for seven weeks.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-09 (a rotation and revocation runbook thorough enough to inventory every credential touched by a third party, not just the ones believed to be in active use), CRED-07 (credentials that expire on their own rather than remaining valid indefinitely until someone remembers to revoke them), OBSV-07 (rehearsing the specific scenario of "credentials we hold are already known to be exposed" — exactly what Cloudflare was responding to when it missed these four).

```yaml
  - https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2024/02/05/anydesk-hacked/
  - https://thehackernews.com/2024/02/anydesk-hacked-popular-remote-desktop.html
```

**What happened.** AnyDesk, the German maker of widely used remote-desktop software, disclosed on February 2, 2024 that attackers had compromised its production systems. The company confirmed source code and its private code-signing certificate were stolen, and responded by revoking all security-related certificates, issuing new ones, resetting all web-portal passwords, and rebuilding affected systems with CrowdStrike's help.

**Entry.** Not disclosed. AnyDesk's public statements describe discovery and response but not how the attacker first got in; some press reporting placed the start of the intrusion as early as December 2023, a date AnyDesk itself never confirmed.

**Escalation.** Whatever access the attacker obtained reached both the source code repositories and the signing infrastructure that issues AnyDesk's code-signing certificate — the certificate that makes every AnyDesk installer trusted by Windows and endpoint security tools. Stealing it let the attacker sign arbitrary malicious binaries as genuine AnyDesk software.

**Blast radius.** AnyDesk revoked and replaced its code-signing certificate and said it found no evidence of end-user device compromise. Separately, around 18,000 AnyDesk account credentials appeared for sale on a dark-web forum days after the disclosure; AnyDesk said these did not appear to originate from its own breach — a reminder of how hard it can be for a vendor to separate its own incident from unrelated credential-stuffing noise during a live response.

**Human factor.** AnyDesk never said whether source-code access and code-signing access were meant to be reachable from the same compromise, which is itself the finding: for a vendor whose entire product is trusted remote access, the boundary between "an engineer's workstation was compromised" and "our software can now be signed by someone else" needs to be an explicit, tested one, not an assumption.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-09 (a rehearsed rotation-and-revocation runbook — which is what limited the damage once discovered), CICD-03 (build provenance and artifact signing kept separate enough from general engineering access that one compromise cannot reach both), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a disclosure clock, given how much of the public timeline here rests on outside reporting rather than AnyDesk's own account).

**What happened.** LockBit ransomware operators breached Infosys McCamish Systems (IMS), an Infosys subsidiary that provides insurance and deferred-compensation plan administration software to financial institutions, between October 29 and November 3, 2023, exfiltrating data before encrypting more than 2,000 of IMS's corporate systems. **What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of exactly what data every subprocessor like IMS holds, so a vendor breach's blast radius is known immediately, not discovered), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification-window requirements tight enough that a vendor's ransomware incident reaches affected customers in days, not months), OBSV-05 (a disclosure clock that starts the moment a vendor reports a breach, not the moment the receiving company finishes its own investigation).

**What happened.** On February 12, 2024, an ALPHV/BlackCat affiliate used compromised credentials to log into a Change Healthcare Citrix portal used for remote desktop access. The attacker moved laterally through Change Healthcare's environment for nine days, undetected, before deploying ransomware on February 21 that took down the systems Change Healthcare uses to process roughly one in three US patient records and a large share of the country's pharmacy and medical claims. UnitedHealth Group CEO Andrew Witty testified before two congressional committees on May 1, 2024, giving the fullest public account of the intrusion.

**Entry.** The Citrix portal did not have multi-factor authentication enabled. Witty told Congress the company had "relatively high confidence" the login credentials used were stolen by infostealer malware from a device and then sold or traded on a criminal marketplace, rather than obtained by directly attacking Change Healthcare's own systems.

**Escalation.** Once inside via the Citrix portal, the attacker moved laterally across Change Healthcare's network for nine days before triggering ransomware, giving them time to identify and exfiltrate high-value data ahead of encryption.

**Blast radius.** The individual notification count has been revised upward repeatedly: an initial placeholder of about 500 filed with HHS, then roughly 100 million, then approximately 190 million during 2024, and finally 192.7 million as of Change Healthcare's July 31, 2025 update to the HHS Office for Civil Rights breach portal — the largest healthcare breach disclosed in US history. Pharmacy claims processing, prior authorizations, and payment systems across the US healthcare system were disrupted for weeks. UnitedHealth confirmed paying a ransom, reported by multiple outlets via blockchain tracing at $22 million in bitcoin to the ALPHV affiliate.

**Human factor.** The Citrix portal had been part of Change Healthcare's environment since before UnitedHealth acquired the company in October 2022, and Witty testified that Change Healthcare's security practices — including this portal's lack of MFA — had not been brought up to UnitedHealth's own standard in the time since: an acquired system kept running on the acquired company's older posture rather than the acquirer's.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (MFA on every remote-access portal is table stakes, not a rollout in progress), CRED-03 (rotating credentials that could plausibly have passed through infostealer logs, rather than waiting for them to be used), VENDOR-07 (auditing and uplifting an acquired company's internet-facing systems to the parent's security baseline before, not years after, the acquisition closes).

**What happened.** JFrog's security research team published findings on February 29, 2024 that roughly 100 models hosted on the Hugging Face Hub — the most widely used public repository for sharing machine-learning models — contained malicious code that ran automatically the moment a data scientist loaded the model, not when it was queried or run. Hugging Face removed the flagged models after JFrog's disclosure; no specific victim compromise tied to these models was confirmed publicly.

**Entry.** The malicious models were built in PyTorch's native pickle-based serialization format and abused Python's `pickle` `__reduce__` method, which lets a serialized file specify a function to call during deserialization. A data scientist running the ordinary, expected command to load a downloaded model file was — invisibly — also executing attacker-chosen code, typically to open a reverse shell back to the attacker.

**Escalation.** None required. Deserializing the file was itself the code-execution event; no further vulnerability or privilege step was needed once a victim loaded the model.

**Blast radius.** JFrog identified about 100 malicious models before any mass exploitation was confirmed. Because Hugging Face hosts models used by major technology companies and individual researchers alike, and pickle-format models are downloaded and loaded largely on trust, the disclosure was treated as evidence of a systemic platform risk rather than a single contained incident.

**Human factor.** The ML ecosystem inherited Python's pickle format — designed for trusted, same-application data interchange, not for distributing files from strangers on the internet — as its default model-sharing format, on an open platform where anyone can publish a model that looks like any other.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-06 (never deserialize or evaluate untrusted data — the `safetensors` format Hugging Face has pushed as a safer default exists specifically to avoid this), DEPS-07 (automated scanning of uploaded artifacts for known-malicious patterns before they are servable, the gap JFrog's own scan was filling), DEPS-09 (treating an unfamiliar model publisher's history and reputation as a selection factor, the same discipline applied to code dependencies).

**What happened.** France's national unemployment agency, France Travail (formerly Pôle emploi), disclosed on March 13, 2024 that attackers had accessed its systems in an intrusion running from February 6 to March 5, exposing the personal data of up to 43 million current and former job seekers — anyone registered with the agency at any point over the preceding 20 years. Three French nationals, aged 21 to 23 at the time, were later arrested by Paris police.

**Entry.** The attackers called the IT administrators' help desk posing as advisors from Cap Emploi, a partner organization that supports job seekers with disabilities, and talked the help desk into resetting a Cap Emploi partner account's password — impersonation of a legitimate caller rather than a technical exploit.

**Human factor.** A help-desk password reset was granted on the strength of a phone call claiming to be a known partner organization, with no independent verification of the caller's identity before restoring access to an account that could reach tens of millions of records.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential or password reset that does not depend on the caller's own claim), AUTH-05 (account-recovery and help-desk paths held to the same strength as the login path itself, since they bypass it entirely when they work), DATA-01 (per-request, scoped authorization so a single partner-advisor account can reach only its own caseload, not the entire national database).

```yaml
date_occurred: "2024-02"

sector: [sector/tech]

entry: [entry/supply-chain-dependency]
escalation: [escalation/supply-chain-cascade]

scale: undisclosed
confidence: high
controls: [DEPS-09, DEPS-03, OBSV-06]
sources:
  - https://www.openwall.com/lists/oss-security/2024/03/29/4
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2024/03/29/reported-supply-chain-compromise-affecting-xz-utils-data-compression-library-cve-2024-3094
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XZ_Utils_backdoor
```

**What happened.** A backdoor tracked as CVE-2024-3094 was discovered on March 29, 2024 inside xz-utils, a compression library used by nearly every Linux distribution and, through liblzma, in the dependency chain of OpenSSH on systems that link against it. It was the endpoint of a social-engineering campaign that began around 2021, in which an account using the name "Jia Tan" spent more than two years building trust with Lasse Collin, xz-utils' sole, overworked maintainer — contributing genuine patches and, with the help of sockpuppet accounts pressuring Collin over his slow release cycle — before being granted co-maintainer access and eventually inserting the backdoor into release versions 5.6.0 and 5.6.1.

**Entry.** Obfuscated binary test files sat in the git repository but stayed inert there; the piece that actually spliced the malicious code into the build during compilation existed only in the maintainer-published release tarballs, not in the git history itself — so anyone auditing the public repository alone would not have seen how the backdoor was actually triggered.

**Escalation.** None required beyond distribution. Once a system built and installed a backdoored xz-utils release and that build's `sshd` was linked against the affected liblzma, the backdoor could intercept SSH authentication and grant a remote attacker holding a specific private key silent code execution, with nothing further to compromise on the target.

**Blast radius.** No confirmed victim exploitation was ever identified. The backdoored versions had reached the unstable and development branches of Debian, Fedora, openSUSE, and Arch, and a development snapshot of Ubuntu 24.04, but had not yet reached any distribution's stable production release when Andres Freund, a PostgreSQL developer, caught it after noticing SSH logins were taking roughly half a second longer than expected and causing unrelated Valgrind errors.

**Human factor.** Collin had said publicly, before the attack, that he was maintaining xz-utils essentially alone while dealing with long-term mental health strain. The social-engineering campaign specifically targeted that burnout, using sockpuppet pressure to make accepting an unfamiliar co-maintainer look like the reasonable choice rather than a red flag.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-09 (maintainer health and the bus-factor of a critical dependency treated as a real selection and support criterion, not an externality), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before rolling-release distributions pull a days-old version into anything more than a testing branch), OBSV-06 (Andres Freund's own report is the reason this worked — a half-second of extra SSH latency was enough for one engineer to pull the thread; the corpus-wide lesson is that anomalies this small are worth escalating).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA issued back-to-back emergency directives this quarter — ED 24-01 (Ivanti, January) and ED 24-02 (Microsoft/Midnight Blizzard, April, addressing the January breach) — compelling federal civilian agencies to act on vendor compromises outside the normal patch cycle.
- CISA publicly stated that Ivanti's own Integrity Checker Tool "is not sufficient to detect compromise," a rare acknowledgment from an agency that a vendor's own verification tool could not be trusted, and pushed disconnect-and-rebuild guidance over trust-and-verify.
- CISA published "Lessons from XZ Utils: Achieving a More Sustainable Open Source Ecosystem," using the near-miss to argue for funding and support of critical but under-resourced open-source maintainers rather than treating supply-chain risk as purely a scanning problem.
- Atlassian closed the unauthenticated Trello profile-lookup API and required authentication on the endpoint the scraper had abused.
- France's CNIL fined France Travail €5 million and opened GDPR investigations into Viamedis and Almerys, applying regulatory pressure specifically to help-desk identity verification and third-party payment-processor concentration risk.
- AnyDesk's full certificate revocation-and-reissue became a widely cited response template for code-signing compromise: treat the signing key as burned the moment source-code exposure is confirmed, not only once signing abuse is observed.
- Hugging Face expanded scanning of uploaded models for unsafe pickle-based serialization following JFrog's disclosure, part of a broader platform push toward the `safetensors` format as a safer default.
