# 2020 Q3 — Internal Tools Become the Target

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/vishing — voice-based impersonation talked its way past technical defenses at Twitter and, in a business-pretext variant, at Experian South Africa
> - **New technique this quarter:** none genuinely new in mechanism; the notable shift was aim — vishing and pretexting were pointed at internal admin and support tooling rather than at customer-facing logins
> - **Recurring failure:** high-privilege internal actions (account-recovery changes, bulk data exports, third-party app grants) proceeded with no second-person approval and no anomaly alerting
> - **Records in this file:** 10

## Incidents

  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Twitter_account_hijacking
```

**What happened.** On the afternoon of July 15, 2020, attackers took over 130 high-profile Twitter accounts — including Barack Obama, Joe Biden, Elon Musk, Apple, and Uber — and used 45 of them to post a bitcoin "double your money" scam that collected roughly $110,000–$118,000 from victims in about three hours before Twitter froze tweeting, password resets, and profile changes platform-wide to contain it. The attackers also read the private direct-message inboxes of 36 accounts and downloaded the full account archive, including private messages, for at least 8 accounts, among them one sitting member of parliament.

**Entry.** The attackers first used LinkedIn and other public sources to identify which Twitter employees were likely to hold access to internal support tools, then phoned them, posing as Twitter IT staff, and walked them through a fake VPN login page. When an employee entered credentials there, the attackers relayed them to Twitter's real VPN login in real time and also asked for the one-time code the employee received — defeating one-time-passcode MFA by simply asking the victim to hand it over.

**Escalation.** With VPN access, the attackers reached Twitter's internal account-support tool. Per the New York State Department of Financial Services' investigation, that tool let an authorized support employee view account details and change the email address tied to an account — the single step needed to trigger an ordinary password reset — with no second person's sign-off, no hardware-bound MFA step-up, and no real-time alert to a security team for doing it. No further technical escalation was required: the internal tool was already the crown jewels.

**Blast radius.** 130 accounts were accessed, 45 were used to post the scam, 36 had their DMs read, and at least 8 had their full account archives downloaded. Twitter restricted all employee access to internal tools for days while rebuilding the process around it. Graham Ivan Clark (17 at the time) pleaded guilty as a Florida youthful offender and was sentenced to three years; Mason Sheppard and Nima Fazeli also pleaded guilty; UK national Joseph James O'Connor was extradited and sentenced in 2023 for his role.

**Human factor.** Employee security training covered phishing email, not a phone call that used accurate internal jargon and correctly targeted people known to hold tool access — and the tool itself was built for support-staff speed, on the assumption that anyone who reached it over VPN was automatically trustworthy. The NY DFS report also noted Twitter had cut security staff and had no permanent CISO in the months leading up to the attack.

**What would have stopped it.** Putting admin and account-support tooling behind separate, harder authentication with mandatory two-person approval before any action that changes account-recovery details (AUTH-07, HUMAN-02); and centrally logged, alerting monitoring of internal-tool usage, so a burst of unrelated account-email changes across high-profile accounts is caught before it reaches production tweets, not after (OBSV-01).

**What happened.** Blackbaud, whose donor-management and CRM software runs behind the scenes for universities, hospital foundations, and charities worldwide, detected a ransomware attack in May 2020, paid the attackers to delete a copy of data exfiltrated before systems were encrypted, and publicly disclosed the incident on July 16, 2020. Blackbaud's initial notice said bank account details and Social Security numbers had not been accessed; its own staff learned within days that this was untrue for at least some customers, and the correction did not reach the public until much later.

**Entry.** Blackbaud has never publicly detailed how the attacker first gained access, describing the intrusion only as unauthorized access to its "self-hosted environment."

**Escalation.** The attacker moved from that initial, undisclosed foothold to exfiltrating a subset of customer data and deploying ransomware across Blackbaud's environment before detection and containment — a reach consistent with substantial lateral movement inside a shared multi-tenant platform, though Blackbaud has not published the path it took.

**Blast radius.** Blackbaud said more than 13,000 customer organizations worldwide were affected, including at least 20 universities and charities named in UK, US, Dutch, and Canadian filings; exposed data included names and contact details for most, and for a subset, bank account information, Social Security numbers, and usernames/passwords. In 2023 the SEC fined Blackbaud $3 million and Blackbaud separately paid $49.5 million to settle with 49 US states and DC, both tied to the disclosure conduct rather than the intrusion itself; the FTC's 2024 settlement required data-minimization and retention changes without an additional fine.

**Human factor.** Blackbaud's own teams learned within days that the "no bank/SSN access" statement was wrong, but that correction never reached senior management or the public in time — the disclosure-control failure the SEC order treats as the core violation. Underneath it, Blackbaud was still holding years of donor financial data that a subset of customers no longer had any active need for.

**What would have stopped it.** Encrypting sensitive donor fields at rest and enforcing retention limits, so years-old bank and Social Security data isn't sitting in a live, reachable database long after collection (DATA-07, DATA-09); and an incident-communication process that routes a "the earlier statement was wrong" finding to whoever owns public disclosure within days, not into a subsequent quarterly filing (OBSV-05).

  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GEDmatch
```

**What happened.** On Sunday, July 19, 2020, genetic-genealogy site GEDmatch — used by hobbyist genealogists and, controversially since 2018, by law enforcement to identify suspects through DNA relative-matching — had the privacy settings on its roughly 1.4 million user profiles reset for about three hours, making profiles that had specifically opted out of law-enforcement matching visible to it again. A second, separate incident soon after briefly exposed some users' raw DNA data to other genealogists who should not have been able to see it. GEDmatch, owned by forensic-genomics company Verogen, took the site offline twice to contain the two incidents.

**Entry.** GEDmatch and Verogen never publicly detailed the technical mechanism that let someone reset opt-out flags site-wide; contemporaneous reporting described it only as a "security breach," without a confirmed vector, which is why this record uses entry/unknown rather than guessing.

**Escalation.** None required — whatever access was obtained was sufficient on its own to flip privacy settings across the user base, consistent with either a compromised account that already held administrative-level privileges or a flaw in how profile visibility was enforced, with no further pivot described in the public record.

**Blast radius.** Effectively GEDmatch's full contemporaneous user base of roughly 1.4 million profiles was briefly reachable for law-enforcement-style matching regardless of individual opt-out choices, and an undisclosed smaller number of users had raw genetic data exposed to people who should not have seen it. No financial loss was reported; the harm is the exposure itself, in a data category that cannot be reset the way a password can.

**Human factor.** Genetic-genealogy sites had been a known target for exactly this kind of access since GEDmatch's 2019 reversal on law-enforcement matching made the opt-in/opt-out flag itself valuable to compromise; the incident suggests that value was never matched with controls — MFA, rate-limiting, anomaly detection — proportional to holding a permanent, unchangeable dataset.

**What would have stopped it.** Phishing-resistant MFA on any account able to touch privacy defaults at scale (AUTH-01); rate limiting and breached-password screening on login and admin surfaces (AUTH-06); and alerting on abnormal bulk changes to privacy flags, a pattern with no legitimate high-volume use case (OBSV-02).

controls: [CLOUD-08, CLOUD-01, OBSV-02]
sources:
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa20-206a
```

**What happened.** F5 patched a critical remote-code-execution flaw (CVE-2020-5902) in the Traffic Management User Interface of its widely deployed BIG-IP application-delivery controllers on June 30, 2020. A working proof-of-concept was public within days, and by July 6 CISA was observing widespread scanning and exploitation attempts against both federal and commercial BIG-IP devices, warning that any unpatched, internet-reachable device should be assumed compromised.

**Entry.** The flaw let an unauthenticated attacker who could reach the management interface execute arbitrary system commands, create or delete files, and run arbitrary Java code — full compromise with no credentials needed, against an admin interface that many organizations had left reachable from the open internet instead of a restricted management network.

**Escalation.** CISA's own incident responders, investigating two confirmed compromises, found attackers using the device compromise to move further into victim networks rather than stopping at the BIG-IP box itself, since these devices commonly sit in front of VPNs and internal applications and can observe credentials passing through.

**Blast radius.** CISA confirmed at least two compromises during its own response work and described exploitation as widespread across government and private-sector networks alike; the total number of devices actually breached, as opposed to merely scanned, was never centrally disclosed.

**Human factor.** The month between patch availability (June 30) and CISA's public alert (July 24) is the whole story: enough administrators had not applied a known, actively exploited fix on an internet-facing admin interface for the agency to treat it as a national-level emergency.

**What would have stopped it.** Keeping admin interfaces off the public internet entirely, reachable only over a VPN or management network (CLOUD-08); routine enumeration of what is actually internet-reachable, so an exposed TMUI instance doesn't go unnoticed (CLOUD-01); and alerting on the command-execution and file-write behavior CISA's indicators of compromise described, rather than relying on patching alone (OBSV-02).

```

**What happened.** A ransomware attack on July 23, 2020 took down Garmin Connect, flyGarmin (used by pilots for flight planning and aviation-database updates), Garmin's call centers, and parts of its production systems for several days. Researchers identified the payload as WastedLocker, a ransomware family used exclusively by the Evil Corp threat group; Garmin never confirmed publicly whether it paid, though reporting citing incident responders put a ransom demand around $10 million, an unconfirmed figure this record does not treat as fact.

**Entry.** Garmin has not disclosed how the attackers first gained access.

**Escalation.** The outage spanned customer-facing services (Connect, the website), aviation-specific systems (flyGarmin and aviation-database downloads pilots rely on for legal flight operations), call centers, and manufacturing — a breadth suggesting the attacker reached widely across Garmin's internal network before deploying ransomware, rather than being contained to one segment.

**Blast radius.** Millions of Garmin device users worldwide lost the ability to sync fitness and activity data for several days; pilots could not download current navigation data, a safety-relevant disruption. Garmin said it found no indication that customer personal or payment information had been accessed or stolen.

**Human factor.** Whatever the initial foothold, a single ransomware deployment simultaneously taking down consumer web services, aviation data feeds, and call-center systems points to a flat, under-segmented internal network, where compromising one part of Garmin put unrelated business lines at risk together.

**What would have stopped it.** Network segmentation keeping aviation-safety systems, consumer-facing services, and back-office systems on separate trust boundaries, so one ransomware deployment cannot reach all three (CLOUD-05); backups isolated from production credentials and regularly restore-tested, so recovery doesn't depend on negotiating with the attacker (DATA-08); and a rehearsed incident plan that lets the company state clearly and quickly what was and wasn't accessed (OBSV-05).

sources:
  - https://databreaches.net/2020/08/12/sans-infosec-training-org-suffers-data-breach-after-phishing-attack/
```

**What happened.** SANS Institute, a well-known cybersecurity training and certification organization, disclosed that a phishing email led an employee to install a malicious Office 365 add-in, which quietly added a rule forwarding incoming mail matching certain patterns to an external address. Roughly 28,000 records of personal information belonging to SANS students and members were exposed before the rule was found and removed.

**Entry.** A single successful phishing email was the entire entry path: it did not harvest a password directly, but induced the employee to grant a third-party mail add-in permission to read and forward their mail, functioning like an unreviewed application-access grant.

**Escalation.** None required — the forwarding rule itself was the payload, silently exfiltrating matching mail for as long as it went unnoticed, with no further technical pivot needed.

**Blast radius.** About 28,000 records of PII belonging to SANS's own community — a pointed target, given SANS's business is training people to catch exactly this — were forwarded out before detection; SANS published indicators of compromise and notified affected individuals.

**Human factor.** One employee's decision to grant a plausible-looking mail add-in permission — a low-friction action that doesn't look like "entering a password on a fake site" — was sufficient on its own, and it is exactly the kind of consent-based phishing that credential-focused training tends to miss.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating a new mail add-in or connected-app grant as an access decision requiring review, the same as a new OAuth application (VENDOR-03); security-awareness training that explicitly covers consent-based and add-in phishing, not just credential-harvesting pages (HUMAN-05); and logging and alerting on new mailbox-forwarding rules, which are rare in legitimate use and a well-known exfiltration technique (OBSV-01).

**What happened.** A fraudster contacted Experian's South African operation posing as a representative of a legitimate client seeking a data and analytics engagement, and was able to obtain a large extract of Experian's consumer and business database — described afterward as South Africa's largest data breach to date, covering an estimated 24 million individuals and close to 800,000 business entities, with financial details compromised for roughly 24,838 of those businesses. Experian initially said the incident was contained; that claim did not hold up under further scrutiny, and South African authorities later pursued at least one conviction tied to the case.

**Entry.** No malware or system intrusion was involved. The fraudster impersonated a legitimate business counterpart closely enough, over what public reporting describes as an extended business-development interaction, to convince Experian staff to hand over a bulk data extract as part of what looked like a normal commercial engagement. The exact channel was not fully detailed in public reporting; it is recorded here under entry/vishing as the closest defined category — voice- or contact-based impersonation from the attacker's side — rather than a technical exploit, and that imprecision is noted rather than guessed away.

**Escalation.** None required — the pretext itself produced the full extract in a single transaction; there was no system to break into.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 24 million people and hundreds of thousands of businesses had personal and, for a subset, financial information disclosed to a fraudster whose subsequent handling of the data was not fully made public.

**Human factor.** A large, valuable data export was released on the strength of a plausible business story, with no independent verification of the requester's identity or authority, and without the export itself being logged as the kind of high-risk action that should require a second approver.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating a bulk data export as a privileged, logged, alerting action in its own right, not just another client request (DATA-11); requiring a second person's sign-off before an extract of this size leaves the organization (HUMAN-02); and verifying a requester's identity and authority independently of what the requester themselves claims — the same standard normally applied to a credential reset (HUMAN-01).

```

**What happened.** On September 13, 2020, automated attackers compromised and planted payment-card-skimming code on more than 2,000 online stores still running Magento 1 within a matter of hours — described at the time as the largest single-day Magecart campaign yet recorded. Magento 1 had reached official end-of-life on June 30, 2020, meaning no security patches had been issued for the platform for over two months when the campaign hit.

**Entry.** The campaign exploited known, unpatched vulnerabilities in the Magento 1 core — flaws that would ordinarily be fixed, but for which no fix existed anymore because the platform was out of support — against merchants who had not migrated to Magento 2 or another platform by the end-of-life date.

**Escalation.** Compromise of the outdated, no-longer-patched Magento 1 codebase directly gave attackers server-side access sufficient to insert skimmer code into checkout pages; the deprecated platform itself was the path past whatever other controls a given store had.

**Blast radius.** Over 2,000 stores were compromised in the initial single-day wave, each exposing the payment-card details of any customer who checked out while the skimmer was active; the campaign continued in smaller waves as more end-of-life stores were found. No aggregate card-fraud total was ever centrally published.

**Human factor.** Thousands of small and mid-sized merchants kept running software their vendor had stopped patching months earlier, typically because migrating an e-commerce platform is costly and disruptive — a widespread, foreseeable version of "we'll migrate later" that turned an announced end-of-life date into a mass-casualty event.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating an approaching platform end-of-life as a tracked, owned migration with a deadline, so unsupported software doesn't quietly stay in production processing payments (CLOUD-09); and a content security policy restricting what scripts checkout pages can load or send data to, so injected skimmer code can't run or exfiltrate even after the server itself is compromised (VENDOR-05, INPUT-03).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- The New York State Department of Financial Services' October 2020 "Twitter Investigation Report" recommended regulated entities require phishing-resistant authentication and strict, monitored, two-person-approval controls on any internal tool able to reset account access — naming, in effect, the exact gap Twitter's support tool had.
- CISA's Activity Alert AA20-206A (July 24, 2020) pushed federal agencies and the public to patch F5 BIG-IP CVE-2020-5902 immediately and published indicators of compromise after confirming active, widespread exploitation of unpatched devices.
- Secura's public disclosure of the Zerologon vulnerability (CVE-2020-1472) in September 2020, following Microsoft's initial August patch, led CISA to issue Emergency Directive 20-04 on September 18, ordering all federal civilian agencies to patch domain controllers within days — anticipating the ransomware-affiliated exploitation of Zerologon that followed in Q4.
- Adobe and the Magento community had warned throughout 2020 that Magento 1's June 30 end-of-life meant no further security patches and lapsed PCI DSS compliance; the September mass-compromise campaign made that warning concrete and drove renewed urgency around forced migration to a supported platform.
- The SEC's Blackbaud enforcement action (finalized 2023 but rooted in this quarter's disclosures) put public companies on notice that walking back an inaccurate breach statement internally, without correcting it externally, is itself a disclosure-control violation distinct from the breach.
- The GEDmatch incidents renewed scrutiny of whether opt-out privacy flags on genetic-genealogy platforms deserve protections comparable to account credentials, given that exposed genetic data cannot be reset the way a password can.
