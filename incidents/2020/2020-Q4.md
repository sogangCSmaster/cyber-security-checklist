# 2020 Q4 — Trust Exploited at Scale

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-build — the SolarWinds Orion build-system compromise (SUNBURST) reached roughly 18,000 organizations through one legitimately signed update; most other records in this file used more ordinary paths in, but this is the story that defined the quarter's response.
> - **New technique this quarter:** Golden SAML (escalation/token-forgery) — attackers stole SAML token-signing certificates to mint their own valid authentication tokens, satisfying password and MFA checks at once for federated cloud services.
> - **Recurring failure:** entry vectors went undisclosed almost as often as they were named, and where a mechanism was known it was rarely novel — a blank database password, a vished support agent, a cloud credential pasted into chat, a patch that had been available for months.
> - **Records in this file:** 8

```yaml
scale: undisclosed
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: high
controls: [CLOUD-01, AUTH-01, CLOUD-10]
sources:
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa20-283a
```

**What happened.** Through 2020, one or more advanced persistent threat actors systematically scanned for and exploited a known set of vulnerabilities — each with a vendor patch already available, but left unapplied — in internet-facing VPN and remote-access appliances used by U.S. state, local, tribal, and territorial (SLTT) government networks. CISA's advisory (first issued October 9, 2020, revised October 24) described the activity only as "APT actors" without naming a specific group, and noted that in some cases it reached systems supporting election administration. The campaign's distinguishing feature was combining several unrelated, long-patched CVEs into one reusable playbook rather than relying on a single exploit.

**Entry.** Initial access came through whichever of several unpatched, internet-facing appliances a target happened to be running: Fortinet FortiOS SSL VPN path traversal (CVE-2018-13379), MobileIron MDM remote code execution (CVE-2020-15505), Citrix Application Delivery Controller (CVE-2019-19781), Pulse Secure VPN (CVE-2019-11510), F5 BIG-IP (CVE-2020-5902), and Palo Alto GlobalProtect (CVE-2020-2021) are all named in the advisory, each with a vendor patch that had been publicly available for months before exploitation.

**Escalation.** Once inside, the actors used the Windows Netlogon privilege-escalation flaw known as Zerologon (CVE-2020-1472) — patched by Microsoft roughly two months earlier — to go from a low-level foothold to domain-level control, then moved laterally to reach systems that in some jurisdictions supported voter registration or other election-administration functions.

**Blast radius.** CISA did not disclose how many organizations were affected or name specific victims, stating only that the activity was "often, but not exclusively" directed at SLTT networks and had in some cases resulted in unauthorized access to elections-support systems. The agency found no evidence that vote counts, voter registration data, or other election data had been altered.

**Human factor.** Every vulnerability in the chain had a vendor patch available before it was used against these networks; the failure was not a novel technique but months of unpatched, internet-facing infrastructure inside resource-constrained government IT organizations.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (regularly enumerating what is actually reachable from the internet would have flagged the unpatched appliances), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA so a single compromised account does not equal domain control), and CLOUD-10 (a maximum patch-application SLA for internet-facing systems, tracked like any other production asset), which this incident argues for directly.

### I2020Q4-02 · Vastaamo

```yaml

entry: [entry/public-database]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/phi, impact/pii, impact/extortion]
factor: [factor/misconfiguration, factor/no-encryption]
scale: 36000 patients
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: medium
controls: [DATA-04, DATA-07, CLOUD-01]
sources:
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vastaamo_data_breach
```

**What happened.** Vastaamo, a private psychotherapy provider operating roughly two dozen clinics across Finland, had its patient records database accessed without authorization in November 2018 and again in March 2019; the access went unnoticed for close to two years. In autumn 2020 the intruder contacted Vastaamo demanding about 40 bitcoin (roughly €450,000) to not publish the stolen therapy notes, then — when the company did not pay — began publishing patient records in batches on Tor, and from around October 21, 2020 emailed individual patients directly, demanding €200–500 in bitcoin from each of tens of thousands of people or their session notes, including notes describing infidelity, suicide attempts, and other highly sensitive disclosures, would be published.

**Entry.** The patient database was reachable over the network without a firewall restricting access to it, and its root database account had no password configured at all — a state that had persisted since at least the November 2018 intrusion.

**Escalation.** None required: an unauthenticated, internet-reachable database with an open root account gave immediate read and write access to the entire patient record set, including unencrypted verbatim therapist session notes.

**Blast radius.** Vastaamo's own cited figure is about 36,000 patients plus roughly 400 staff records, though other reporting has put the number of affected patients as low as 25,000; the discrepancy likely reflects the two separate access windows touching different slices of the data, so this record uses Vastaamo's figure with medium confidence. Finland's data protection authority later fined Vastaamo €608,000 for GDPR violations, and the company was driven into bankruptcy.

**Human factor.** Vastaamo stored some of the most sensitive personal data imaginable — word-for-word psychotherapy notes — on a database that was never encrypted and was left reachable with no administrative password, a basic control gap that predated both intrusions.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases not reachable from the public internet), DATA-07 (sensitive fields encrypted at rest), CLOUD-01 (routine enumeration of internet-reachable assets would have caught the exposed database years before it was exploited).

```yaml
sources:

  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capcom
```

**What happened.** On November 2, 2020, Capcom discovered that ransomware had encrypted data across its internal networks. The company confirmed the Ragnar Locker group was responsible, that files had been copied before encryption, and that it had received a ransom message; Capcom declined to pay, and the group later began publishing stolen files online.

**Entry.** Capcom's public disclosures did not name an initial access vector, stating only that the incident followed "unauthorized access to its network."

**Escalation.** Not detailed by Capcom, though the ransomware reaching systems across the company's Japanese, North American, and other regional operations indicates lateral movement from a single foothold to a broad swath of internal infrastructure before detonation.

**Blast radius.** Capcom said up to approximately 350,000 records were potentially affected across several categories: roughly 134,000 Japanese customer-support contacts, 40,000 shareholder records, 28,000 former-employee and family records, 125,000 job-applicant records, 14,000 current-employee HR records, 14,000 North American store members, and 4,000 esports-site members. Capcom said no credit-card data was involved, since card processing was outsourced to a third party, but confirmed some unreleased game-development material was also taken. The company did not disclose whether or how much ransom was paid.

**Human factor.** Capcom was unusually transparent about the scope of affected records — breaking the count down by category and region — but never identified how the intrusion began, leaving the most actionable lesson for other defenders incomplete.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal read/export volume, which should fire well before ransomware finishes encrypting systems across multiple regions), DATA-08 (backups that are restore-tested and not writable by the same production credentials an attacker would already control), CLOUD-05 (network segmentation between regional operations to contain lateral movement).


**What happened.** Around the Halloween weekend in early November 2020, the Ragnar Locker ransomware group encrypted data across 24 of Davide Campari-Milano's servers worldwide, disrupting IT systems at the spirits company. Campari confirmed the intrusion and encryption; Ragnar Locker demanded $15 million in cryptocurrency to decrypt the systems and not publish stolen files.

**Entry.** Campari's public statements did not disclose an initial access vector.

**Escalation.** Not detailed publicly, though reaching 24 servers across the company's global operations again implies lateral movement from a single foothold rather than a directly exposed crown-jewel system.

**Blast radius.** Campari confirmed encryption and acknowledged the possibility that data had been exfiltrated but did not itself detail what was taken. Ragnar Locker separately claimed on its own leak site to have stolen roughly 2TB of files, including bank statements, Social Security numbers, tax forms, contracts, and passport details; this is an extortion claim, not an independently confirmed scale, which is why this record's confidence is medium rather than high. Campari made no public statement about whether it paid the ransom, instead describing efforts to rebuild services on multiple sites and add security controls against reinfection.

**Human factor.** As with Capcom, the entry vector was never made public, which limits what other organizations can learn from the incident beyond the broad shape of the extortion playbook — encrypt broadly, exfiltrate first, threaten publication.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-02 (export/read-volume alerting), DATA-08 (isolated, restore-tested backups), CLOUD-05 (segmentation to prevent one compromised host from reaching two dozen servers worldwide).


**What happened.** The criminal group ShinyHunters gained access to WildWorks' internal Slack workspace and, inside it, found long-lived AWS credentials that gave direct access to the backend databases behind Animal Jam, a children's virtual world with more than 130 million registered accounts. WildWorks says the intrusion began around October 12, 2020, and learned of it only when the attackers made contact after already copying the data; the company disclosed the breach publicly on November 11, 2020.

**Entry.** How the attackers first got into WildWorks' Slack workspace was not disclosed by the company.

**Escalation.** Once inside Slack, the attackers found AWS credentials that had been shared or stored there in plaintext — the same failure pattern as a credential left on a wiki or shared drive — and those credentials were scoped broadly enough to reach production databases directly, requiring no further privilege escalation.

**Blast radius.** Around 46 million player usernames and 46 million SHA-1 hashed-and-salted passwords were taken, along with roughly 7 million parent email addresses, signup-time IP addresses, gender and birthdate fields for a subset of accounts, and 116 older (2010-era) records containing parents' names and billing addresses. WildWorks said children's real names and plaintext passwords were not exposed, and it required password resets and referred the matter to the FBI.

**Human factor.** A long-lived, broadly scoped cloud credential was reachable inside an internal chat tool — a secret treated as disposable conversational text rather than as a production credential deserving the same handling as a password.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no secret in any reachable location, extended explicitly to chat and collaboration tools, not only source and config), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job and one resource, so a single leaked key cannot reach the entire database layer), CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime from a manager rather than shared as static text anyone with channel access can read).

```yaml
entry: [entry/vishing]
escalation: [escalation/admin-tooling]

tech: [tech/dns]
scale: undisclosed
cost_usd: undisclosed
dwell_days: unknown
confidence: high
controls: [HUMAN-01, AUTH-05, AUTH-07]
sources:
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2020/11/godaddy-employees-used-in-attacks-on-multiple-cryptocurrency-services/
```

**What happened.** Starting around November 13, 2020, criminals used voice-based social engineering against GoDaddy support staff to gain control of DNS and account settings for domains belonging to several cryptocurrency trading platforms, including Liquid.com and NiceHash, redirecting email and web traffic for those domains. KrebsOnSecurity first reported the incidents publicly on November 21, 2020.

**Entry.** Attackers contacted GoDaddy's support staff and, posing as the legitimate domain owners, talked their way into changes to domain and account records — the same voice-impersonation ("vishing") pattern that had already been used against hosting customers of registrars earlier in 2020.

**Escalation.** No further escalation was required beyond the access GoDaddy's own support tooling already granted: once an agent was convinced to make the change, the attacker had the same control over the domain the legitimate owner would have had.

**Blast radius.** At least five cryptocurrency platforms were affected — Liquid.com, NiceHash, Bibox, Celsius Network, and Wirex. NiceHash froze customer withdrawals as a precaution and said no funds were stolen; other affected platforms did not publicly detail losses. GoDaddy said a "limited number" of its employees were targeted, and that it locked down the affected accounts, reverted the changes, and assisted affected customers.

**Human factor.** The weak point was not the cryptocurrency platforms' own security but GoDaddy's account-recovery and support process, which a convincing phone call was apparently enough to satisfy — the same identity-verification gap that makes registrars and other account-recovery paths attractive third-party targets no matter how well the end customer secures their own systems.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any account or credential change, independent of the caller's own claims), AUTH-05 (account-recovery and support paths held to the same strength as the login path itself), AUTH-07 (support tooling capable of changing DNS or ownership sitting behind separate authentication and full audit logging).

**What happened.** On December 8, 2020, FireEye disclosed that a nation-state actor had broken into its network and stolen the Red Team assessment tools the company uses to simulate attacks for its own customers. FireEye said it had found no evidence the tools had yet been used elsewhere but released detection countermeasures as a precaution. FireEye's own investigation, made public five days later, traced the intrusion to a trojanized update to SolarWinds' Orion monitoring software — the discovery that triggered the broader SUNBURST investigation covered in this file's SolarWinds record (I2020Q4-08).

**Entry.** FireEye has said its security team identified the intrusion after an employee noticed an unrecognized second multi-factor-authentication device had been registered to their VPN account and reported it. The underlying access had come through the same trojanized Orion update that FireEye, like roughly 18,000 other SolarWinds customers, had installed months earlier.

**Escalation.** Consistent with the broader campaign, the actor is understood to have used stolen credentials and forged SAML authentication tokens to move beyond the initial Orion-granted foothold and reach the systems holding FireEye's Red Team tooling.

**Blast radius.** FireEye did not disclose a specific count of stolen tools or any dollar figure; it described the theft as covering a range of Red Team techniques, published detection rules for the community, and reported the intrusion to the FBI and Microsoft — the joint investigation that surfaced the SolarWinds compromise days later.

**Human factor.** FireEye's own detection worked as intended — an employee noticed an anomalous MFA enrollment and reported it — but that catch came only after the attacker had already been inside for months via a fully trusted, digitally signed software update no one had reason to distrust.

**What would have stopped it.** OBSV-01 (centrally logged authentication and MFA-enrollment events, which is literally what surfaced this intrusion), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party — including a monitoring tool with broad network visibility — holding meaningful access to your environment), CLOUD-05 (segmentation limiting what a compromised monitoring platform can actually reach).

### I2020Q4-08 · SolarWinds (SUNBURST)

```yaml

  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa20-352a
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_federal_government_data_breach
```

**What happened.** Attackers — later attributed by the FBI and other U.S. agencies to Russia's SVR foreign intelligence service (tracked as APT29/Cozy Bear, and referred to as UNC2452/Dark Halo before attribution) — compromised SolarWinds' software build environment and inserted a backdoor, later named SUNBURST, into the Orion IT-monitoring platform's source before it was compiled and digitally signed. SolarWinds said in a regulatory filing that fewer than 18,000 customers downloaded one of the trojanized Orion versions (2019.4 through 2020.2.1 HF1), distributed between March and June 2020; a much smaller number, publicly estimated at around 100 organizations, were selected by the attackers for a hands-on-keyboard second stage. Confirmed victims ultimately included the U.S. Treasury, Commerce (NTIA), State, Homeland Security, Justice, Energy, and Labor departments, the federal courts' case-management system, Microsoft, and FireEye, whose December 8 breach disclosure (I2020Q4-07) led directly to this campaign's discovery.

**Entry.** The backdoor was inserted upstream of any individual customer relationship: attackers manipulated SolarWinds' own build pipeline (built on JetBrains TeamCity) so that legitimate, properly signed Orion updates shipped with SUNBURST already embedded. Every downstream organization that installed the update was compromised the moment it did what security guidance normally recommends — trust and apply a vendor's signed patch.

**Escalation.** Where attackers wanted deeper access, they harvested SAML token-signing certificates from compromised on-premises Active Directory Federation Services servers and used them to forge their own valid SAML authentication tokens — a technique known as Golden SAML — letting them impersonate any user, including highly privileged ones, to Microsoft 365 and Azure Active Directory without ever needing that user's password or triggering MFA, since a forged token satisfies both checks at once. CISA's advisory noted this access, once established, survived even after the Orion backdoor itself was removed.

**Blast radius.** Roughly 18,000 organizations received the compromised update; operational damage concentrated in a much smaller set of high-value government and corporate networks where the actor deployed further tooling. U.S. officials described at least eight to nine months of undetected access before FireEye's own breach investigation surfaced it in December 2020. No total remediation cost has been publicly confirmed for the campaign as a whole, so this record leaves cost_usd undisclosed rather than repeat any single company's later, partial estimate.

**Human factor.** Every downstream victim did exactly what is normally recommended — applied a vendor's signed update promptly — and that update was the attack. The organizational failure sits upstream: SolarWinds' build environment was treated as internal tooling rather than as a production system deserving its own monitoring, and victim networks alike granted Orion's service account far more standing privilege than a monitoring tool needs.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-08 (treating the build system itself as production and monitoring it accordingly, which might have caught months of unauthorized build tampering), CICD-03 (build-provenance and reproducibility checks that catch a difference between source and shipped artifact even when the signature is valid), AUTH-04 (short-lived, revocable sessions that limit how long a forged SAML token stays useful), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed "a vendor we trust was compromised" response plan — CISA's emergency directive was effectively the federal government improvising that plan in real time).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA Emergency Directive 21-01 (December 13, 2020) ordered every Federal Civilian Executive Branch agency to disconnect or power down SolarWinds Orion immediately, forensically image affected systems, reset credentials used by the software, and report findings by noon the next day — the first emergency directive of its kind issued in direct response to a live supply-chain compromise. https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/ed-21-01-mitigate-solarwinds-orion-code-compromise
- CISA Alert AA20-352A gave defenders concrete indicators for detecting Golden SAML abuse — unusual SAML token lifetimes, tokens with identical issue and use timestamps, and tokens missing expected MFA claims — turning a previously obscure technique into something SOC teams could actually hunt for. https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa20-352a
- FireEye published detection countermeasures for its own stolen Red Team tools within days of its December 8 disclosure, giving the wider security community signatures before the tools could plausibly be reused against other targets.
- CISA/FBI/MS-ISAC advisory AA20-283A pushed state and local governments to patch a specific list of actively chained VPN and remote-access vulnerabilities (Fortinet, Pulse Secure, Citrix ADC, F5 BIG-IP, MobileIron) and Netlogon/Zerologon that were being used against SLTT and elections-support networks ahead of the U.S. general election. https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa20-283a
- The NSA issued its own advisory on December 18, 2020 warning that Russian state-sponsored actors were separately exploiting a VMware Access and Identity Manager vulnerability to forge SAML-based authentication, broadening federal guidance beyond SolarWinds-specific indicators.
- The FBI, CISA, and ODNI formed a Cyber Unified Coordination Group in mid-December 2020 to coordinate the national response to SUNBURST, an institutional pattern later reused for subsequent major incidents.
- The November vishing incidents against GoDaddy support staff renewed scrutiny of phone-based, registrar-level account-recovery processes and pushed affected platforms toward stronger out-of-band verification for support-initiated account and DNS changes.
