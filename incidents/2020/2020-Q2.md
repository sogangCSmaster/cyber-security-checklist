> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/misconfiguration — broken access control and permission settings, not attacker sophistication, handed over source code and government records this quarter

```yaml
  - https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/healthcare-giant-magellan-health-hit-by-ransomware-attack/
  - https://www.hipaajournal.com/extent-of-magellan-health-ransomware-becomes-clear-more-than-364000-individuals-affected/
```

**What happened.** A Magellan Health employee responded to a spear-phishing email on April 6, 2020 that impersonated one of the company's own clients. The attacker used that foothold to install credential-stealing malware, and by April 11 had deployed ransomware against Magellan's network, affecting the national managed-care company and eight of its affiliates and provider entities.

**Entry.** A targeted email crafted to look like a genuine Magellan client convinced an employee to interact with it in a way that let the attacker into that employee's email account and, from there, install malware built to harvest further login credentials.

**Escalation.** The harvested credentials gave the attacker access to at least one Magellan corporate server holding employee tax and identification data (names, addresses, employee IDs, Social Security numbers, W-2 and 1099 information); from there the intrusion reached systems across eight affiliated healthcare entities before ransomware was detonated network-wide.

**Blast radius.** Magellan's notified count grew as the investigation continued, ultimately covering more than 364,000 individuals — patients, health plan members, and employees across the affected affiliates. A subset of current employees separately had Social Security numbers and tax-form data stolen directly from the compromised corporate server. Magellan later reached a reported $1.43 million settlement tied in part to delayed breach notification.

**Human factor.** One employee's response to a well-targeted, client-impersonating email was enough to reach both payroll data and, ultimately, patient-facing systems across a multi-affiliate healthcare organization — email compromise alone should not have been able to bridge that far.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training reflecting current spear-phishing technique, particularly client impersonation), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on email and remote access so a stolen password alone is not enough), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication anomalies to catch credential-theft malware before it reaches a second system).

```yaml
scale: undisclosed

sources:
  - https://www.thelocal.de/20200409/german-state-of-north-rhine-westphalia-halts-coronavirus-aid-payments-over-fake-websites
  - https://polizei.nrw/artikel/vorsicht-vor-fakeseiten-corona-soforthilfe
```

**What happened.** In early April 2020, fraudsters built fake websites mimicking North Rhine-Westphalia's official portal for Corona-Soforthilfe, the state's emergency grant program paying stranded freelancers and small businesses €9,000–€15,000 to survive the pandemic shutdown. The lookalike sites ranked prominently in search results alongside the genuine portal, and applicants who landed on them unknowingly submitted their personal and banking details to the fraudsters instead of the state. NRW's economy ministry discovered the scheme, filed a police fraud complaint, and paused disbursements on April 9, 2020 while it investigated.

**Entry.** No state system was breached directly. Fraudsters registered domains and built pages replicating the real Soforthilfe application form closely enough, and positioned them highly enough in search results, that applicants under pressure to get emergency funding out the door could not tell the copy from the original — a phishing campaign aimed at the public rather than at NRW's own staff or infrastructure.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial deception: whatever an applicant typed into the fake form — name, address, tax ID, bank account details — went straight to the fraudsters. A parallel phishing-email campaign, sent from a spoofed corona-zuschuss@nrw.de.com address, tried the same trick by claiming a Soforthilfe payment had been made in error and asking the recipient to "repay" it.

**Blast radius.** NRW did not publish a confirmed count of applicants who submitted data to the fake sites, or a confirmed financial loss; the ministry's public statements describe the discovery and the program-wide pause, not a final tally. The state broadened its warning to direct all applicants to a single verified domain, soforthilfe-corona.nrw.de.

**Human factor.** A benefit program built and launched in days, to get money to businesses before they collapsed, gave fraudsters a narrow but real window: for as long as a search for NRW's Corona-Soforthilfe could return a convincing fake ahead of or alongside the real portal, applicant urgency did the fraudsters' work for them.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-07 (a pre-launch checklist for an emergency benefit portal that includes registering lookalike domains and monitoring search results before go-live, not after fraud reports arrive), OBSV-05 (a written incident and public-warning plan ready before launch, since NRW's response was reactive), HUMAN-NEW (a standing practice of monitoring for and pursuing takedown of domains and search placements impersonating a public-facing benefit portal — no existing control ID covers this).

```yaml
  - https://socprime.com/news/ragnar-locker-ransomware-sets-own-record-demanding-about-11m-from-edp/
```

**What happened.** On April 13, 2020 — Easter Monday — the Ragnar Locker ransomware group encrypted systems belonging to EDP (Energias de Portugal), one of the world's largest producers of wind energy and a major European utility. EDP confirmed the attack publicly by mid-April. The attackers claimed to have exfiltrated roughly 10 TB of data, including employee credentials, financial records, and partner and customer information, and demanded 1,580 bitcoin — reported at around $10.9 million at the time — to both decrypt systems and not publish the stolen data.

**Entry.** EDP did not disclose how Ragnar Locker's operators first got in. Security researchers who examined the group's ransom note and activity concluded the attackers likely had a foothold in EDP's network from around April 6, roughly a week before the ransomware was deployed — long enough to move through the environment and stage data for exfiltration before triggering encryption.

**Escalation.** Consistent with that week-long gap, reaching corporate IT systems broadly enough to justify a company-wide ransom note, and gathering a claimed 10 TB of varied data (employee, financial, partner, customer), implies lateral movement across EDP's network rather than a single system being fully compromised at the point of entry.

**Blast radius.** EDP said its power generation and distribution operations, and the broader electricity grid, were never compromised and continued running normally throughout — a genuinely positive finding, suggesting operational technology was segmented from the corporate network that Ragnar Locker actually hit. The 10 TB figure and the $10.9 million ransom demand come from the attackers' own note and claims as reported by researchers who reviewed them, not from an independent audit by EDP or a regulator, which is why this record carries low rather than high confidence.

**Human factor.** A roughly week-long gap between an unexplained initial foothold and a company-wide ransomware detonation is a detection gap, not just an encryption event — whatever let Ragnar Locker's operators move through EDP's corporate network for days went unnoticed until the moment they chose to act.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (the segmentation that evidently protected EDP's operational/grid systems should extend to limit lateral movement on the corporate side too), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of the access and movement that occurred in the week before detonation), OBSV-03 (monitoring the monitors — a week of unnoticed presence on a utility's corporate network is itself a detection failure worth treating as its own finding).


**What happened.** A misconfigured internal code repository let anyone register a new account and log in, exposing Clearview AI's facial-recognition application source code, cloud credentials, and Slack access tokens to the open internet. Security researcher Mossab Hussein of SpiderSilk found the exposed server and reported it directly to Clearview rather than accepting a bug-bounty payment that would have required his silence. TechCrunch published the finding on April 16, 2020, while Clearview was already under scrutiny for scraping billions of photos to sell facial-recognition matching to law enforcement.

**Escalation.** None required. Registering an account granted the same access a legitimate engineer would have had: the full source tree and the secret keys and Slack tokens sitting alongside it in the same repository.

**Blast radius.** What was confirmed publicly was internal: proprietary source code, cloud storage credentials, and Slack tokens. No breach of the underlying scraped-photo database or the law-enforcement client list was confirmed in this incident; Clearview said it rotated the exposed keys and audited the host for unauthorized access.

**Human factor.** A small, fast-moving startup ran its own developer tooling without the access review that a company holding a database of billions of scraped faces should have applied to its own infrastructure first.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-01 (no live secret key sits in a reachable repository), CRED-03 (rotate anything a researcher — or anyone else — could have seen, immediately), CLOUD-08 (keep internal developer tooling off the public internet entirely).

**What happened.** Cognizant, one of the world's largest IT outsourcing and consulting firms, confirmed on April 18, 2020 that the Maze ransomware group had encrypted internal systems and disrupted service for its clients. Maze's operators also exfiltrated data before encrypting, and Cognizant later confirmed personal and financial information belonging to employees and clients had been stolen. The company estimated the incident would cost it $50–70 million, a figure it disclosed to investors on its Q1 2020 earnings call rather than in a security notice — hence the disputed, range-only cost and this record's medium confidence.

Some clients proactively cut off Cognizant's access to their own networks to contain the risk, pausing outsourced projects. Cognizant put the direct cost at $50–70 million in its Q1 2020 results — the company's own estimate, not an audited final figure.

**Human factor.** As a firm whose business model depends on holding privileged access into hundreds of client networks, Cognizant's failure to disclose (or determine) its own entry vector left every client unable to assess their own exposure through Cognizant's side of the connection.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between tiers so ransomware cannot walk from one compromised host to the whole estate), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the remote-access or admin accounts ransomware crews typically use for initial access), OBSV-05 (a written incident plan with a disclosure clock, since Cognizant's client-facing communication badly lagged its technical response).


**What happened.** The SBA's online portal for COVID-19 Economic Injury Disaster Loan (EIDL) applications briefly showed one applicant's submitted personal and business information to a different, unrelated applicant. The agency said it discovered the issue on March 25, 2020, did not send written notice to affected applicants until April 13, and the exposure only became public through press reporting on April 21.

**Entry.** No attacker was involved. Using the browser's back button while navigating the rushed, newly built EIDL application portal could return a previously cached page belonging to a different applicant's session, exposing that person's name, Social Security number, address, date of birth, and other loan-form information.

**Escalation.** None required — the data rendered directly in the browser to whoever hit "back" at the wrong moment. No further access or privilege was needed once the page was reachable.

**Blast radius.** The SBA said approximately 8,000 EIDL applicants may have had their information viewed by another applicant during the exposure window. The agency offered a year of free credit monitoring; it did not report confirmed fraud tied specifically to this exposure.

**Human factor.** The EIDL portal was built and launched under extreme political and public pressure at the start of the pandemic, with the agency trying to open emergency relief applications within days; ordinary session-isolation testing that would normally catch cross-user data bleed was evidently skipped or insufficient.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-01 (default-deny, per-request authorization so one session can never render another user's stored data), DATA-02 (a basic negative test — can user A see user B's submission? — run before launch), HUMAN-07 (a pre-launch checklist for sensitive government intake systems, even under emergency timelines).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://techcrunch.com/2020/04/24/after-160000-accounts-are-compromised-nintendo-shuts-down-nnid-logins/
  - https://securityboulevard.com/2020/06/nintendo-confirms-additional-140000-accounts-compromised-in-april-data-breach/
```

**What happened.** Starting in April 2020, attackers used email/password combinations leaked in earlier, unrelated breaches to log into Nintendo Accounts through the older Nintendo Network ID (NNID) linking system. Nintendo confirmed 160,000 affected accounts on April 24, then confirmed a further 140,000 in June, bringing the total to roughly 300,000.

**Entry.** Credential stuffing: none of Nintendo's own systems were breached. Attackers replayed passwords stolen from other services against the legacy NNID login path, which many users had reused across sites.

**Escalation.** Where a reused password matched, the attacker inherited the linked Nintendo Account and any stored payment method. Nintendo attributed the exposure specifically to the older NNID sign-in path rather than its current login, and retired NNID-based login entirely in response.

**Blast radius.** Personal information (nicknames, date of birth, country/region, email, gender) was exposed across roughly 300,000 accounts; Nintendo said fraudulent purchases using stored payment methods affected less than 1% of the compromised accounts.

**Human factor.** Password reuse by end users was the proximate cause, but Nintendo's own decision to keep a legacy login path active alongside its current account system, without MFA required by default, gave that reuse a second door to walk through.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-06 (rate limiting and breached-password checks on login attempts would have slowed or blocked the stuffing), AUTH-01 (MFA required by default rather than opt-in), AUTH-10 (retire legacy login paths instead of leaving them live alongside the current one).


**What happened.** After Seoul eased lockdown restrictions in late April 2020, a man who had visited several nightclubs and bars in the capital's Itaewon district over the night of May 1–2 tested positive for COVID-19. South Korea's contact-tracing system, built around detailed public exposure alerts, traced and publicized the venues involved; the cluster grew to 54 confirmed cases by May 10 and 79 by May 11. Several media outlets reported that one of the venues was a gay bar, and the story became a national controversy over outing as much as a public-health one.

**Entry.** No hack or unauthorized access was involved. South Korea's COVID-19 response routinely published detailed exposure notices — venue type, and often the sequence and timing of an infected person's movements — as a legitimate public-health tool to let others self-check for exposure. Applied to nightlife venues associated with Seoul's gay community, that same disclosure mechanism, combined with media reporting that characterized the venues, functioned as an inadvertent outing of individuals who had never disclosed their sexual orientation.

**Escalation.** None required. The alerts and reporting were themselves the entire exposure; no further access or technical step was needed to turn public-health data into identifying, stigmatizing information once venue type was reported alongside movement and timing detail.

**Blast radius.** No official count was published of how many people were identified or outed as a result. Documented consequences included a marked increase in online harassment directed at Seoul's gay community, harassment through gay dating apps where users shared others' information "for spreading coronavirus," and public calls for LGBTQ venues to close. Health officials and advocacy groups warned at the time that the backlash risked undermining the alert system's own purpose — discouraging people who had visited the venues from coming forward for testing out of fear the disclosure would out them.

**Human factor.** The alert system was designed to maximize public-health value by publishing granular movement and venue detail; nobody building or approving that disclosure process appears to have weighed that granular detail is far more dangerous when a venue's other public association — as a space tied to a marginalized group — turns location data into identity data.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimization — publish only what public-health value requires, not precise venue identity or timing for sensitive-context locations), HUMAN-07 (a review checklist for public exposure disclosures that flags sensitive venue categories before publication), HUMAN-NEW (a standing policy to generalize or redact venue category and identity for locations associated with a protected or marginalized group, balancing public-health notification against outing risk — no existing control ID covers this).

**Blast radius.** 28,000 hosting accounts had their SSH login credentials exposed for approximately six months before detection. GoDaddy reset the affected passwords, removed the attacker's public key from affected accounts, and offered a year of free website security and malware-removal service.

**Human factor.** A six-month gap between intrusion and detection on infrastructure that hosts other companies' websites meant every affected customer's site was a potential secondary risk for that entire window, without GoDaddy or its customers knowing it.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-07 (credentials, including SSH access, should expire rather than remain valid indefinitely), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of admin/access-control changes, which would have flagged the altered SSH file far sooner), OBSV-03 (monitor the monitors — six months undetected is itself a detection-capability failure worth treating as its own incident).

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-10 (a feature that fans out health data about others to any user should be rate-limited and coarsened, not left to return arbitrarily fine-grained aggregates), DATA-09 (data minimization — aggregate counts below a location-size or population threshold should not be returned at all), HUMAN-07 (a privacy and security review checklist for a mandatory national health-tracing app before launch, not after a researcher demonstrates the gap in public).

---

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA's ICS-CERT coordinated the Ripple20 disclosure with 21+ device vendors publishing advisories simultaneously (ICSA-20-168-01, June 16, 2020) — a large-scale template for handling one vulnerable embedded library shared across an entire industry's supply chain.
- Nintendo permanently retired NNID-based account login rather than patching around it, and pushed two-factor-authentication adoption to users following the credential-stuffing wave.
- GoDaddy force-reset SSH credentials across all affected accounts, stripped the attacker's public key, and extended a year of free security monitoring to affected customers.
- Illinois IDES and the SBA both drew legislative and oversight scrutiny over emergency relief-portal security; Illinois's contractor (Deloitte) offered credit monitoring, and both episodes fed a broader 2020 debate about security review timelines for pandemic-era government systems built in days rather than months.
- Cognizant's Q1 2020 earnings call became one of the earliest instances of a major public company quantifying a ransomware incident's cost to shareholders ($50–70M) in near-real time, ahead of any formal breach-cost disclosure requirement.
- The EasyJet breach renewed scrutiny of card-verification-code retention: CVV data is not supposed to survive authorization under PCI-DSS, and the case was widely cited afterward as evidence the rule is routinely broken in practice.
- The Illinois ACLU sued Clearview AI in May 2020 under the state's Biometric Information Privacy Act, part of a broader legal reckoning that intensified through the quarter as Clearview's data practices and security posture both drew scrutiny.
