# 2020 Q1 — The Scramble to Remote Access

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/misconfiguration — wrong defaults and wrong access settings (Zoom's password-less meetings, Sprint's indexed support forum, Virgin Media's marketing database) outnumbered any single exploit chain this quarter
> - **New technique this quarter:** mass automated enumeration of password-less, ID-only video meetings ("Zoombombing") at a scale collaboration platforms had never had to defend against before hundreds of millions of people went online overnight
> - **Recurring failure:** credentials and vendor access that reached far past the job in front of them — two franchise employees' logins reaching 5.2 million Marriott guests, a marketing vendor holding 1.7 million unencrypted Nedbank records, a support agent's account able to rewrite a registrar's DNS
> - **Records in this file:** 10

**What happened.** Currency-exchange firm Travelex took its UK and international websites and apps offline on December 31, 2019, first describing it as planned maintenance; by January 7, 2020 it confirmed a ransomware attack by the Sodinokibi/REvil group had encrypted systems across its global operations. The outage also knocked out foreign-exchange services Travelex ran white-label for banks and retailers.

**Entry.** Travelex never publicly confirmed its initial access vector. Security researchers reported in the following weeks that Travelex had been running internet-facing Pulse Secure VPN appliances vulnerable to CVE-2019-11510 — an unauthenticated arbitrary file-read flaw the vendor patched in April 2019 — and CISA warned on January 10, 2020 that REvil-linked actors were actively using that same flaw to plant ransomware on unpatched VPN concentrators.

**Escalation.** The ransomware reached systems across Travelex's worldwide operations before detonation, consistent with little containment between an edge VPN appliance and core infrastructure, though Travelex did not publish forensic detail on the path the attackers actually took.

**Blast radius.** Travelex's sites and apps were down for roughly two weeks, forcing manual, paper-based transactions at some locations. The company paid a reported $2.3 million ransom against REvil's initial $6 million demand. REvil separately claimed on its leak site to have exfiltrated customer data, including card numbers and dates of birth, before encrypting — a claim Travelex disputed and that was never independently confirmed, so no `impact/pii` or `impact/extortion` tag is applied here. Travelex entered administration in August 2020 with over 1,300 UK job losses, though the ransomware attack was only one of several pressures on the business.

**Human factor.** Whoever owned patching for Travelex's remote-access infrastructure left a fixed, actively-exploited vulnerability running on a system reachable from the entire internet for months after a patch existed.

**What would have stopped it.** An inventory that flags internet-facing infrastructure for enumeration and patch tracking (CLOUD-01, CLOUD-09), and phishing-resistant MFA on the VPN itself (AUTH-01), which would have blocked use of any credentials the file-read flaw exposed even after exploitation.

```yaml
  - https://krebsonsecurity.com/2020/01/sprint-exposed-customer-support-site-to-web/
```

**What happened.** Journalist Brian Krebs found that Sprint's internal "Social Care" support forum, where frontline representatives escalated customer complaints to managers, was reachable without any login and indexed by search engines, exposing months of internal postings to anyone who searched for them.

**Entry.** A configuration error made a section of the support forum that was meant to require authentication publicly reachable and crawlable; no credential theft or software vulnerability was involved, the pages were simply left public.

**Escalation.** None required — once that forum section was reachable, its contents were fully readable with no further access needed.

**Blast radius.** The postings included customer names, device identifiers, location details, and internal escalation notes — information useful for SIM-swap social engineering against the same customers — though Sprint described the content as containing only "minimal customer information." Sprint did not disclose how many customers appeared or how long the section had been exposed. Krebs reported the exposure privately to Sprint, which took it down; no evidence of malicious access before that report was identified.

**Human factor.** Someone changed, or failed to set, the forum's access configuration without checking whether the result was reachable and indexable from the open internet.

**What would have stopped it.** Treating every new internet-facing surface as something to inventory and periodically re-enumerate, rather than assume, (CLOUD-01, CLOUD-09).


**What happened.** South African bank Nedbank disclosed on February 13, 2020 that Computer Facilities (Pty) Ltd, a direct-marketing vendor that sent SMS and email campaigns on Nedbank's behalf, had suffered a security incident exposing personal data on roughly 1.7 million Nedbank Retail clients, about 1.1 million of them active customers.

**Entry.** The incident occurred entirely inside Computer Facilities' own environment — Nedbank said its own banking systems and customer accounts were never touched. Nedbank has not detailed how the vendor itself was compromised.

**Escalation.** None required for the exposed data itself — Computer Facilities held a working copy of client contact records directly, with no further access needed to read it.

**Blast radius.** Names, ID numbers, phone numbers, and physical or email addresses for 1.7 million clients were exposed. Nedbank said no banking credentials, account numbers, or funds were involved, and reported no confirmed financial loss to customers. It disconnected the vendor's systems from the internet and had the client data destroyed after discovering the incident through routine monitoring.

**Human factor.** Nedbank had handed a large, standing extract of customer contact data to a marketing vendor rather than limiting what the vendor held or for how long — the bank's own code and systems were never the problem.

**What would have stopped it.** An inventory of every vendor holding customer data with a defined minimization limit (VENDOR-01), sanitizing what a marketing processor is given in the first place (VENDOR-04), and time-boxed, separately monitored vendor access to that data (VENDOR-08).


**What happened.** In February 2020, journalists reported that a file containing personal details on roughly 10.6 million former MGM Resorts hotel guests had been posted for free on a hacking forum. MGM confirmed a past security incident had exposed guest information; Have I Been Pwned loaded 3.1 million unique email addresses from the file on February 20, 2020.

**Entry.** News organizations that reviewed the file reported the underlying intrusion had happened the previous summer and involved unauthorized access to a cloud server holding guest records. MGM did not publicly detail how that access was obtained, so this record uses `entry/unknown` rather than guess.

**Escalation.** None required — once the cloud server was reached, the guest records it held were directly readable.

**Blast radius.** Records included names, addresses, phone numbers, emails, and dates of birth for 10.6 million former guests, including some celebrities and government officials; that figure comes from journalists' review of the leaked file rather than an MGM-published count, hence this record's medium confidence. No payment card or password data was reported in the set.

**Human factor.** Whatever the initial access method, the roughly seven-month gap between the 2019 intrusion and its February 2020 public reckoning meant guests learned their data was exposed only once someone else posted it, not from complete, proactive notification at the time the exposure was found.

**What would have stopped it.** Regular enumeration of what is actually reachable from the internet (CLOUD-01), keeping guest databases off the public internet entirely (DATA-04), and a disclosure plan with a stated clock rather than one effectively triggered by a forum posting (OBSV-05).

```yaml
controls: [AUTH-03, DATA-01, HUMAN-10]
sources:

```

**What happened.** Phone-accessory retailer Slickwraps had customer order data exposed in February 2020 after someone found systems that returned account records without verifying the requester was authorized to see them. Contemporary press coverage cited roughly 377,000 affected customer records; Have I Been Pwned loaded 857,600 accounts from the incident on February 22, 2020 — this record gives the range rather than picking one.

**Entry.** Order and account data was reachable through at least one endpoint that did not check authorization before returning records; Slickwraps did not fully detail the mechanism publicly.

**Escalation.** None required — the exposed endpoint(s) returned customer records directly to any requester.

**Blast radius.** Names, emails, physical addresses, and order history for hundreds of thousands of customers were exposed. Slickwraps' own breach-notification email to affected customers then compounded the incident: its send process itself mishandled recipient data, so the company's response added to the exposure rather than only containing it.

**Human factor.** No server-side check confirmed a requester was entitled to the record it asked for, and when the company tried to notify affected customers it repeated the same category of mistake — moving quickly without checking what the outgoing message would itself expose.

**What would have stopped it.** No endpoint returning user data without authentication (AUTH-03), default-deny row-level authorization on customer tables (DATA-01), and confirming recipients and message contents before a breach notice — or anything else — leaves the organization (HUMAN-10).

**What happened.** Facial-recognition company Clearview AI disclosed that an intruder had gained unauthorized access to its systems and obtained its entire client list. BuzzFeed News reported on February 28, 2020 that the list covered roughly 2,200 organizations — police departments, other government agencies, and private companies — across 27 countries.

**Entry.** Clearview attributed the access to a security flaw and said it had been fixed, but did not publicly describe the specific mechanism the intruder used, so this record uses `entry/unknown`.

**Escalation.** None required for what was taken — Clearview said its core facial-recognition database and scraped-image corpus were not compromised, only the client account list and related usage data.

**Blast radius.** The client list's exposure was significant chiefly for what it revealed about Clearview's controversial business: a company built on unconsented scraping of billions of photos for law-enforcement facial search, whose customer roster included agencies that had previously denied using the tool.

**Human factor.** A small, fast-moving company handling a highly sensitive customer roster did not prevent, or fully explain afterward, how an outsider reached that roster.


**What happened.** Virgin Media disclosed in March 2020 that a database used for marketing purposes had been left accessible without proper controls for around ten months, exposing personal details on approximately 900,000 customers and prospective customers.

**Entry.** A staff member failed to follow the company's own security procedures when setting up or maintaining the database, leaving it reachable in a way it was never meant to be. Virgin Media confirmed at least one instance of unauthorized access during the exposure window.

**Escalation.** None required — the database held the exposed records directly, with no further step needed to read them.

**Blast radius.** Exposed data included names, home addresses, email addresses, and phone numbers for roughly 900,000 people. Virgin Media said it did not believe the data had been used for fraud, but could not rule out further access during the ten-month window.

**Human factor.** A configuration step that should have restricted the database's reach was skipped or reversed, and nothing in the surrounding process caught it for the better part of a year.

**What would have stopped it.** Default-deny access controls verified on every client- or process-reachable data store (DATA-01), infrastructure managed as code so drift from the intended configuration is detectable (CLOUD-07), and a checklist for standing up any database holding customer records rather than relying on one person doing it correctly (HUMAN-07).

```yaml
sources:
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoom_(software)
  - https://www.zoom.com/en/blog/a-message-to-our-users/
```

**What happened.** As remote work and school closures drove Zoom's daily meeting participants up by more than an order of magnitude within weeks, uninvited strangers began joining meetings en masse to disrupt them with harassment, pornography, and hate speech — dubbed "Zoombombing." The FBI's Boston field office issued a public warning on March 30, 2020, and Zoom's CEO published a company-wide response the following day.

**Entry.** Zoom meetings did not require a password by default and used short numeric meeting IDs that were often reused or shared; tools that scanned or guessed valid IDs, combined with hosts and organizations posting join links publicly, let uninvited participants find and enter meetings with no credential of any kind.

**Escalation.** None required — once inside a meeting, a participant had immediate access to screen-share, unmute, and chat, with no further foothold needed to disrupt it.

**Blast radius.** No systematic count of affected meetings exists, so this record leaves `scale` undisclosed; incidents were reported across thousands of schools and businesses through March 2020, ranging from pornographic image-sharing to racist and antisemitic harassment aimed at students and employees. The tag vocabulary has no dedicated category for meeting hijacking; `impact/messages` is the closest fit for the private, real-time conversations uninvited participants gained access to.

**Human factor.** Zoom's defaults were tuned for pre-arranged enterprise meetings among known participants, not for an entire population of schools and first-time consumer users sharing links into an ID-only join flow, and no one revisited that default before adoption exploded.

**What would have stopped it.** Meeting identifiers that are not the only thing standing between a link and a live meeting — passwords and waiting rooms as the default, not an opt-in (INPUT-09) — and rate-limiting or lockout on repeated meeting-ID guesses (AUTH-06); Zoom made both changes shortly after this quarter.


**What happened.** A spear-phishing attack against a GoDaddy customer-service employee on March 30, 2020 gave an attacker access to internal support tools, which they used to alter DNS settings on at least six customer accounts. The only publicly identified victim, escrow service Escrow.com — a small company with no part in the failure — had its domain pointed at a server in Malaysia and its homepage defaced for about two hours before GoDaddy reverted the change.

**Entry.** The compromised employee account gave the attacker visibility into customer account notes, including one stating that domain changes required verbal authorization via a specific phone number, and the ability to make DNS changes on customers' behalf directly through GoDaddy's own support tooling.

**Escalation.** None beyond what the compromised support account already granted (`escalation/admin-tooling`) — GoDaddy's internal admin/support tooling itself performed the DNS changes, with no further technical escalation needed.

**Blast radius.** Escrow.com's domain was hijacked and its site defaced for roughly two hours; the attacker also obtained a free Let's Encrypt SSL certificate for the hijacked domain. GoDaddy said it locked down the affected accounts, reverted all unauthorized changes within the day, and found no customer data, funds, or domains were ultimately lost across the six reported accounts — hence the same-day `dwell_days`.

**Human factor.** One employee's response to a targeted phishing message gave an outsider the same DNS-changing power as GoDaddy's own support staff, with no independent step to confirm a change was genuinely requested by the domain's actual owner.

**What would have stopped it.** Verifying identity for any account or DNS change independent of what the requester claims (HUMAN-01), separate authentication and full audit logging on admin/support tooling itself (AUTH-07), and training that covers current spear-phishing technique against support staff specifically (HUMAN-05).

**What happened.** Marriott disclosed on March 31, 2020 — separate from its much larger 2018 Starwood breach — that an unauthorized party had used the login credentials of two employees at a franchise property to access guest information through an application Marriott uses to provide guest services, affecting approximately 5.2 million guests.

**Entry.** The attacker used valid login credentials belonging to two franchise-property employees; Marriott did not disclose how those credentials were originally obtained, so this record does not assert a specific credential-theft mechanism.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the credentials themselves (`escalation/overscoped-credential`) — those two property-level accounts had access to the guest-services application at a scale reaching 5.2 million guests system-wide, far beyond what front-desk staff at a single franchise property should need.

**Blast radius.** Exposed data included contact details (name, address, email, phone), loyalty account information, and for some guests additional details such as company, gender, birthday, and airline loyalty program numbers; Marriott said payment card and account password/PIN data were not involved.

**Human factor.** Marriott's franchise model granted property-level staff accounts reach into a guest database far beyond their own property, so compromising two ordinary logins was enough to reach millions of records system-wide.

**What would have stopped it.** Scoping franchise-property credentials to that property's own guests rather than the whole guest database (CRED-06), server-side per-object authorization checks on the guest-services application (AUTH-02), and treating franchise/partner access as vendor access that is time-boxed and separately monitored (VENDOR-08).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA Alert AA20-010A (January 10, 2020) confirmed REvil/Sodinokibi-linked actors and nation-state groups were actively exploiting CVE-2019-11510 in unpatched Pulse Secure VPN servers, months after a patch had shipped, stating there were "no viable workarounds except for applying the patches."
- CISA Alert AA20-073A, "Enterprise VPN Security" (March 13, 2020), was issued directly in response to the pandemic-driven telework surge, recommending VPN and network-device patching, MFA on every VPN connection, and capacity planning for the sudden jump in remote-access load.
- The FBI's Boston field office issued a public service announcement on March 30, 2020 warning of "Zoombombing" and teleconference hijacking, recommending meetings default to private with passwords, screen-share restricted to hosts, and meeting links kept off public posts.
- Zoom published a 90-day security plan on April 1, 2020 (https://www.zoom.com/en/blog/a-message-to-our-users/), freezing feature development to focus engineering on trust and safety and committing to third-party security review, an expanded bug bounty, and moving passwords and waiting rooms toward defaults rather than opt-in settings.
- Zyxel shipped an emergency patch in late February 2020 for a critical, unauthenticated remote command-injection flaw in its network-attached storage devices after researchers found it being sold on a cybercrime forum (https://krebsonsecurity.com/2020/02/zyxel-fixes-0day-in-network-storage-devices/) — a reminder that network-edge storage and firewall appliances, not just VPNs, were under active scrutiny the same quarter.
- Several of this quarter's records (Nedbank, Sprint, Virgin Media) share the same underlying gap regardless of scale: personal data exposed through a marketing vendor, an internal support tool, or a database, caught by routine monitoring, a journalist, or the vendor itself — not by the victim's own detection program.
