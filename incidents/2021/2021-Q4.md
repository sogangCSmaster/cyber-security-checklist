# 2021 Q4 — Log4Shell and Leaky Registries

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/unknown — five of this quarter's eleven records (the npm maintainer hijacks, MediaMarktSaturn, Panasonic, Volvo, and Brazil's Ministry of Health) never named how the attacker actually got in
> - **New technique this quarter:** a single attacker-controlled string reaching an ordinary log call resolved a JNDI lookup into unauthenticated remote code execution (Log4Shell) — the code path was years old, but December 2021 is when it became everyone's problem at once
> - **Recurring failure:** registry and repository accounts without phishing-resistant MFA, and extortion-group leak-site claims (Snatch on Volvo, Lapsus$ on Brazil's Ministry of Health) publicized well ahead of anything the victim would confirm
> - **Records in this file:** 20

sources:
  - https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2021/10/15/updates-on-the-twitch-security-incident/
  - https://thehackernews.com/2021/10/twitch-suffers-massive-125gb-data-and.html
```

**What happened.** An anonymous poster leaked a 125GB torrent of Twitch's internal data on October 6, 2021, tagged with the hashtag "#DoBetterTwitch." The dump included Twitch's full source code with commit history, its mobile/desktop/console clients, internal security tooling, an unreleased Amazon Game Studios project code-named Vapor, and creator payout reports for 2019–2021 that revealed individual top streamers' earnings. Twitch said the underlying access had occurred on October 4, and published a full account on October 15 once its investigation concluded.

**Entry.** Twitch's own statement attributed the incident to "a server configuration change that allowed improper access by an unauthorized third party" — a change that made an internal server, apparently a Git host carrying the full source repository, reachable in a way it should not have been. No credential theft, phishing, or code-level exploit was described.

**Escalation.** None required. Once the misconfigured server was reachable, the entire source tree, internal tooling, and creator payout archive sitting on it were directly readable; Twitch said the systems storing login credentials were a separate system and were not touched, which is the only reason this incident did not also become a credential-exposure event.

**Blast radius.** 125GB of source code, internal tooling, an unreleased product, and multiple years of individual creator earnings data were published. Twitch said passwords, full credit card numbers, and bank/ACH information were not exposed, and that the incident affected only "a small fraction" of users directly.

**Human factor.** A routine-sounding infrastructure change reached a system holding the company's entire source history and business-sensitive payout data without the review or testing that should have caught it also making that system reachable from outside.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (nothing is publicly reachable unless it was decided to be, checked on a regular cadence), CLOUD-07 (infrastructure declared as code with drift detection, so an unreviewed configuration change is itself an alert), CICD-02 (required review before any change — infrastructure or application — reaches a system holding the company's full source history).

impact: [impact/pii, impact/identity-documents]

confidence: medium

sources:
  - https://therecord.media/hacker-steals-government-id-database-for-argentinas-entire-population
  - https://www.iproup.com/innovacion/26646-hackeo-dni-argentina-venden-datos-de-renaper
```

**What happened.** On October 13, 2021, Argentina's government confirmed that RENAPER (Registro Nacional de las Personas), the national registry that issues ID cards for the country's roughly 45 million citizens, had been improperly queried after a seller posted government-issued photo IDs of 44 Argentine public figures — including President Alberto Fernández and footballer Lionel Messi — to Twitter and Telegram as proof of access, then began advertising bulk citizen records for sale.

**Entry.** The government's own account was limited to confirming that a VPN account issued to the Ministry of Health — a separate agency with a legitimate integration into RENAPER's data for public-health purposes — had been used to run the queries; officials did not disclose whether that VPN credential was phished, guessed, reused, or handed over by the employee it belonged to, so this record uses entry/unknown rather than picking among them. Asked directly about the compromised account, the seller told The Record only "careless employees, yes."

**Escalation.** escalation/overscoped-credential. A VPN account provisioned for one ministry's narrow public-health use case was able to query RENAPER's full identity-document database across agency lines — no further privilege escalation was needed because the account was already scoped far beyond the task it was issued for.

**Blast radius.** Confirmed proof was limited to 44 public figures' ID photos and details posted publicly; the seller separately claimed to hold the whole registry and to be preparing a release of one to two million records for sale, a claim the government did not confirm. Argentina's government stated RENAPER itself was not directly breached in the sense of an intrusion, framing it instead as unauthorized use of an existing, valid access path, and opened an investigation into eight government employees.

**Human factor.** A cross-agency credential meant to support one narrow, specific data-sharing purpose between the Health Ministry and RENAPER was broad enough to pull identity-document images and personal data for any citizen in the registry, and its handling was casual enough that an outside seller could use it undetected until they chose to advertise the access themselves.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-06 (a VPN credential scoped to the one health-verification purpose it was issued for, not the full identity registry), AUTH-08 (access reviewed and inventoried so a cross-agency integration this broad is a deliberate, owned decision rather than a standing account nobody is watching), OBSV-02 (alerting on abnormal query volume against a national identity database would have surfaced bulk lookups well before the seller chose to go public).

### I2021Q4-03 · Missouri DESE educator Social Security number exposure

```yaml
id: I2021Q4-03
name: Missouri DESE educator Social Security number exposure
org: Missouri Department of Elementary and Secondary Education
date_occurred: unknown
date_disclosed: 2021-10-14
region: United States
sector: [sector/government, sector/education]
actor: [actor/researcher]
entry: [entry/misconfiguration]
escalation: [escalation/none-required]
impact: [impact/pii, impact/none-confirmed]
factor: [factor/process-failure, factor/legacy-debt]
scale: 576000 people
confidence: high
controls: [CRED-02, DATA-09, OBSV-06]
sources:
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Parson
```

**What happened.** On October 14, 2021, the St. Louis Post-Dispatch reported that a public Missouri Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (DESE) web tool, used to search and verify teacher certifications, embedded educators' Social Security numbers directly in the page's underlying HTML — never displayed on screen, but present in the raw source for anyone who looked. Reporter Josh Renaud found the flaw, confirmed it against a handful of records, and privately reported it to DESE, delaying publication to give the state time to fix it. Rather than acknowledging the disclosure, Governor Mike Parson publicly branded Renaud a "hacker," referred the matter to the Missouri State Highway Patrol, and vowed to prosecute him — a response widely condemned by security researchers and press-freedom advocates. A subsequent state investigation (158 pages, roughly 175 hours of work) cleared Renaud of any wrongdoing, found he had accessed only information the site itself already sent to any visitor, and found the underlying flaw had existed since 2011, ultimately affecting 576,000 educator records; prosecutors brought no charges.

**Entry.** The certification-search tool's response page included each educator's SSN in the HTML sent to the browser, readable with an ordinary "view source" or developer-tools action — no authentication bypass, exploit, or credential was needed. The data was simply present in a response the public tool already returned to anyone using it as intended.

**Escalation.** None required. Whatever record a user searched for arrived with its SSN already embedded in that same response; there was no separate system to reach and no privilege beyond what the public search page itself granted.

**Blast radius.** 576,000 current and former Missouri educators' Social Security numbers were exposed in page source for roughly a decade before discovery. DESE and the state's investigation found no evidence the flaw had been exploited maliciously before Renaud's report.

**Human factor.** Two, at different levels. First, a basic client-side data-exposure mistake — serializing a sensitive field into a page the UI never displayed — went unnoticed through a decade of the tool's operation. Second, and more consequential for the wider field: the state's own leadership response, threatening the person who disclosed responsibly, is a case study in what discourages exactly the kind of reporting that fixed this before it was exploited.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-02 (the same "anything the browser can read is public" principle that governs secrets applies to any sensitive field sent to the client — an SSN should never have been serialized into a response the interface didn't display), DATA-09 (data minimization: an application should not transmit fields it has no reason to render), OBSV-06 (a published, safe vulnerability-disclosure channel and policy would have made this report something the state welcomed rather than threatened to prosecute).

The outage was visible to viewers the same day: several Sinclair-owned stations lost the ability to display lower-third and full-screen graphics, including during NFL broadcasts on their NBC, CBS, and Fox affiliates.

region: global

entry: [entry/unknown]
escalation: [escalation/supply-chain-cascade]
impact: [impact/credentials]
factor: [factor/missing-mfa, factor/third-party-trust]
tech: [tech/npm]
scale: "undisclosed (combined packages exceeded 30 million weekly downloads; confirmed infections never published)"
confidence: high
controls: [AUTH-01, DEPS-01, DEPS-03]
sources:
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2021/10/22/malware-discovered-popular-npm-package-ua-parser-js
  - https://www.rapid7.com/blog/post/2021/11/05/new-npm-library-hijacks-coa-and-rc/
  - https://github.com/faisalman/ua-parser-js/issues/536
```

**What happened.** On October 22, 2021, an attacker took over the npm publishing account of ua-parser-js (about 8 million weekly downloads) and pushed three malicious versions (0.7.29, 0.8.0, 1.0.0) that installed a cryptocurrency miner and, on Windows, a password-stealing trojan; the versions were live for roughly four hours before being pulled. Less than two weeks later, on November 4–5, 2021, the same pattern repeated against two more widely depended-on packages, coa and rc (a combined roughly 22 million weekly downloads), whose malicious versions ran an obfuscated installer that fetched further malware, which researchers linked to the Qakbot trojan family. In each case the package's own source history and code review were fine — the attacker did not need to find a bug, only to control the account that could publish under the legitimate maintainer's name.

**Entry.** npm, CISA, and the maintainers' own advisories describe the mechanism only as an attacker gaining control of the maintainer's npm account; none of the public post-mortems specify how (password reuse, phishing, or otherwise), so this record uses entry/unknown rather than guessing. What is well documented is that none of the three accounts had two-factor authentication enabled — npm did not require it for any package at the time, regardless of how many downstream projects depended on it.

**Escalation.** escalation/supply-chain-cascade. Publish rights on npm were the only thing that mattered; owning that one credential let the attacker ship a malicious release directly to every project with a loose version range, with no further technical escalation. The damage cascaded outward instead of upward: ua-parser-js alone was used directly or transitively by projects at Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and many others, so one hijacked maintainer account reached an enormous, largely invisible downstream install base.

**Blast radius.** Exact infection counts were never published for either wave. The ua-parser-js compromise was caught and pulled within about four hours; coa and rc were discovered after their malicious post-install scripts started crashing consuming projects' build pipelines. npm removed all malicious versions from the registry in both cases.

**Human factor.** Three unpaid or lightly resourced open-source maintainers, each controlling a dependency a meaningful share of the JavaScript ecosystem relies on, had no requirement from npm, from the ecosystem, or from any of the companies depending on their work for free, to protect their publishing credentials with anything beyond a password.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on the npm account itself would have blocked a stolen or reused password from being sufficient), DEPS-01 (a committed, frozen lockfile means a newly published malicious version cannot silently reach a build the moment it ships), DEPS-03 (a cooldown before automatically adopting a newly published dependency version would have kept most consumers out of the four-hour and same-day windows these versions were live).

sources:
  - https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387921000073/exhibit991november82021blo.htm
  - https://robinhood.com/us/en/newsroom/robinhood-announces-data-security-incident-update
```

**What happened.** On November 3, 2021, an attacker called a Robinhood customer support employee and, through a social-engineering pretext over the phone, persuaded them to install remote access software on their work computer. Using that access the attacker reached a customer support system and pulled a large customer list before Robinhood detected and cut off the intrusion; the attacker then emailed Robinhood demanding an extortion payment not to sell or further distribute what they had taken. Robinhood disclosed the incident on November 8, 2021, reported it to the SEC, and said it did not pay the demand.

**Entry.** A phone-based social-engineering attack (vishing) against a support representative, who was convinced to grant remote access to their own machine. No password was phished and no software vulnerability was exploited — the entry point was a human decision made under the pressure and plausibility of what looked like a legitimate call.

**Escalation.** escalation/admin-tooling. The compromised representative's own access to Robinhood's customer support system was the target: from that one workstation the attacker could query and export records across a large share of Robinhood's user base without needing to compromise any additional system.

**Blast radius.** Up to about 7 million people were affected in total: roughly 5 million had only an email address exposed, and about 2 million also had their full name exposed. A smaller group of 310 customers had more extensive personal details exposed (name, date of birth, zip code), about 10 people had even more extensive account information taken, and a November 16 update added that just over 4,400 customers' phone numbers were also stolen. Robinhood said Social Security numbers, bank account numbers, and debit card numbers were not exposed.

**Human factor.** A single support representative, with no identity-verification step independent of the caller's own claims, was able to install software that gave an outside party a working window into millions of customers' data — the process treated the phone call itself as sufficient authorization for a high-impact action.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-01 (identity verification for any credential or access action independent of the caller's claim, including calls that claim to be internal or urgent), HUMAN-05 (support-staff training that specifically covers phone-based impersonation, not only email phishing), AUTH-07 (customer support tooling with reach into millions of records should sit behind its own additional authentication and full audit logging, separate from a representative's general login).

**What happened.** GoDaddy disclosed on November 22, 2021 that an unauthorized party had used a compromised password to access the provisioning system for its Managed WordPress hosting product, a legacy part of GoDaddy's codebase. **What happened.** On December 9–10, 2021, the Apache Software Foundation disclosed CVE-2021-44228 ("Log4Shell"), a maximum-severity (CVSS 10.0) remote-code-execution flaw in Log4j 2, the Java logging library used — directly, or far more often as an unenumerated transitive dependency — inside an enormous share of enterprise Java software. The flaw had been privately reported to Apache by Chen Zhaojun of Alibaba Cloud Security on November 24, 2021; a fix (2.15.0, with 2.12.2 and 2.3.1 for older Java versions) shipped December 6, but exploit details spread publicly — first through the Minecraft community — before most downstream users even knew they were affected, and mass scanning and exploitation began within hours of the public disclosure. The flaw lived in the library itself, not in any specific application's own code, and most organizations running it had no direct relationship with the Log4j project at all.

**Escalation.** escalation/none-required. A successful JNDI lookup directly yielded code execution on the host running the vulnerable logging call; there was no separate privilege-escalation step between "log line got parsed" and "attacker's code is running."

**Blast radius.** No single confirmed count exists or ever will. CISA and vendors described Log4j as present in hundreds of millions of devices and a very large share of enterprise Java applications, but both CISA and independent researchers cautioned the true figure is unknowable because the library is routinely bundled several layers deep inside other vendors' products. Confirmed victims of the resulting exploitation wave include Belgium's Ministry of Defence, recorded separately in this file, among many organizations still being identified months later.

**Human factor.** None identified at the level of any single victim — the structural failure was ecosystem-wide. A huge population of organizations had built systems on a dependency graph too deep to fully see, so when a critical flaw appeared in one widely reused piece of it, most could not even determine their own exposure quickly, let alone patch it.

**What would have stopped it.** DEPS-07 (dependency scanning in CI that flags a newly-CVE'd library immediately, including transitive ones), DEPS-08 (surfacing new transitive dependencies for review — the core problem this quarter was that most organizations could not even enumerate where Log4j was running), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed, named response for "a library we depend on, however deeply buried, was just found critically vulnerable" — this quarter's scramble showed how few organizations had one).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- npm announced enhanced login verification on December 7, 2021 and a phased mandatory-2FA rollout for maintainers of high-impact packages (top-100 by download/dependents from February 1, 2022, expanding through the year) — a direct structural response to a quarter in which ua-parser-js, coa, and rc were all hijacked through unprotected maintainer accounts.
- CISA published dedicated Log4j guidance and coordinated a cross-government response as multiple organizations, including Belgium's own Ministry of Defence, confirmed direct exploitation; the scramble to answer "do we even use this, and where?" renewed momentum behind software bills of materials (SBOMs) as a baseline dependency inventory, building on the "minimum elements" NTIA had published earlier in 2021 under Executive Order 14028.
- Missouri's own 175-hour, 158-page state investigation into the DESE disclosure cleared reporter Josh Renaud of wrongdoing and found no evidence of malicious exploitation — a rare instance of a government formally vindicating, rather than punishing, a researcher's responsible disclosure, after its governor's initial public threat to prosecute him.
- GoDaddy and Robinhood both filed SEC Form 8-Ks describing their incidents within days of discovery, reflecting the routine use of US federal securities disclosure as a breach-notification channel for public companies during this period.
- Both Volvo (Snatch) and Brazil's Ministry of Health (Lapsus$) show the same pattern: an extortion group's leak-site claim became public before, or well ahead of, anything the victim organization would itself confirm — a gap this corpus treats as a confidence signal, not a detail to smooth over.
