# 2021 Q2 — Ransomware Targets Critical Infrastructure Worldwide

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/unknown — four of the eleven records (JBS, Waikato DHB, Fujitsu, Air India/SITA) never had a technical mechanism disclosed, more than any single named technique

sources:

```

**What happened.** Codecov, a code-coverage tool used inside CI pipelines by roughly 23,000 organizations, found on April 1, 2021 that its Bash Uploader script — code customers' CI jobs download and execute on every run — had been silently modified to exfiltrate the contents of every environment variable in the job to an attacker-controlled server. The tampering had been live since January 31, 2021, so any customer running the compromised script for about two months had potentially sent their CI secrets to the attacker. Codecov published a root-cause post-mortem on April 15, 2021.

**Entry.** An error in Codecov's own Docker image creation process left a Google Cloud Storage service-account HMAC key recoverable from an intermediate layer of Codecov's public Self-Hosted Docker image — a credential embedded in a build artifact that Codecov itself distributed to the internet.

**Escalation.** That single key was sufficient on its own: it granted write access to the Google Cloud Storage bucket serving the live Bash Uploader script, so no further privilege escalation was needed to alter code that would then run, trusted, inside every customer's CI environment — a compromised vendor becoming the path into its customers.

**Blast radius.** The modified script ran inside CI jobs at organizations including HashiCorp and Rapid7, both of which rotated signing keys and credentials as a precaution. Codecov never disclosed how many of its roughly 23,000 customers actually had secrets exfiltrated, only that anyone who ran the script between January 31 and April 1, 2021 should treat every credential in their CI environment as compromised.

**Human factor.** Two trust failures stacked: Codecov's build pipeline let a live credential leak into a publicly distributed artifact, and thousands of customers' CI systems were configured to fetch and execute a third-party shell script directly from the internet on every run with no integrity check on what came back.

**What would have stopped it.** CRED-04 (secrets injected at runtime from a manager, never baked into a Docker image layer), DEPS-06 (no script loaded from a URL you do not control, pinned or vendored instead of fetched live on every run), CICD-01 (CI secrets scoped per job so a compromised third-party script cannot read the whole environment).

```

**What happened.** GEICO notified customers, via a breach notice filed with the California Attorney General, that between January 21 and March 1, 2021 fraudsters used personal information they already held on individual policyholders — obtained elsewhere, not from GEICO — to pull those same people's driver's license numbers out of GEICO's own online quote and sales system.

**Entry.** No credential theft or system exploit was needed: the online sales portal's "get a quote" workflow returned a driver's license number when fed a policyholder's name, address, and date of birth, information widely available from earlier, unrelated breaches — an intended feature abused as a lookup tool rather than a vulnerability exploited.

**Escalation.** None required — the driver's license number came back directly from the same workflow, with no further access needed once the attacker had enough identity data to pass as the policyholder requesting a quote.

**Blast radius.** GEICO did not disclose a total count; it filed notice with California under the state's 500-resident threshold and warned affected customers that the stolen driver's license numbers were likely intended for fraudulent state unemployment insurance claims filed in their names during the pandemic.

**Human factor.** A quoting workflow built to help a prospective customer retrieve their own information was never tested against the question of what someone already holding a stranger's stolen personal data could extract through the same door.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-06 (rate limiting and breached-data anomaly detection on a lookup flow that returns a sensitive identifier), INPUT-09 (never treat already-public identity fields as sufficient authorization to return a more sensitive one), OBSV-02 (alerts on abnormal per-account or per-IP query volume against the quoting system).

  - https://www.clickstudios.com.au/security/advisories/
```

**What happened.** Click Studios, the small Australian company behind the enterprise password manager Passwordstate, told its roughly 29,000 customers on April 24, 2021 that its software's own upgrade mechanism had been used to deliver malware. For a roughly 28-hour window between April 20 and 22, any customer who ran an in-place upgrade received a tampered update package containing a malicious loader alongside the legitimate installer files.

**Entry.** Attackers compromised the update-delivery mechanism on Click Studios' own infrastructure — how they first got in was never publicly disclosed — and used it to substitute a malicious "Moserware.SecretSplitter.dll" component into the legitimate in-place upgrade package for the roughly 28 hours it took Click Studios to detect the tampering.

**Escalation.** None required beyond what the update mechanism already had: because customers' Passwordstate servers trusted anything delivered through their own official upgrade feature, the malicious loader ran with the access the legitimate application already had — including read access to every password in the local vault — the moment an administrator clicked "upgrade."

**Blast radius.** The malware, dubbed Moserpass, harvested Passwordstate configuration data and stored credentials and sent them to an attacker-controlled server. Click Studios never disclosed how many of its 29,000 customers — reported to include banks, universities, and government and defense agencies — actually upgraded inside the compromised window, so the confirmed victim count remains undisclosed; it told all customers to treat every password stored in the product as compromised and reset them.

**Human factor.** A password manager's entire value proposition rests on its update channel being trustworthy by default; a small vendor did not have artifact-signing or integrity checks strong enough to stop a substituted file inside that channel from reaching production password vaults undetected for over a day.

**What would have stopped it.** CICD-03 (build provenance and artifact signing, so a tampered update package fails a signature check before installation), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed customer-facing response for "your password manager's update channel was compromised," which is exactly what happened here), VENDOR-01 (customers maintaining an inventory of what a tool like Passwordstate can reach, so a compromise triggers an immediate, scoped credential rotation).

  - https://techcrunch.com/2021/05/05/peloton-bug-account-data-leak/
```

**What happened.** Security firm Pen Test Partners found that several of Peloton's GraphQL API endpoints returned a user's account data — age, gender, weight, location, and workout statistics — to any caller, authenticated or not, including for accounts the user had explicitly set to private. Researcher Jan Masters privately reported the flaw on January 20, 2021; Peloton silently and incompletely patched only one of the issues and went quiet for months, and the finding only became public on May 5, 2021 after Pen Test Partners approached a journalist.

**Entry.** The flaw was in authorization, not authentication: Peloton's API checked that a request was well-formed but never checked that the caller was allowed to see the specific user record requested, so knowing or guessing a user ID was enough to pull that person's profile regardless of privacy settings.

**Escalation.** None required — the same unauthenticated request that exposed one user's data would return any other user's data by changing the ID in the query, with no additional access or privilege needed.

**Blast radius.** No evidence emerged of mass scraping or malicious exploitation before the fix; the exposure was demonstrated by the researcher against Peloton's multi-million-user base, but no theft or third-party misuse of the data was documented, which is why this record carries impact/none-confirmed alongside the exposure itself.

**Human factor.** Peloton's initial response — acknowledging the report, fixing only one of the flaws without telling the researcher, then going silent — turned a straightforward authorization bug into a four-month window where the real fix depended on press attention rather than the disclosure process working as intended.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint returns user data, full stop), OBSV-06 (a vulnerability disclosure process that actually closes the loop with the reporting researcher instead of silently patching one issue and moving on).

```

**What happened.** DarkSide ransomware affiliates encrypted business systems at Colonial Pipeline, operator of the largest refined-products pipeline in the United States, on May 7, 2021. Colonial proactively took its entire 5,500-mile pipeline offline as a precaution while it assessed whether the intrusion had reached operational technology, triggering fuel shortages and panic-buying across the U.S. Southeast and a federal emergency declaration; the pipeline resumed operations five days later, on May 12.

**Entry.** The attackers used a compromised password for a legacy VPN account that was no longer in active use by any employee but had never been disabled and had no multi-factor authentication configured. The password was not obtained by attacking Colonial directly — it had surfaced in an unrelated, earlier breached-credential dump and was reused on this account. Colonial's CEO told Congress the earliest evidence of attacker activity inside the network dated to April 29, 2021, eight days before ransomware was deployed.

**Escalation.** Colonial did not publicly detail the specific lateral-movement path, but a single VPN login led to roughly 100 GB of data exfiltrated and ransomware deployed broadly enough across IT systems — billing, accounting, business networks — to force a precautionary shutdown of the entire pipeline, consistent with a legacy access route that bypassed whatever controls governed current accounts.

**Blast radius.** Colonial paid a $4.4 million ransom in Bitcoin; the Department of Justice recovered 63.7 of those bitcoins (about $2.3 million at the time) in June 2021 after tracing the payment to a wallet for which the FBI held the private key. Colonial did not disclose a personal-record count tied to this incident — the reported harm was overwhelmingly operational, with nearly half of the East Coast's fuel supply disrupted for the better part of a week.

**Human factor.** Nobody had gone back to disable a VPN account after it stopped being used, and MFA was not required broadly enough across remote access to make that omission survivable — a housekeeping gap on a single dormant account shut down critical infrastructure for a week.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every remote-access account, not only actively used ones), AUTH-08 (access inventoried and expired automatically when unused, so a dormant VPN account cannot sit enabled indefinitely), AUTH-10 (legacy login paths disabled outright rather than left reachable alongside current ones).

```

**What happened.** An employee at Ireland's Health Service Executive opened a malicious Excel attachment from a phishing email on March 18, 2021, giving the Conti ransomware gang a foothold that went undetected for roughly eight weeks before Conti encrypted systems across the HSE's national network on the morning of May 14, 2021 — described by Irish ministers as the most significant cybercrime attack on the Irish state to that point. The HSE shut down all of its IT systems nationwide as a precaution, cancelling outpatient appointments, radiotherapy, and diagnostic services and forcing hospitals back onto paper records for weeks.

**Entry.** A phishing email, part of a broader IcedID-linked malspam campaign rather than a message individually crafted for the HSE, delivered a malicious Excel document that a single employee opened; this was malware execution via an attachment rather than direct credential harvesting, the closest match in this corpus's vocabulary to what actually happened.

**Escalation.** Conti's operators spent close to eight weeks moving through the network before detonating ransomware. The HSE's own commissioned review (PwC) found this was possible because of a flat, poorly segmented network and a single antivirus product that was neither centrally monitored nor kept current — the initial workstation had gone a full year without an antivirus update — leaving no effective way to notice an intrusion already well underway.

**Blast radius.** Conti claimed to have exfiltrated roughly 700 GB of data, including patient records, payroll, and contracts, and published a portion of it online after the HSE refused to pay a $19.99 million ransom demand; Conti later handed over a decryption key for free following international pressure. The HSE never disclosed a precise count of patients or staff whose data was confirmed stolen, and recovery-cost estimates ranged from roughly €100 million in immediate IT rebuilding to considerably higher multi-year totals, which is why this record leaves cost undisclosed rather than citing one contested figure.

**Human factor.** One person opened one attachment, but PwC's review found the HSE had no functioning early-warning system: antivirus alerts that should have fired for a year went unmonitored, and nobody was positioned to notice eight weeks of an attacker moving through a national health network.

**What would have stopped it.** HUMAN-05 (phishing training and simulation reflecting current malspam technique), OBSV-03 (treat a security tool that has stopped alerting as an incident in itself — the HSE's antivirus had been silently broken for a year), CLOUD-05 (network segmentation so eight weeks inside one workstation cannot become nationwide encryption).

**What happened.** A ransomware attack took down hospital computer systems, phone lines, and clinical services across the Waikato District Health Board's hospitals — including Waikato, Thames, Tokoroa, Te Kuiti, and Taumarunui — starting May 18, 2021. WDHB disconnected all of its systems from the internet as a precaution, cancelling hundreds of surgeries and appointments; a group claiming responsibility issued a ransom ultimatum on May 25, which the DHB and the New Zealand government refused to pay.

**Entry.** New Zealand media reported a phishing email attachment as the likely entry point, but this was never officially confirmed in WDHB's own public reporting or independently verified beyond that early account, so — following this corpus's rule against treating a "likely" media report as confirmation — this record records the vector as unknown.

**Escalation.** Human Resources Information System servers had reportedly gone unpatched for years, a legacy-system gap consistent with allowing an initial compromise to spread into clinical and corporate systems broadly enough to justify disconnecting the entire regional health board from the internet; the attackers also deleted a significant portion of WDHB's backup files before detonating ransomware, removing the option to simply restore from backup.

**Blast radius.** Hospitals across the Waikato region lost access to phones, email, and patient-management systems for weeks, with a backlog of cancelled surgeries and outpatient appointments persisting into June. Attackers published confidential patient, staff, and financial data on the dark web after the ransom deadline passed; New Zealand media later reported that personal details of at least 4,200 people had been found among the published data, though WDHB did not confirm a total count of everyone affected.

**Human factor.** Whatever the initial trigger, a regional health board running years-unpatched HR servers, with backups reachable enough to be destroyed in the same intrusion, had no meaningful second line of defense once the first system was compromised.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-08 (backups that are restore-tested and unreachable by the same credentials as production, so they cannot be deleted alongside it), HUMAN-05 (phishing-specific training and simulation, given the suspected though unconfirmed entry point), OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan — WDHB's drawn-out, ad hoc recovery communication left patients and staff without clear information for weeks).

**What happened.** SITA, a Switzerland-based technology provider whose Passenger Service System handles reservations and check-in for numerous airlines, disclosed in early March 2021 that it had suffered a breach around late February 2021. Air India, one of several airlines using SITA's PSS, first learned of the breach on February 25, 2021, but did not identify which of its own passengers were affected until March 25 and April 5, and only made the breach public roughly a month later, in mid-to-late May 2021.

**Entry.** Neither SITA nor Air India ever named a technical entry vector; SITA's own public description — "a sophisticated cyber-attack" — is precisely the kind of adjective without a mechanism this corpus discounts, and no further technical detail was ever released.

**Escalation.** Not detailed publicly, but the effect was that compromising one shared services provider gave the attacker passenger data belonging to multiple airlines at once — Air India, Singapore Airlines, Cathay Pacific, Lufthansa, and others were all reported affected by the same SITA PSS compromise — a vendor breach cascading to its customers rather than any one airline's own systems being penetrated.

**Blast radius.** Air India confirmed personal data belonging to approximately 4.5 million passengers, registered between August 2011 and February 2021, was affected: names, dates of birth, contact details, passport numbers, ticket information, frequent-flyer data, and payment card numbers. Card verification codes and account passwords were not affected. Air India was the single worst-hit airline among SITA's client base, though the total scope across all affected carriers was never centrally disclosed.

**Human factor.** Air India's own systems were not the point of failure — the exposure ran entirely through a shared third-party reservations platform — but the airline's own gap of roughly five to twelve weeks between learning which of its passengers were affected and telling them left customers unable to protect themselves for months after Air India itself already knew.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of exactly what data a shared services provider like a PSS vendor holds, so an airline knows its own exposure the moment the vendor is breached), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows that do not allow a months-long gap between vendor breach and airline disclosure), OBSV-05 (a disclosure plan with a clock, rather than a multi-week internal delay before telling affected passengers).

**What happened.** Fujitsu detected unauthorized access to ProjectWEB, an internal cloud-based project information-sharing tool it operated for corporate and government clients, on May 24, 2021, and suspended the entire platform the next day. Within days, multiple Japanese government bodies — including the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, the Cabinet Secretariat (home to Japan's own national cybersecurity coordination center), and Narita International Airport — confirmed their project data hosted on ProjectWEB had been accessed and exfiltrated.

Fujitsu's own investigation ultimately found 129 of its Japanese customers had experienced unauthorized access, spanning airport operations data to central-government records.

Fujitsu discontinued ProjectWEB entirely rather than restore it, and appointed a dedicated CISO in the aftermath.

**Human factor.** A Japanese government ecosystem that relied on a single shared vendor platform for sensitive interagency project collaboration — including data belonging to the country's own cybersecurity coordination body — had no independent visibility into or control over that platform's account-security practices until Fujitsu's breach forced it into the open.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every account with access to a shared multi-tenant platform, making a stolen ID/password pair alone insufficient), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of authentication and access patterns sensitive enough to catch accounts browsing outside their normal project scope), VENDOR-01 (agencies maintaining an inventory of exactly what a shared SaaS vendor holds on their behalf, rather than learning the scope only after the vendor's own disclosure).

**What happened.** REvil ransomware affiliates disrupted IT systems at JBS S.A., the Brazil-headquartered company that is the world's largest meat processor, on May 30, 2021, forcing the shutdown of beef and pork slaughterhouses across the United States, Canada, and Australia — including all of JBS's U.S. beef plants — and standing down roughly 7,000 employees in Australia alone. JBS confirmed the attack publicly on May 31 and restored most production within days, but confirmed on June 9 that it had paid an $11 million ransom, negotiated down from REvil's initial $22.5 million demand.

Security vendors speculated about compromised employee credentials or exposed remote-access infrastructure, and some researchers reported evidence of data movement out of JBS's Brazilian operations beginning weeks before the disruptive attack — but none of this rises to a confirmed mechanism, so this record follows the corpus rule and records the entry vector as unknown rather than repeating unverified speculation as fact.

The disruption briefly halted a significant share of North American beef-processing capacity, prompting congressional scrutiny of cybersecurity in the food and agriculture sector.

**Human factor.** As with several of this quarter's other ransomware cases against critical-sector operators, JBS's account of its own response and payment decision was detailed while the actual root cause — how the attackers first got in, and why the disruption reached plants in three countries almost simultaneously — was never addressed, denying the industry any concrete technical lesson from one of the year's highest-profile ransomware payments.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA across remote access and privileged accounts, the most common precondition for this class of ransomware intrusion even where JBS's specific vector is unconfirmed), CLOUD-05 (segmentation between country and plant networks, since production in the US, Canada, and Australia was disrupted within the same short window), OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan naming what would and would not be confirmed publicly, given how much of JBS's own account of the intrusion remains undisclosed).

**What happened.** Volkswagen Group of America and Audi notified 3.3 million current and prospective customers on June 11, 2021 that a marketing vendor had left their data exposed on the open internet for roughly 21 months, from August 2019 until the exposure was discovered and closed in May 2021. Volkswagen did not name the vendor publicly.

**Entry.** The vendor stored marketing data — gathered from sales, leads, and vehicle purchase inquiries — in a Microsoft Azure Blob Storage container that was never configured to require authentication; anyone could find such exposed containers simply by scanning for company names appended to Azure's default `blob.core.windows.net` domain, no exploit or credential needed.

**Escalation.** None required — the storage container itself was the exposed asset, and anyone who found the URL could read its contents directly.

**Blast radius.** For most of the 3.3 million affected people, the exposed data was limited to name, address, email, phone number, and vehicle interest details; a smaller subset of about 90,000 people, concentrated in the United States, also had more sensitive information exposed, including driver's license numbers for most of that group. Data purporting to be from the breach appeared for sale on a criminal forum within days of Volkswagen's public notification.

**Human factor.** Twenty-one months is long enough that whoever configured this storage container, and everyone at the vendor and at Volkswagen who worked with the marketing data it held, had ample opportunity to notice an open container and never did — a gap pointing to no routine audit of vendor-held data existing at all, not a single moment of carelessness.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-01 (nothing publicly reachable unless decided to be, checked on a recurring schedule — this container sat open for the better part of two years), DATA-03 (object storage private by default, with any exception audited rather than assumed), VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding customer data, so Volkswagen would have known this dataset existed and could have verified how it was secured).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- President Biden signed Executive Order 14028, "Improving the Nation's Cybersecurity," on May 12, 2021 — five days after the Colonial Pipeline shutdown began — mandating MFA, encryption, and zero-trust architecture across federal agencies, requiring software vendors selling to the U.S. government to provide a software bill of materials, and establishing a Cyber Safety Review Board.
- CISA published DarkSide ransomware advisory AA21-131A on May 11, 2021, recommending MFA on remote access, network segmentation between IT and OT, and offline backups — guidance written directly in response to the Colonial Pipeline intrusion.
- In the weeks after Colonial Pipeline, the Transportation Security Administration issued the first federal cybersecurity directives for U.S. pipeline operators, requiring incident reporting to CISA and a designated cybersecurity coordinator for a sector previously governed only by voluntary guidelines.
- The Department of Justice's recovery of $2.3 million of Colonial Pipeline's ransom, by tracing the Bitcoin payment to a wallet the FBI could access, showed that ransom payments are not necessarily a dead loss and pushed law enforcement toward following cryptocurrency rather than relying on attribution alone.
- The Codecov and Passwordstate compromises both targeted the software supply chain's most trusted, least-scrutinized layer — a CI script fetched on every build, and a password manager's own update channel — and pushed the security community toward pinning and verifying third-party build and update artifacts rather than trusting them by default.
- HSE Ireland's independent post-incident review, commissioned from PwC and published in December 2021, became one of the most detailed public post-mortems of a national healthcare ransomware attack, and directly informed the HSE's subsequent investment in centralized detection and antivirus monitoring it had lacked.
