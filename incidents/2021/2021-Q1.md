# 2021 Q1 — Exchange Zero-Days, Exposed Infrastructure

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/zero-day — two unrelated internet-facing perimeter products (Microsoft Exchange, Accellion's 20-year-old legacy FTA) supplied this quarter's largest campaigns, both exploited before most victims could patch
> - **New technique this quarter:** none genuinely new at the exploit-primitive level; what was new was the speed at which a narrow, apparently nation-state ProxyLogon 0-day became days-later indiscriminate mass exploitation by unrelated criminal groups once technical details leaked
> - **Recurring failure:** at least two of the quarter's exposures (SocialArks, Bykea) were nothing more than an authentication-free Elasticsearch instance found by an outside researcher, and a third (Verkada) traced back to a single reachable admin credential with no MFA
> - **Records in this file:** 10

**What happened.** In December 2020, Ubiquiti found that AWS and GitHub environments backing its cloud-connected UniFi and mFi products had been accessed and data downloaded by an unauthorized party. On January 11, 2021, Ubiquiti emailed all customers recommending a password change and MFA enrollment, describing a breach at "a third party cloud provider." Nearly a year later, the U.S. Department of Justice charged Nickolas Sharp — a Ubiquiti cloud engineer who had been placed on the internal team investigating the incident — with using his own privileged access to steal the data, then anonymously emailing Ubiquiti executives a ransom demand of 50 Bitcoin to return it.

**Entry.** As the engineer responsible for Ubiquiti's cloud infrastructure, Sharp already held credentials reaching broadly across the company's AWS accounts and GitHub organization; prosecutors said he used a VPN and a fabricated identity to make his own legitimate access look like an external attack.

**Escalation.** None required — a working day-job credential for a cloud engineer already reached the S3 buckets, long-lived AWS keys, and source repositories that made up the entire incident; no further privilege escalation or lateral movement was needed.

**Blast radius.** Ubiquiti told customers their data "may have been accessed," including names, email addresses, and hashed passwords, without ever confirming an exact number affected; the disclosure coincided with a roughly 20% drop in Ubiquiti's stock price. Prosecutors said Sharp also later posed as an anonymous whistleblower to reporters, claiming Ubiquiti was downplaying the incident. He pleaded guilty in 2022 and was sentenced to six years in prison.

**Human factor.** Ubiquiti gave one cloud engineer standing access broad enough to single-handedly exfiltrate the company's customer database and source code, then let that same person join the internal team investigating the breach he had caused.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-03 (least-privilege IAM so no single engineer's credential spans every AWS account), CRED-06 (credentials scoped to one job and one resource, not company-wide), HUMAN-09 (insider risk monitored by behavior and access patterns rather than assumed away by seniority).


**What happened.** Researchers at SafetyDetectives found an Elasticsearch database belonging to SocialArks — a Chinese social-media management and marketing startup — sitting on a Tencent-hosted server in Hong Kong with no password or encryption. The roughly 408GB database held over 318 million scraped records covering 214 million individual Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn profiles that SocialArks had harvested for its marketing-analytics customers, exposing people who had never interacted with SocialArks at all. Reported publicly on January 11, 2021, it was SocialArks' second such exposure in under six months.

**Entry.** The Elasticsearch instance required no authentication; anyone who reached its IP address and port could query the full dataset directly.

**Escalation.** None required — the database itself was the data; there was no separate system to move into or privilege to gain once the server was reachable.

**Blast radius.** Names, phone numbers, email addresses, and profile metadata for 214 million people across three platforms were exposed for an unknown period. Around 55 million of the exposed Facebook profiles were removed from the server within hours of SafetyDetectives' disclosure email, and SocialArks secured the rest the same day; no evidence of prior malicious access was published.

**Human factor.** A company whose business model is aggregating other platforms' user data at scale left the resulting trove on a database with no access control — for the second time in the same year — with nothing in the public record suggesting a deliberate decision was ever made about who should be able to reach it.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases and search indexes are not reachable from the public internet), CLOUD-01 (nothing is publicly reachable unless it was decided to be, checked on a recurring basis), DATA-09 (data minimization — SocialArks had no apparent operational need to keep a live, queryable copy of 214 million scraped third-party profiles).


**What happened.** SafetyDetectives researchers discovered an exposed Elasticsearch server belonging to Bykea, a Karachi-based ride-hailing and delivery app, during routine internet scans on November 14, 2020. The server held more than 400 million records — roughly 200GB — including customer and driver ("Captain") names, phone numbers, and location data, alongside Bykea's own internal staff login credentials stored in plaintext. Bykea secured the database within about a day of being notified on November 24, 2020; the exposure became public through Pakistani press coverage on January 28, 2021.

**Entry.** As with other Elasticsearch exposures this quarter, no authentication guarded the instance; it was reachable directly from the open internet by IP address and port.

**Escalation.** None required. Because Bykea's own employee credentials sat unencrypted in the same exposed store as customer data, a single access point gave a would-be attacker both customer PII and internal staff logins with no further step needed for either.

**Blast radius.** Location history, contact details, and ride records for Bykea's user and driver base sat exposed for at least ten days before discovery, alongside internal employee credentials. Bykea reported no evidence of prior exploitation; the 400-million figure reflects raw records rather than a confirmed unique-user count, which was never separately disclosed.

**Human factor.** A well-funded logistics company ran a production database with no password on the open internet and stored its own staff's login credentials unencrypted in the same place as customer data — the kind of basic configuration check a periodic infrastructure review would catch before an outside researcher does.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-04 (databases not reachable from the public internet without authentication), CRED-01 (no credential, staff or otherwise, sits unencrypted in a reachable data store), CLOUD-01 (a recurring inventory of what is actually internet-reachable, rather than discovering it via a researcher's scan).

```yaml
  - https://www.cnn.com/2021/02/10/us/florida-water-poison-cyber
```

**What happened.** On February 5, 2021, an operator at the water treatment plant serving Oldsmar, Florida (population roughly 15,000) watched in real time as someone remotely took control of his mouse and briefly raised the target level of sodium hydroxide (lye) in the water supply from 100 parts per million to 11,100 — a dose that, left uncorrected, could have made treated water caustic. The operator immediately reverted the change. A joint CISA/FBI/EPA/MS-ISAC advisory (AA21-042A) followed on February 11, though the advisory itself hedges on some technical details, and subsequent reporting (CyberScoop and others) has questioned whether the event was conclusively an external intrusion at all; no public law-enforcement conclusion has ever been issued.

**Entry.** CISA's advisory says the intrusion "may have" come through TeamViewer, a desktop-sharing tool installed for remote support but left running unattended for months — the advisory is explicit that this was not fully confirmed. Every plant computer ran an outdated, unsupported 32-bit Windows 7 build, shared the same password for remote access, and was connected directly to the internet with no firewall, per CISA.

**Escalation.** None required. Whoever connected via TeamViewer landed directly on a machine with an open session into the SCADA human-machine interface controlling chemical dosing; no separate credential or privilege was needed to reach the control that mattered.

**Blast radius.** No member of the public was exposed to unsafe water — the altered dosing never left the plant, and no injuries or service disruption resulted. The significance of this record is what it demonstrated was possible (real-time, unauthorized manipulation of a public water supply's chemical dosing), not any data or financial loss, which is why it carries impact/none-confirmed despite a genuine control-system intrusion.

**Human factor.** A small municipal utility ran safety-critical process-control software on an unsupported operating system, reachable directly from the internet, protected by one password shared across every machine in the plant — a gap common at small public-sector operators without a dedicated OT security budget, caught here only because a person happened to be watching the screen.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-08 (remote-access interfaces for control systems are never reachable from the public internet), CRED-06 (unique, per-machine credentials rather than one password shared plant-wide), AUTH-01 (MFA on remote-access tooling, which CISA specifically recommended alongside disabling TeamViewer's unattended-access feature).

```yaml
  - https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/accellion-analyst-note.pdf
```

**What happened.** Starting in mid-December 2020, attackers exploited a chain of zero-day vulnerabilities (CVE-2021-27101, -27102, -27103, -27104) in Accellion's File Transfer Appliance (FTA), a roughly 20-year-old legacy product still run by an estimated 300 organizations for large-file exchange. Singtel confirmed on February 11, 2021 that files had been taken from its own FTA instance. Over the following months, dozens more Accellion customers worldwide came forward — Kroger, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, Australia's securities regulator ASIC, the QIMR Berghofer medical research institute, the Washington State Auditor's office (exposing 1.4–1.6 million unemployment claimants), and law firm Jones Day among them — each compromised through the same appliance, with no flaw in any victim's own systems.

**Entry.** Attackers exploited the zero-day chain directly against internet-facing FTA appliances, then dropped a web shell (dubbed DEWMODE) to list and exfiltrate whatever files were stored or in transit on the device; no credentials were phished or reused.

**Escalation.** None required. The FTA appliance's entire purpose is to hold files in transit, so code execution on it put the attacker directly in front of the data; Singtel reported no further movement into its broader corporate network.

**Blast radius.** Singtel said personal information — names, dates of birth, mobile numbers, and addresses — for approximately 129,000 customers was leaked, along with bank details for 28 former employees and card details for 45 staff of one corporate client. Investigators traced the intrusion's start to January 20, 2021, about three weeks before Singtel discovered it on February 9. Mandiant linked the campaign to FIN11 and the Clop ransomware gang's data-extortion arm, which emailed victims threatening to publish stolen files on a leak site — without ever deploying ransomware itself.

**Human factor.** Singtel, like every other victim in this campaign, had done nothing wrong in its own application code; the failure sat with a vendor that kept a two-decade-old appliance in production without the security architecture a modern internet-facing file-transfer product needs, leaving customers with no visibility into that appliance's posture until it was too late.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party, including legacy on-premises appliances, holding your data), DEPS-09 (vendor/product health as a selection and retention criterion — Accellion itself announced it would retire FTA rather than keep patching it), DEPS-10 (a rehearsed response for "a vendor we depend on was compromised," since the extortion phase caught many victims unprepared).


**What happened.** On March 2, 2021, Microsoft released emergency out-of-band patches for four previously undisclosed Exchange Server vulnerabilities — CVE-2021-26855, -26857, -26858, and -27065, collectively "ProxyLogon" — that a China-based group Microsoft calls HAFNIUM had been exploiting since at least January 2021 to read mailboxes and install web shells at a relatively small number of targeted organizations (infectious-disease researchers, law firms, universities, defense contractors, policy think tanks). Within days of the patch and public proof-of-concept code appearing, exploitation became indiscriminate: ESET counted at least ten distinct groups exploiting the same flaws by mid-March, and Brian Krebs reported at least 30,000 U.S. organizations compromised via web shells by March 5 alone. A U.S. government advisory later (July 2021) formally attributed the original campaign to actors affiliated with China's Ministry of State Security.

**Entry.** CVE-2021-26855 is a server-side request forgery flaw letting an unauthenticated attacker send crafted HTTP requests that the Exchange server itself would authenticate, reaching internal backend services with no credentials; chained with a post-authentication arbitrary file-write flaw, this let an attacker write a web shell directly to disk.

**Escalation.** None required for the core chain — an unauthenticated network request converted directly into code execution and a persistent web shell on the mail server that itself held the target mailboxes. Some actors then moved further into Active Directory environments from that foothold, but the SSRF-to-RCE chain itself needed no separate privilege escalation.

**Blast radius.** An estimated 250,000+ Exchange servers were still unpatched globally at disclosure. Figures for how many were actually compromised via web shell range from roughly 30,000 U.S. organizations (Krebs, citing incident responders, as of March 5) to broader global estimates that were never fully reconciled, so this record uses a range at confidence: medium. Weeks later, a subset of already-web-shelled servers were hit with DearCry and other opportunistic ransomware by unrelated criminal actors capitalizing on HAFNIUM's initial access.

**Human factor.** Thousands of organizations ran Exchange Server directly reachable from the internet without the patch cadence, segmentation, or monitoring that internet-facing mail infrastructure demands, and the roughly two-month gap between Microsoft's private awareness (via researcher Volexity's alert in early January) and the public patch left a long window in which exploitation widened before most defenders could act.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint should reach internal backend services — the precise flaw in CVE-2021-26855), CLOUD-01 (Exchange admin interfaces reachable from the internet only as a deliberate, reviewed decision), OBSV-05 (an incident and emergency-patch plan capable of acting within hours of an out-of-band vendor patch, given how fast exploitation became indiscriminate).

**What happened.** On March 9, 2021, physical-security camera vendor Verkada discovered that a group identifying as "APT-69420 Arson Cats" — including Swiss hacker Tillie Kottmann — had obtained a "Super Admin" credential and used it to view live and archived footage from customer cameras, including feeds reportedly inside Tesla facilities, hospitals, jails, schools, and Verkada's own offices, before Bloomberg broke the story publicly.

**Entry.** The attackers first found working administrator credentials for Verkada's customer-support tooling sitting on a misconfigured, internet-exposed support server; those credentials in turn let them reach a Super Admin account with root access across Verkada's customer base, which had no MFA protecting it.

**Escalation.** None required beyond the initial credential find — the Super Admin account was already the highest privilege level in the system, giving immediate access to every customer's camera feeds and, in a subset of cases, stored WiFi credentials and physical access-badge data.

**Blast radius.** Verkada's own incident report says it cut off the attackers' access within two hours of discovering the intrusion, and that the confirmed impact covers 97 of roughly 6,000 customer organizations (under 2%) and 4,530 cameras, plus WiFi and badge credentials for at least 8 customers. The widely cited "150,000 cameras" figure describes the theoretical scope the stolen credential could reach across Verkada's full fleet, not a confirmed number of cameras actually viewed.

**Human factor.** A physical-security vendor entrusted with live video inside hospitals, schools, and jails ran a customer-support server exposed to the internet with reachable administrator credentials, and put no MFA on the one account with root access to every customer's cameras — the kind of "god mode" support tooling many SaaS vendors run with far less scrutiny than their production application.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on every account with this much reach, starting with Super Admin), AUTH-07 (admin and impersonation tooling — like the customer-support interface here — sits behind separate authentication and full audit logging, not on a support server exposed like any other host), CLOUD-08 (admin interfaces, including internal support tooling, are never reachable from the public internet).

SITA's compromise separately affected passenger data at Singapore Airlines, Lufthansa, Malaysia Airlines, Cathay Pacific, Finnair, SAS, Jeju Air, and Air New Zealand — the airlines' own reservation and loyalty systems were not themselves breached; SITA's shared infrastructure was.

**Entry.** Never publicly disclosed at a technical level; SITA described the incident only as a cyberattack without naming a vulnerability, credential, or entry point, which is why this record uses entry/unknown rather than guessing at a mechanism.

**Escalation.** SITA's compromise reached every airline client processed through the affected PSS infrastructure — a vendor breach cascading to its downstream customers rather than requiring separate escalation inside each airline's own network.

**Blast radius.** Air India confirmed names, contact details, dates of birth, passport numbers, ticket information, and some credit card data (without CVV) for approximately 4.5 million passengers spanning nearly a decade of records. SITA supplied Air India the full scope of affected records only in stages, on March 25 and then April 5, 2021, so Air India's own passenger-facing notification and final scale confirmation lagged the initial disclosure by weeks. Totals across all affected Star Alliance carriers combined were never publicly aggregated.

**Human factor.** Airlines outsourced passenger-data processing to a shared industry utility precisely because building and securing that infrastructure independently is expensive — a reasonable trade that nonetheless meant one compromise at SITA became a breach-notification obligation for more than a dozen airlines with no way to independently audit SITA's security, exposing passengers to a processor most had never heard of.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of every third party holding your data, including shared industry-utility processors like a PSS provider), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows and active monitoring of vendor advisories, given how slowly full scope reached the airlines' own customers), DATA-07 (encryption of sensitive fields like passport numbers at rest, and deletion of identity documents once no longer needed — nearly a decade of retained passport data materially increased this breach's severity).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA, the FBI, EPA, and MS-ISAC jointly published advisory AA21-042A (February 11, 2021) on the Oldsmar incident, specifically recommending disabling unattended TeamViewer access, enforcing MFA, retiring Windows 7, and adopting independent "cyber-physical safety systems" that cannot be overridden by a compromised HMI.
- Microsoft shipped emergency out-of-band patches for all four ProxyLogon CVEs on March 2, 2021, followed within days by a one-click Exchange On-premises Mitigation Tool for organizations that could not patch immediately; CISA issued Emergency Directive 21-02 ordering federal civilian agencies to patch or disconnect internet-facing Exchange servers.
- Accellion announced it would retire the 20-year-old FTA appliance entirely rather than keep patching it, urging customers to migrate to its newer Kiteworks platform.
- The Department of Justice's prosecution of the Ubiquiti insider (indicted December 2021, later a guilty plea and six-year sentence) established a rare public template for treating an employee who steals data and then extorts their own employer as an insider-threat criminal case rather than an anonymous external "breach."
- The Verkada breach intensified scrutiny of always-on super-admin support access at SaaS and IoT vendors; several named customers independently disclosed the extent of their own exposure and pulled cameras offline pending review.
- SITA's episode renewed airline-industry attention on shared Passenger Service System infrastructure, since one processor's compromise reached passenger data at more than a dozen carriers through no fault of the airlines' own systems.
- A wave of researcher-reported, authentication-free Elasticsearch exposures continued this quarter (SocialArks, Bykea, among others tracked by firms like SafetyDetectives), keeping pressure on cloud providers and CERTs to speed up abuse-report response for exposed database instances.
