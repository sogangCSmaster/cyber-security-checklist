# 2021 Q3 — Trusted Channels, Vendors, and Silence

> **Quarter at a glance**
> - **Dominant entry vector:** entry/supply-chain-vendor — Morgan Stanley and Saudi Aramco were both breached through a vendor's systems rather than their own, and Kaseya's own auth-bypass/SQLi compromise then cascaded outward through that same kind of trusted vendor channel to reach roughly 1,500 further businesses
> - **New technique this quarter:** an authentication-bypass and SQL-injection chain in Kaseya VSA let a single REvil affiliate push a fake "hotfix" through a trusted software-update channel straight to downstream businesses over a single holiday weekend — turning routine remote-monitoring tooling into a mass-ransomware delivery mechanism
> - **Recurring failure:** four of eleven records (Guntrader, Accenture, Lojas Renner, Olympus) never disclosed how the intruder actually got in, leaving only "ransomware" as the explanation
> - **Records in this file:** 17

## Incidents

sources:
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaseya_VSA_ransomware_attack
  - https://www.tenable.com/blog/cve-2021-30116-multiple-zero-day-vulnerabilities-in-kaseya-vsa-exploited-to-distribute-ransomware
  - https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2021/07/07/analyzing-the-revil-ransomware-attack
```

**What happened.** On July 2, 2021, a REvil affiliate exploited a chain of zero-day flaws in Kaseya VSA, an on-premises remote-monitoring-and-management (RMM) server that managed service providers (MSPs) use to administer their clients' machines, and used compromised VSA servers to push a malicious "certificate update" to every endpoint they managed. Roughly 60 MSPs were hit directly; through them, an estimated 800 to 1,500 downstream small and mid-sized businesses had files encrypted within a single weekend, including Coop Sweden, whose point-of-sale systems were managed by an affected MSP and which had to close around 800 grocery stores for nearly a week.

**Entry.** The attacker chained an authentication-bypass and business-logic flaw (CVE-2021-30116) with a SQL-injection-adjacent request against the VSA web interface's `userFilterTableRpt.asp` endpoint, letting them authenticate without valid credentials and upload an arbitrary file — the payload later pushed to every agent the compromised VSA server managed.

**Escalation.** No lateral movement inside any single victim's network was required: the compromised VSA server was itself the trusted distribution channel for legitimate software to every endpoint it managed, so one exploited server became thousands of ransomware deployments at once. The payload also side-loaded through a legitimate, signed executable to slip past endpoint defenses that trusted the certificate.

**Blast radius.** REvil demanded $70 million for a universal decryptor; Kaseya did not pay, and on July 23 announced it had obtained a working decryptor from "a trusted third party" — later reported by the Washington Post to have been the FBI, which had obtained REvil's keys before the ransom demand was even public. Direct financial cost to Kaseya was never disclosed; downstream businesses individually absorbed days of encrypted operations and, in Coop's case, a full systems rebuild.

**Human factor.** The Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure (DIVD) had privately reported seven vulnerabilities in VSA to Kaseya starting March 23, 2021, and the two organizations were mid-patch-cycle, having fixed four of seven, when REvil independently found and exploited three of the remaining unpatched ones on July 2 — a responsible-disclosure process still in progress when the attacker won the race.

**What would have stopped it.** INPUT-01 (parameterized queries eliminate the injection path in the reporting endpoint), AUTH-03 (no unauthenticated endpoint should have been able to trigger privileged file uploads), VENDOR-06 (downstream MSPs and their clients tracking DIVD's and Kaseya's own advisories would have known patching was still incomplete going into the July 4 weekend).

  - https://techcrunch.com/2021/07/08/the-accellion-data-breach-continues-to-get-messier/
```

**What happened.** Morgan Stanley sent breach notification letters dated July 2, 2021 to participants in its StockPlan Connect employee stock-plan business, after learning that Guidehouse — a third-party vendor performing account maintenance for that business — had itself been compromised through a zero-day in Accellion's legacy File Transfer Appliance (FTA) product around January 2021. Morgan Stanley's own systems and code were never touched; the exposure ran entirely through a subprocessor's use of a third party's product.

**Entry.** The Clop-linked group tracked as UNC2546 exploited SQL-injection zero-days in Accellion FTA, a file-transfer appliance Guidehouse used to exchange files, giving them access to whatever Guidehouse had stored or transferred through it — which included Morgan Stanley participant data.

**Escalation.** None required. The files Guidehouse held were encrypted, but the attackers also obtained the decryption keys during the same intrusion, so the protection that encryption was meant to provide did not hold once the keys themselves were taken.

**Blast radius.** Exposed data included participant names, last known addresses, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, and employer names; Morgan Stanley said no account passwords or trading credentials were involved. Guidehouse told Morgan Stanley it found no evidence the stolen data had been further distributed. No national total was ever published; state filings show only per-state breakdowns, such as 108 affected residents reported to New Hampshire.

**Human factor.** Guidehouse patched the Accellion vulnerability that compromised it within days of Accellion's own disclosure in January 2021, but did not recognize that Morgan Stanley client data had been part of what was taken until it told Morgan Stanley in May — a four-month gap between a already-patched vulnerability and the vendor identifying whose data had gone out the door.

**What would have stopped it.** VENDOR-01 (an inventory of which third parties, and their own third parties, hold your data), VENDOR-06 (contractual notification windows tight enough that a four-month recognition gap is not possible), VENDOR-08 (vendor access and the systems it touches monitored independently of the vendor's own detection).

**What happened.** REvil compromised HX5, LLC, a small Florida-based engineering and technical-services contractor whose clients include the US Army, Navy, Air Force, and NASA on space and weapons-launch programs, and by July 7, 2021 had posted stolen internal documents to its "Happy Blog" extortion site. The New York Times, reporting the same day in coverage of the Biden administration's response to a wave of ransomware including Kaseya, assessed the disclosed material as not of "vital consequence" — it did not appear to include classified information, though the episode still put a small firm with sensitive defense-adjacent clients on a criminal group's leak site.

**Entry.** Neither HX5 nor any independent forensic account has disclosed how REvil's affiliate first got in; the company's small size and the period's dominant REvil playbook (bought RDP/VPN credentials or unpatched edge devices) make either plausible, but attributing a specific one would be guessing.

**Escalation.** Not detailed publicly, but REvil's 2021 operating model typically required reaching file shares and often domain infrastructure before both encrypting hosts and staging documents for the leak site, a pattern consistent with some lateral movement inside HX5's network rather than a single directly exposed store of files.

**Blast radius.** The scale of what was taken was never quantified by HX5 or independently verified; the New York Times' characterization of the leaked material as not highly consequential is the only public assessment of severity, and no regulator or breach-notification filing has added further detail.

**Human factor.** A small contractor working on sensitive government programs disclosed essentially nothing about the intrusion beyond REvil's own leak-site claim — the same disclosure gap that recurs across several ransomware cases in this file, where the "how" is left entirely to the attacker's account or outside inference.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on remote-access paths, a baseline against the credential- and VPN-focused entry techniques dominant among REvil affiliates this quarter), CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA for internet-facing systems, closing the edge-device route REvil affiliates used elsewhere in 2021), OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan — a small contractor with sensitive government clients has the least room to rely on "no further comment").

```

**What happened.** Beginning July 18, 2021, a consortium of 17 media organizations coordinated by Forbidden Stories, working with Amnesty International's Security Lab, published the Pegasus Project: an investigation built around a leaked list of over 50,000 phone numbers said to belong to people of interest to government clients of NSO Group, the Israeli maker of the Pegasus spyware. Amnesty's forensic team confirmed active or attempted infection on a subset of phones it was able to examine, including those of journalists, human-rights defenders, and opposition politicians, and within days heads of state including France's Emmanuel Macron and South Africa's Cyril Ramaphosa were reported among those targeted.

**Entry.** By this period Pegasus's dominant infection method was a zero-click exploit chain delivered through apps like iMessage, requiring no click or action from the target; Amnesty's forensic methodology report documented the traces this leaves — network-injection redirects, a process it identified as "BridgeHead," and modified system files — as direct evidence against NSO's own claim that the tool left no trace.

**Escalation.** None required — a successful zero-click exploit gave Pegasus the access level to read messages and calls, harvest photos and location, and activate the camera and microphone directly, with no further step needed once the exploit landed.

**Blast radius.** Confirmed and alleged targets spanned dozens of countries; India's own count from the leaked list alone exceeded 300 people, including journalists, an opposition leader, and a sitting Supreme Court justice, while other reporting partners tied targeting to government clients in Mexico, Morocco, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Hungary, and Azerbaijan, among others. NSO Group disputed that the leaked list itself represented confirmed Pegasus targets, calling that characterization "false," without disputing that Pegasus had been sold to the government clients named.

**Human factor.** NSO Group markets Pegasus with a stated customer-vetting process meant to restrict sales to government clients for lawful counter-terrorism and law-enforcement use; the scale and pattern of confirmed targeting — journalists, activists, and opposition figures rather than that stated use case — is a vetting and accountability process the investigation showed did not hold in practice.

**What would have stopped it.** No conventional technical control fully addresses state-client spyware aimed at a personal device outside any organization's management; CLOUD-10 (a patch SLA — Apple shipped fixes for the specific exploit chains once given indicators, though these were zero-days with no patch available beforehand), OBSV-05 (a named forensic and disclosure process — Amnesty's own Mobile Verification Toolkit, and Apple's later threat-notification system for targeted users, are exactly this control built in direct response), VENDOR-NEW (export control and enforceable end-use vetting on commercial spyware sold to government customers — the accountability gap this investigation exposed, and one no control in this checklist yet names).

  - https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/uk-gun-owners-data-exposed/
```

**What happened.** Guntrader, a small UK website for buying and selling firearms, notified users on July 21, 2021 that its customer database had been stolen and subsequently dumped on a criminal forum. The stolen SQL export covered every registered user from 2016 through July 17, 2021 — about 111,000 licensed gun owners.

**Entry.** Guntrader never publicly confirmed the technical mechanism, and no regulator or independent forensic report has settled it either; a security analyst who reviewed the leaked data called it consistent with an opportunistic, untargeted "drive-by" compromise rather than a bespoke attack, but that remains speculation rather than a confirmed vector.

**Escalation.** None required — the leak was a complete export of the users table, meaning whatever access was gained led directly to the whole database rather than requiring further privilege escalation.

**Blast radius.** The dump included names, home addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, bcrypt-hashed passwords, IP addresses, and — because Guntrader logged it — the latitude and longitude of each user's last login, effectively a geotagged list of likely firearm storage locations. The UK's National Crime Agency and South West Regional Cyber Crime Unit opened an investigation; Guntrader itself was later placed into liquidation, though it continued trading under a new entity.

**Human factor.** A small marketplace accumulated five years of highly sensitive, physically actionable data — home addresses tied to firearm ownership plus precise login geolocation — without evident investment in the kind of security review that dataset's sensitivity warranted, and retained all of it rather than aging out old accounts.

**What would have stopped it.** DATA-09 (data minimization and enforced retention limits, so five-year-old accounts and login-location history are not sitting in the same export as active users), CLOUD-01 (regular enumeration of what is actually reachable from the internet), OBSV-01 (centralized logging of database access, which would at minimum let a small operator answer the "how" question its users and regulators were never given).

```

**What happened.** On July 22, 2021, Transnet, the South African state-owned company that operates the country's freight rail network and major container ports, was hit by a ransomware attack that forced it to declare force majeure at its container terminals in Durban, Ngqura, Port Elizabeth, and Cape Town. Durban alone handles roughly 60% of South African container traffic; port staff fell back to manual, paper-based cargo clearance for several days while systems were rebuilt, in what South Africa's Institute for Security Studies called an unprecedented disruption to the country's ports.

**Entry.** Transnet never disclosed how the attacker first got in, and no independent technical write-up has established it either; the malware itself was linked by researchers to a family variously called "Death Kitty," "Hello Kitty," and "Five Hands," which CrowdStrike assessed as likely operated from Russia or Eastern Europe without confirming a specific named group.

**Escalation.** Not detailed by Transnet, but its own remediation — which a UN Conference on Trade and Development case study describes as including a rebuild of Active Directory and a new security stack deployed across its environment — indicates the attacker reached Transnet's core identity infrastructure rather than a single isolated system, consistent with lateral movement across a national IT estate that was not segmented tightly enough to contain the disruption to one site.

**Blast radius.** No customer or cargo data was reported stolen — Transnet stated that none of its clients' data had been compromised — and the impact was operational rather than a data breach: multiple terminals offline, cold-storage capacity lost, and a cargo backlog that took days to clear even after core systems were restored by July 26–28. No ransom amount, payment, or direct financial cost was ever disclosed.

**Human factor.** Post-incident policy analysis in South Africa pointed to a governance gap rather than a single mistake — prior audits had already identified weaknesses in Transnet's IT security posture, described by one review as "a gulf separating governance aspirations from on-the-ground execution" at a state operator whose infrastructure the whole economy depends on.

**What would have stopped it.** CLOUD-05 (segmentation between ports, regions, and IT tiers, so a domain-wide compromise cannot take every terminal offline at once), AUTH-01 (phishing-resistant MFA on remote-access and administrative paths, a standard baseline given the entry vector is unknown), OBSV-05 (a written incident and disclosure plan — the days between attack and full recovery suggest response competence that a documented plan would make repeatable and faster next time).

  - https://blog.kraken.com/product/security/abusing-smart-contracts-to-steal-600-million-how-the-poly-network-hack-actually-happened
  - https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2021/08/10/cross-chain-defi-site-poly-network-hacked-hundreds-of-millions-potentially-lost
```

**What happened.** On August 10, 2021, an attacker drained more than $610 million in cryptocurrency from Poly Network, a cross-chain protocol that lets assets move between the Ethereum, Binance Smart Chain, and Polygon blockchains — at the time the largest DeFi theft on record. The attacker began returning funds within days of the exploit becoming public and, after Poly Network publicly offered a $500,000 bug bounty and a "chief security advisor" title, had returned essentially all of the stolen assets by August 25.

**Entry.** The exploit abused Poly Network's own "EthCrossChainManager" contract, which was designed to accept calls to a restricted set of methods but validated them only by a 4-byte hash of the method name; the attacker brute-forced an unused method name whose hash collided with that of a privileged function, letting them invoke that function as the Manager contract itself — abuse of intended functionality rather than a classic code-execution bug.

**Escalation.** None required — the colliding method call gave the attacker's transaction owner-level authority over the "EthCrossChainData" contract in one step, which they used to overwrite the "Keeper" public keys controlling all funds held in the cross-chain contracts, directly seizing control of the entire pool.

**Blast radius.** Assets were drained across all three supported chains — roughly $610 million at the time — before Poly Network could react; the attacker, who later said the theft was meant to expose the vulnerability and described themselves as a "white hat," returned $340 million within three days and the remainder soon after, an outcome security researchers noted was as much luck and public pressure as design, given nothing forced the attacker to give the funds back.

**Human factor.** None identified as an individual operator mistake — this was a smart-contract design flaw, specifically a single internal contract granted far more calling privilege than any of its legitimate functions needed, the same "excess access" failure mode that recurs throughout this corpus in more conventional systems.

**What would have stopped it.** AUTH-02 (authorization checked per call and per method, not inferred from a truncated hash of the method name), DATA-12 (capping what a single compromised contract or credential can reach, in value as well as volume — the core lesson of a bug that reached the entirety of three chains' locked funds at once), INPUT-09 (identifiers are unguessable, and unguessability is never the only control — a 4-byte method-name hash is exactly the kind of narrow, brute-forceable identifier this warns against trusting as a security boundary).

## Countermeasures and guidance

- CISA and the FBI's rapid, direct engagement with Kaseya over the July 4 weekend — plus the eventual delivery of a universal decryptor later reported to have come from the FBI's own prior access to REvil's infrastructure — became a template for federal response to a single vendor compromise cascading across thousands of downstream victims.
- DIVD's responsible disclosure to Kaseya, begun in March 2021, was still mid-patch-cycle when REvil exploited the unpatched remainder on July 2 — a reminder that a disclosure process already in progress does not protect against a second party finding the same bugs independently.
- CISA and the NSA jointly published MSP- and RMM-specific hardening guidance in the aftermath of Kaseya, treating remote-management tooling as Tier-0 infrastructure requiring host-based isolation and least-privilege agent permissions rather than blanket trust.
- T-Mobile's breach led to a 2024 FCC consent decree requiring $31.5 million in mandated future security investment alongside a fine — one of the first times a US telecom regulator tied a settlement explicitly to forward-looking security spending rather than a fine alone.
- Four of this quarter's eleven records (Guntrader, Accenture, Lojas Renner, Olympus) never disclosed an entry vector at all, and none faced a regulatory requirement to do so — illustrating how little public accountability currently exists for the "how" once a ransom note appears.
- HIPAA Journal's monthly aggregation of HHS OCR breach-portal filings remains one of the few systematic ways to see how often business-email-compromise and phishing-driven mailbox breaches recur at small and mid-sized healthcare practices well below the threshold of national news coverage.
